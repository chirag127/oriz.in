Gold occupies an unusual place in Indian household finance. It is inherited, gifted, worn, pledged, worshipped and sold in a crisis. The same metal can be a store of value, a consumption purchase, a status object and a source of emergency liquidity. Most gold advice collapses those jobs into one number, which is why families end up with a lot of jewellery and very little understanding of what it costs.

This guide separates the jobs. It covers every common route to owning gold in India, the all-in cost of each, the tax questions that need current verification, the allocation decision, and the liquidity test that matters when the reason for selling is not a market view but a hospital bill.

## The four jobs of gold

Before comparing products, decide which job the gold is doing.

| Job | What it means | Better-suited routes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consumption | Jewellery for a wedding, festival or daily wear | Jewellery, with the making charge treated as a consumption cost |
| Long-term saving | A rupee allocation to a metal with a different cycle from equity | SGB, low-cost ETF, gold fund |
| Crisis liquidity | Money available when the household needs cash quickly | ETF, SGB collateral, gold loan, digital gold with tested settlement |
| Diversification | A small portfolio allocation that moves differently from other assets | SGB, ETF, gold fund |

A single purchase can serve more than one job, but the costs differ. A necklace bought for a wedding is a consumption purchase that happens to contain metal. An ETF bought for a portfolio allocation is a financial asset. Treating the necklace as an investment because the metal price rose is how the making charge disappears from the family's mental accounts.

## Every route, priced honestly

The table below is comparative, not a recommendation. Actual costs vary by provider, city, purity, ticket size and date. The point is the structure of the cost, not a precise basis-point estimate.

| Route | What you own | Entry cost | Ongoing cost | Exit | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Physical bars or coins | The metal, usually 24K | Dealer premium + 3% GST on metal | Storage, insurance, assay | Dealer or buyer discount | Purity, theft, resale spread |
| Jewellery | The metal plus fabrication | Making charges + GST | Locker, insurance, maintenance | Net weight × rate minus melting/assay | Making charge loss, design risk |
| Gold ETF | Units backed by vaulted gold | Brokerage + small spread | Expense ratio | Exchange sale, usually same day | Market price, tracking difference |
| Gold fund of funds | Units of a fund that holds an ETF | Fund expenses | Higher than direct ETF | Redemption, one to two days | Layered costs |
| Sovereign Gold Bond | A government security denominated in grams | Issue price, no making charge | None beyond custody | Secondary market or maturity | Issuance windows, secondary liquidity |
| Digital gold | A contractual claim on a vault operator | Platform spread + GST | Platform spread | Platform buy-back | Operator and contract risk |
| Gold Monetisation Scheme | A deposit of gold with a bank | Purity testing and documentation | Scheme terms | Maturity or redemption | Bank process, scheme terms |
| Gold loan | Borrowing against pledged gold | Processing fee, interest | Interest, possible foreclosure fee | Repay and release | Forced sale if unpaid, valuation haircut |

### Sovereign Gold Bonds

SGBs are issued by the Reserve Bank of India on behalf of the Government of India in tranches. They are denominated in grams, pay a fixed rate of interest on the issue price, and have a defined maturity and exit window. When issued at par, they avoid the making charge and dealer spread that dominate physical gold.

The important questions are not only the coupon. Check:

- whether a tranche is currently open;
- the issue price and the interest payment dates;
- the maturity and the early-exit window;
- the secondary-market price if you need to sell before maturity;
- the tax treatment at maturity versus an early sale;
- whether you hold the bond in demat or certificate form, and who the nominee is.

Tax rules and issuance terms change. A guide cannot substitute for the current RBI notification, your broker's schedule, or a tax professional's reading of your situation.

### Gold ETFs and gold funds

A gold ETF is a listed fund that holds physical gold through a custodian. You buy units on an exchange through a demat account. The expense ratio is embedded in the NAV; the buy and sell spreads depend on the day's liquidity. A gold fund of funds invests in an ETF and adds another layer of expenses, so compare the total cost rather than the headline return.

ETFs are useful when you want a small, liquid allocation and do not need the metal in hand. They are not a substitute for a demat account, and they are not free of price risk simply because the underlying is gold.

### Digital gold

Digital gold apps sell fractional claims on gold held by a vault operator. The app quotes a buy price that includes a spread, adds GST on the metal, and may charge for storage or delivery. The crucial structural fact is that the product is a contractual claim, not a SEBI-regulated mutual fund and not an RBI-insured deposit. Read the custody arrangement, insurance, audit, buy-back terms and grievance route before treating it as a long-term holding.

The small ticket size is genuine convenience. It is not a reason to ignore the spread. A 3% buy spread plus GST and a 3% sell spread is a large round trip for an asset whose annual appreciation is not guaranteed.

### Jewellery and coins

Jewellery is the most emotionally important and financially least efficient route for new investment money. The making charge can be a substantial percentage of the bill, GST applies, and at resale the buyer typically pays for net metal, not the design. A ₹1,00,000 purchase with a 15% making charge holds about ₹85,000 of metal value before any other cost; selling at a discount can return less than that.

Coins are simpler than jewellery but still carry a dealer premium, GST and a resale discount. If the goal is investment, compare the full round trip with an ETF or an SGB. If the goal is a gift or a ritual, buy the piece you actually want and record the cost as consumption.

### The Gold Monetisation Scheme

The Gold Monetisation Scheme allows households to deposit idle gold with participating banks and earn interest under the scheme's terms. It can make sense for gold that is already sitting in a locker and is not worn. The practical friction is the purity test, documentation, bank participation and the difference between the scheme's valuation and what a local jeweller would pay. Read the current scheme master directions and the individual bank's terms; the product has changed over time.

### Gold loans

A gold loan is secured borrowing, not a gold investment. The lender values the pledged metal, applies a loan-to-value limit, charges interest and may add processing or foreclosure fees. It can be a cheaper bridge than an unsecured personal loan for a short, certain need, but it creates the risk of losing the pledged jewellery if repayment slips. Compare the effective annual cost, the repayment structure and the release process, not only the advertised monthly rate.

## The cost stack in one comparison

For a ₹1,00,000 gold purchase, the round-trip cost is roughly:

| Route | Entry friction | Exit friction | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 24K coins | Dealer premium + 3% GST | Dealer discount | Purity certificate, buy-back rate |
| Jewellery | Making charges + GST | Net-weight discount, melting | Design premium, store policy |
| Gold ETF | Brokerage and spread | Brokerage and spread | Expense ratio, liquidity |
| SGB at issue | Issue price | Secondary-market price or maturity | Issuance window, tax, tenure |
| Digital gold | Platform spread + GST | Platform spread | Operator risk, buy-back terms |
| Gold fund of funds | Fund expenses | Redemption terms | Layered expense ratio |

The cheapest route on paper is not always the right route. An SGB bought at issue can be cheaper than an ETF, but if you need the money in year two, the secondary-market price and liquidity matter more than the entry cost. An ETF is liquid on an exchange, but it requires a demat account and a trading day. Jewellery is available in a crisis, but at a discount and on a buyer's timetable.

## Allocation: how much gold, and in which form

Gold is a diversifier and a crisis reserve, not an income-producing asset. A household's existing jewellery counts toward its gold allocation, even if it was bought for a wedding. That is the single most useful correction to make before adding more.

| Household stage | A reasonable planning range | Form to consider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early accumulation | 5–10% | Low-cost ETF or SGB; avoid buying jewellery as an investment |
| Established household | 10–15%, including inherited gold | SGB/ETF for new money; count existing jewellery |
| Near or in retirement | 10–15% | Liquidity and collateral matter more; keep records and a ladder |

These ranges are educational starting points, not personalised advice. A household with a large inherited gold stock may need no new purchases. A household with no emergency fund should build that first. A household with a high gold allocation and a low equity allocation should ask whether the concentration is intentional.

## Tax and paperwork

Gold tax treatment depends on the form, the holding period and the year of sale. SGB interest is generally taxable at the holder's slab; capital-gains treatment at maturity and on secondary-market sales has changed across budgets. ETF and fund gains follow the rules for the relevant asset class. Physical gold has its own capital-gains rules, and jewellery may have cost-of-acquisition questions when it was inherited or gifted.

Do not rely on a rate remembered from an old article. Keep:

- purchase invoices and purity certificates;
- demat statements for ETFs and SGBs;
- SGB issue and interest records;
- gift deeds, wills or inheritance documents;
- gold-loan agreements and repayment receipts;
- a record of making charges, because they affect the acquisition cost and the eventual gain.

## The crisis-liquidity test

A useful exercise is to ask how each route behaves on a Sunday when the household needs ₹3,00,000.

- An ETF holder waits for a trading day and sells at the market price, with settlement.
- An SGB holder may sell in the secondary market, pledge the bond, or wait for the exit window; the price and process matter.
- A jewellery holder finds a buyer, accepts a discount, and may need an assay.
- A digital-gold holder uses the platform's buy-back process and waits for settlement.
- A gold-loan borrower pledges metal and receives a loan, not a sale, with interest and a repayment obligation.

The point is not that one route is always better. It is that the route should match the reason the household might need the money. A long-term allocation can tolerate a market price; an emergency reserve cannot tolerate a multi-day settlement and a buyer's discount.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it costs |
| --- | --- |
| Counting jewellery at its purchase price | The making charge is not recoverable at resale |
| Buying digital gold for the spread alone | The spread plus GST can be a large round trip |
| Treating an SGB as liquid cash | The secondary market can be thin and the exit window has rules |
| Ignoring existing household gold | The portfolio may already be over-allocated |
| Using a gold loan for consumption | The pledged metal is at risk and interest accrues |
| Forgetting tax and records | The eventual gain and the proof of cost both matter |
| Assuming gold always hedges inflation | Gold has no earnings and its real return is uncertain |
