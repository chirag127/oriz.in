The joint account is the most misunderstood product in Indian retail banking. Families open one because it feels
like trust, close it after the first dispute, and nominate wrongly throughout. The account itself is simple; the
legal questions around it — who owns the money, who can sign, what happens on death — are where households lose
money and time. This article lays out the structures, the survivor problem, and the division most families settle on.

## The four structures, and what each one legally means

Banks offer more than one kind of "joint" account, and the fine print decides everything.

| Structure                   | Who can operate            | On death of one holder                                                             | Typical use                          |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Either or Survivor (E or S) | Either holder alone        | Full balance passes to survivor per bank mandate; nominee claim applies after both | Married couple, day-to-day account   |
| Former or Survivor (F or S) | Former primary while alive | Survivor gets balance; some banks require former's consent patterns                | Older couples, one active operator   |
| Anyone or Survivor          | Any holder alone           | Same as E or S, extended to more holders                                           | Parents with an adult child          |
| Jointly (Both signatures)   | All holders together       | All must sign; heirs' chain after both                                             | Rare; business or safeguarding needs |

The critical point: **nomination is not ownership.** The nominee is a trustee of the money — for bank accounts,
the nominee receives the balance on behalf of the legal heirs under the Banking Companies Act framework, unless
the account falls under the 2015 nomination rules that let banks clear small balances against nomination alone.
For mutual funds and PPF, nomination simplifies the claim but does not extinguish heirs' rights. A second holder
named "either or survivor" does pass full operating rights to the survivor while alive; after both die, the money
follows succession law, not the card in your wallet.

## The survivor problem: the first 90 days

When a holder dies, the survivor with an E or S mandate keeps full access immediately — this is why couples should
hold at least one account in that structure. The problems start when every account was "jointly" operated or when
the deceased held everything single-name: the survivor then waits for death certificate, KYC re-verification, and,
where nomination is missing, for legal-heir paperwork that can take months. Keep the salary account, the bill-pay
account and one emergency-fund account in E or S form precisely so that the first 90 days after a death do not
also become a liquidity crisis. See [RBI's customer service norms on deceased account settlement](https://www.rbi.org.in).

## The tax wrinkles nobody mentions

Joint FD interest is taxed in the hands of the person who _earned_ it — typically the first holder whose money
funded it — not split 50:50 by default. If a working spouse funds a joint FD held with a non-earning spouse, the
Interest Income still clubbed under Section 64 of the Income-tax Act. Gift between spouses is exempt, but income
**arising from** the gifted money is clubbed back to the giver. A joint savings account's interest still flows to
each holder's 10,000-rupee Section 80TTA deduction only in proportion to their share. None of this is a reason to
avoid joint holdings; it is a reason to know which holder's PAN the bank has on file for TDS purposes.

## The division most households settle on

After the first decade of marriage, most financially calm households converge on the same architecture: **one
joint account for household flow** — both salaries sweep a fixed share in, all bills, EMIs, school fees, groceries
auto-debit from it — **plus one own account per person** that nobody audits. The joint account covers the shared
life; the own accounts preserve the adult dignity of spending money that requires no justification. The shares
need not be 50:50: proportional to take-home is cleaner and survives salary gaps, career breaks and the
asymmetry of Indian family obligations. What does not work is the reverse — everything joint, no own space —
which turns every ₹500 purchase into a negotiation.

## What the joint decision actually protects

A joint account is not a trust exercise; it is an operational decision about what happens when one person is in
surgery, travelling, or dead. The E or S structure, a correct nomination on every single-name holding, a
documented list of what exists and where — those three cost one weekend and remove the two failure modes Indian
families actually experience: the survivor who cannot access money, and the heirs who discover the parent's
holdings only after the claim window has hardened. Choose the structure for the bad day, not for the wedding photo.

## The nomination audit, done once

The joint-account decision is half of the survivor system; the other half is nomination on every single-name
holding. The audit is one evening: list every bank account, FD, RD, demat account, mutual-fund folio, PPF,
EPF-EPFO record, insurance policy and provident-style balance in the household, and check each for (a) a live
nominee, (b) the right nominee, and (c) a nominee who knows they are one. The common failure is not the
absence of nomination but the stale one — the father whose every account still names his brother, alive,
decades after the arrangement stopped mattering; the folio that names a parent now deceased, which forces the
claim into legal-heir territory anyway. Banks and AMCs let nomination be updated online in minutes; EPFO and
insurance take forms but nothing heroic. Run the audit when anything changes — a marriage, a birth, a death,
a move — and once a year regardless. The nomination is the cheapest inheritance tool the Indian system
offers, and the only one that works at 2 a.m. from a phone.
