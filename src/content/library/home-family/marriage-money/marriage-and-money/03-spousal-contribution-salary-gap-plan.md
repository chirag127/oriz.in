Most Indian dual-income households do not split expenses 50:50 — they split them by negotiation, annually, after
the last argument about money. The working-spouse salary gap is the norm: 70:30, 60:40, sometimes 100:0 when one
career pauses for a child or a parent. A contribution model that only works when incomes are equal is not a
model. This one survives unequal pay, pauses, and the resentment that builds when fairness is felt but never
stated. It needs one table, one insurance layer and one annual meeting.

## The proportional model, with worked numbers

Split shared expenses in proportion to take-home, not gross, and count only what actually lands in the bank.

|                          | Spouse A  | Spouse B | Household |
| ------------------------ | --------- | -------- | --------- |
| Take-home monthly        | ₹1,20,000 | ₹60,000  | ₹1,80,000 |
| Share of household       | 67%       | 33%      | 100%      |
| Shared pool contribution | ₹40,200   | ₹19,800  | ₹60,000   |
| Own account keeps        | ₹79,800   | ₹40,200  | ₹1,20,000 |

The shared pool covers rent, EMIs, utilities, groceries, school fees, insurance premiums, the household
emergency-fund SIP — the costs of the shared life. Each own account covers personal spending, gifts to one's own
side of the family, and one's own subscriptions, with **no audit rights** over it. The proportional split has one
property the 50:50 split lacks: it scales. A raise, a bonus month, or a salary cut adjusts everyone's
contribution automatically without a fresh negotiation. A 50:50 split on unequal incomes quietly taxes the
lower earner's entire discretionary life.

## The career-break insurance layer

The model breaks at exactly the moment it matters: when one spouse's income drops to zero for a child, a
parent's illness, or a layoff. Fix it with a rule set while both are earning:

- **The break fund:** the household emergency fund is sized on _one_ salary covering essentials for 6 months,
  not both — because the most likely single-year shock is one income pausing, not both.
- **The continuity rule:** during a break, the earning spouse's contribution percentage does not change; the
  pausing spouse's own-account spend draws from the shared pool at a fixed personal allowance both agreed at
  the start. This converts a humiliation into a policy.
- **The comeback rule:** within 3 months of resuming work, contributions reset to the new proportions — no
  arrears debates, no penalty for the pause.

Households that skip this layer pay for it later in the ugliest currency: the lower earner's reduced standing in
their own house. Money arguments in marriages are rarely about money; they are about whose money it is.

## The own-money floor and why it is not optional

Each working spouse keeps an untouchable personal floor — commonly ₹8,000–₹15,000 a month — that arrives in
their own account before any shared math begins. This is not secrecy; both partners know it exists and its
approximate size. It exists because a household where every rupee requires explanation eventually stops the
lower-autonomy partner from spending at all, and unspent personal money curdles into either resentment or a
secret account — the two things the floor is designed to prevent. For the non-earning spouse the floor is even
more important: money in one's own name, from a household that can afford it, is the difference between an
adult and a dependent in every conversation for the next thirty years.

## The annual reset meeting

Once a year — bonus week works — the couple updates three numbers in one sitting: take-homes, the shared-pool
target (did rent, EMIs or school fees change?), and each personal floor. Thirty minutes, one spreadsheet. The
meeting's real product is not the numbers; it is that fairness stays _stated_. Every unspoken money assumption
in a marriage becomes a fight within five years, and every spoken one becomes a policy nobody has to defend
twice. If the conversation surfaces a genuine gap — one spouse carrying disproportionate family obligations —
the reset is where it gets priced into the split, rather than surfacing as a December argument.

## What the model actually buys

A proportional split with a break layer and a personal floor does three things. It survives inequality — the
70:30 household stops pretending symmetry it does not have. It survives pauses — the career break becomes a
planned expense, not a status change. And it survives the decades: the model's own accounts and stated rules
mean both partners enter their fifties with credit histories, investments and, frankly, self-respect in their
own names. Money systems in marriages fail loudly or quietly; the loud failures get fixed, and the quiet ones
become the household's personality. Choose the structure on a calm Sunday, not in the middle of one.

## The clubbing rules underneath the model

The tax law has opinions about spousal money, and the model should be built around them. Income from assets
funded by transferring money to a spouse is clubbed back to the transferor under Section 64(1) — the classic
trap is the salary-earning partner who gifts ₹10 lakh, buys a fixed deposit in the spouse's name, and then
discovers the interest lands in their own return anyway. The exemption-routed fix is the one this model
already uses: each partner's own earnings, own accounts and own investments need no clubbing analysis at all.
Gifts between spouses are tax-free at any amount, but the _income from gifted assets_ follows the giver — so
the housewife's-corpus route is done properly with a one-time gift, documented, invested in instruments whose
income is declared in the giver's return, and gradually converted to the spouse's own earnings over the years.
The full clubbing provisions are on [incometax.gov.in](https://www.incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal) and are
worth one reading before the first large intra-household transfer.

## The three failure modes to watch for

Even a good model degrades in predictable ways. First, **contribution drift:** the shared pool was sized in
year one and never revisited while rents and school fees rose — the pool runs a monthly deficit that gets
funded by whichever spouse hates awkward conversations more, which is how proportional splits rot into
implicit single-earner households. The annual reset fixes it by calendar, not by courage. Second, **floor
erosion:** personal floors get "borrowed" from in a tight month and never returned — the floor that survives
is the one that is sacred even when the month is ugly; a borrowed-from floor is a debt between spouses, which
is the exact thing the floor exists to prevent. Third, **asymmetric knowledge:** one spouse holds all the
demat, insurance and tax paperwork and the other would need a week to reconstruct the household's finances.
The test is brutal and worth running annually: could either partner, from the shared filing, list every
policy, folio and loan in thirty minutes? If not, the model is one accident away from a blind household.

## What to do in year one

New households should run the model small before trusting it: start with a modest shared pool that covers
only the certain, recurring costs (rent, utilities, groceries), keep personal floors generous, and add
categories to the pool only after both partners have watched the system work through one full month. The
first year's real work is calibrating the numbers to reality — the pool that was sized on estimates meets
actual electricity bills, actual in-law obligations, actual spending temperaments — and the household that
adjusts monthly in year one arrives at year five with a system that needs only the annual reset. Couples
who skip the small-start phase tend to discover the model's weak points in month three, when every amount
is still negotiable and every negotiation sets a precedent.
