An NRI's Indian assets — accounts, fixed deposits, demat holdings, a flat in Pune — do not transfer themselves.
The transfer runs through three instruments that most families discover only when they are needed: nomination,
the will (or the succession law that applies without one), and the power of attorney that lets a trusted person
act while the NRI is alive but unavailable. Setting these up is cheap. Getting them wrong after a death costs
years.

## Nomination: what it does and what it does not do

Nomination is a _facilitation_ clause, not a transfer of ownership. Section 45ZA of the Banking Regulation Act
makes the nominee the bank's authorised recipient of deposits; Section 72 of the Companies Act does the same
for shares; the SEBI nomination rules apply to demat and mutual fund holdings. The nominee receives the money
or securities — and then holds it for the legal heirs according to succession law.

| Instrument                       | Governing law                  | What the nominee gets    | What heirs can still claim       |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| Bank deposit nomination          | Banking Regulation Act s.45ZA  | Discharge of the deposit | Their succession share           |
| Demat/securities nomination      | Companies Act s.72, SEBI rules | Transfer of securities   | Their succession share           |
| Mutual fund nomination           | SEBI/AMFI rules                | Redemption proceeds      | Their succession share           |
| Property (no nomination concept) | Succession law                 | N/A                      | The whole estate per will or law |

The distinction matters most where a nominee is _not_ a natural heir. A brother nominated on a deposit does
not extinguish the widow's and children's succession claims; it simply gives him the cheque, with a duty to
account to the estate. Families that nominate one child "to keep it simple" create exactly the dispute the
nomination was meant to avoid — the clean fix is a will that matches the nominations.

## Succession: which law applies to an NRI's estate

Succession of immovable property in India is governed by **Indian law**, regardless of the deceased's
domicile. Movable property (bank balances, shares, mutual funds) follows the law of the deceased's domicile —
which for an NRI settled abroad is often the foreign country's law, subject to the Indian Succession Act and
personal-law frameworks for Indians.

| Asset                  | Law that governs succession                                                   | Instrument families need                                      |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Flat, land in India    | Indian law (Hindu Succession Act, Muslim personal law, Indian Succession Act) | Will, or legal heirship/succession certificate                |
| Bank balances, FDs     | Domicile law, practically bank's succession process                           | Death certificate, KYC of heirs, will/probate where available |
| Demat shares, MF units | Domicile law, transmitted through registrar                                   | Transmission form, death certificate, will/probate            |
| NRE/NRO accounts       | Same as bank balances                                                         | Account-specific closure/transmission forms                   |

Two documents do most of the practical work. A **legal heirship certificate** (issued by the revenue
authorities or tehsildar) establishes who the heirs are for smaller estates. A **succession certificate**
(issued by a civil court under the Indian Succession Act) is what banks, registrars and brokers demand for
debts and securities when there is no will — and it takes months to years in a crowded court.

## Probate, and why a will is not enough

A will is the cheapest instrument and the most under-executed. In India, probate — court certification of
the will — is _mandatory_ in the cities of Mumbai, Kolkata and Chennai and for immovable property there,
whatever the will says; elsewhere it is optional but increasingly demanded by banks for large estates.
An un-probated will speeds nothing at the bank counter; the bank's own transmission process still requires
the death certificate, KYC of the claimant, and either the will (with indemnities) or the succession
certificate.

For an NRI, the will should exist in two forms: a valid one under the foreign country's law (covering foreign
assets), and one covering Indian assets, executed with the formalities the Indian Succession Act requires —
two witnesses, testator's signature, and ideally a doctor's certificate of capacity for the date of execution.
A single will trying to govern both jurisdictions usually means probate in both.

## Power of attorney: the working instrument

While the NRI is alive, the power of attorney is what lets Indian affairs run — property management, bank
operations, tax filings, litigation. A PoA executed abroad must be **notarised in the foreign country and
then attested/apostilled and adjudicated in India** (stamp duty paid at the district registrar) within the
prescribed period — usually three months of receipt in India — for it to be used before Indian authorities.

| PoA type               | What it permits                    | Typical use                            |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| General PoA            | Wide powers across affairs         | When the NRI is abroad long-term       |
| Specific PoA           | One transaction (sale of Flat 402) | A single sale or registration          |
| PoA with bank          | Account operation, fixed limits    | Day-to-day banking                     |
| Durable/healthcare PoA | Continues on incapacity            | Estate planning in the foreign country |

Two cautions keep the PoA clean. Keep it _specific_ — a blanket general PoA over all property is a standing
invitation to misuse and is increasingly refused by sub-registrars for sale transactions. And keep the
attestation chain intact: notarisation abroad, apostille (or Indian consulate attestation where the country
is not a Hague apostille member), and adjudication with stamp duty in India. An unadjudicated PoA is why
property registrations stall.

## Death of an NRI: the FEMA and tax aftermath

After death, the estate's assets continue under FEMA's provisions for deceased NRIs' accounts. The nominee or
heir operates the account for the estate; repatriation of the balances follows the NRO route — up to USD 1
million per financial year per the [RBI's procedure for remittances from deceased accounts](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/FAQView.aspx?Id=66),
with Form 15CA/CB. Income earned
on the estate after death is taxable in India as the estate's income; the ITR for the year of death is filed
by the legal representative.

The grey zone is _residential status of the estate and heirs_: an heir who is a foreign citizen and
non-resident inherits immovable property freely (inheritance is not a "purchase" requiring RBI permission),
but the subsequent sale proceeds still route through NRO with the USD 1 million cap, and tax on the gain uses
the deceased's cost basis. Families that assume inherited property can be sold and repatriated like a resident's
property discover the cap and the 15CA/CB machinery at the worst moment.

## The legality note

The instruments are legal; the failure modes are procedural, and some are grey. Holding a resident relative's
account as a de facto operating account for an NRI's money is a FEMA violation — the penalty framework for
contraventions allows monetary penalties that can extend to three times the amount involved in certain
violations, with compounding available on application. Unregistered PoAs used to execute registrations invite
challenge to the transaction itself; a sale executed under an unadjudicated PoA can be set aside, and the
sub-registrar's refusal is the least of the consequences. Where a nominee disputes heirs, civil suits for
succession take years — the _prosecution_ risk is nil, but the cost is real. The clean configuration costs
one trip to a notary: a specific PoA properly attested, nominations that mirror the will, and a will executed
with two witnesses and a capacity certificate.

## The bottom line

Three documents decide whether an NRI's Indian estate transfers in months or in years: nominations that match
the will, a will that satisfies the Indian Succession Act's formalities (and probate where required), and a
properly adjudicated PoA for the years before death. Do them while alive and healthy, in that order. Every
year of delay adds a court visit, a bank hold, or a family dispute that no amount of posthumous paperwork
fixes.
