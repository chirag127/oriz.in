A woman's money in India sits inside three overlapping frameworks: stridhan (the traditional concept of a
woman's own property, now read by courts), the succession law that governs what she inherits, and the gift
tax rules that govern what she is given. The three are confused in family conversations with expensive
consequences — jewellery assumed to be "hers" that proves nothing, inheritance assumed to follow the brother's
wording, gifts assumed to be tax-free because family was involved. This article states what each actually
says.

## Stridhan: what it is and how courts read it

Stridhan, in the Hindu law framework (applied through statutes and judicial interpretation), is property
acquired by a woman through gifts before, during or after marriage — from her family, her husband, or
strangers — along with property she acquires herself. Its defining feature is her absolute ownership: she
can hold, sell, gift or bequeath it, and it is not the husband's family's property by marriage. The courts
have read the concept with precision:

| Source of property                            | Character (traditional reading)            | Practical proof needed                 |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| Gifts from her parents/relatives at marriage  | Stridhan                                   | Lists, receipts, bank trails           |
| Gifts from husband or in-laws                 | Stridhan (with nuances by source and era)  | The same paper trail                   |
| Her own earnings                              | Absolutely her own (statutory, all faiths) | Salary credits, folios, ITRs           |
| Property bought with pooled family funds      | Ownership follows the payment trail        | Whose money, whose account, whose name |
| Mithakshara property (ancestral, coparcenary) | Her rights changed by the 2005 amendment   | See below                              |

The disputes are decided on evidence, not sentiment: the jewellery listed nowhere and purchased in cash
decades ago is the hardest to prove, while the gold bought with documented transfers and listed in a marriage
inventory is the easiest. The practical instrument is unromantic — a written list of gifts received at
marriage, signed by both families, with approximate values and bank trails where they exist.

## Daughters' inheritance: the 2005 amendment and what it settled

The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 made daughters _coparceners_ in ancestral property by birth, with
the same rights and liabilities as sons — including the share that comes with partition and the liability for
debts. The Supreme Court's 2020 ruling in _Vineeta Sharma v. Rajesh Sharma_ settled the retroactivity
question: the daughter's coparcenary right exists by birth and does not depend on whether the father was
alive on the amendment date. The practical map:

| Family situation                             | The daughter's position                                         |
| -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ancestral (coparcenary) property             | Equal share by birth; partition claim available                 |
| Father's self-acquired property, no will     | Class-I heir equally with brothers and mother                   |
| Father's self-acquired property, with a will | The will governs; sons and daughters inherit only as bequeathed |
| Married before or after 2005                 | No difference in coparcenary right (post-2020 ruling)           |
| Agricultural land                            | State-level succession rules can vary                           |

Marriage does not extinguish the natal family's inheritance rights; the "married daughter gets nothing" house
rule has no statutory basis in coparcenary or intestate succession. What the family _can_ lawfully do is
estate planning — a will, a partition deed, a family settlement — and those instruments are lawful precisely
because they are documented, not because they are announced at a family meeting.

## Gifts: the tax rules that actually apply

| Gift                                   | Tax treatment                                             |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| From specified relatives, any value    | Not income in her hands (Section 56's relative list)      |
| From non-relatives, above ₹50,000/year | Taxable as income from other sources at slab              |
| On marriage occasion, from anyone      | Exempt (the marriage-specific exclusion)                  |
| Property gifted (land, house)          | Stamp duty still applies on registration                  |
| Gifted asset's later sale              | Capital gains computed from the donor's cost (Section 49) |

The relative list includes parents, siblings, spouse, spouse's siblings and linear ancestors/descendants —
which covers most family gifting. The received-gift documentation that matters later: a simple signed gift
deed (registered for immovable property), the bank trail for money gifts, and the date — because the gifted
asset's cost basis and holding period run from the _donor's_ acquisition, and the eventual sale's capital
gains computation needs that date.

## The safety net: maintenance, protection orders and where to file

The financial protections that exist for women are statutory, not discretionary: maintenance under Section
125 CrPC (and the personal-law equivalents) for wives, children and parents; the Protection of Women from
Domestic Violence Act's monetary reliefs and residence orders; and the Hindu Adoption and Maintenance Act's
provisions. The DV Act's protection officers and the family courts are the filing points; the
[wcd.gov.in](https://wcd.gov.in/) ministry pages list the schemes and helplines (181), and
[indiacode.nic.in](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/) carries the statutes' current text. The economic-abuse
definition in the DV Act includes denying access to household money — a legal frame for the "control the
accounts" pattern that financial-independence advice usually discusses only in softer terms.

## The legality note

Everything here is law, and the boundaries are sharp. The grey edges families walk into: _benami_ property —
an asset bought in the woman's name with the husband's undisclosed money, held for his benefit — is a
statutory offence under the Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act with forfeiture consequences; a
genuine gift with the money's source documented is legal, a name-lending arrangement for unexplained income
is not. Nominee-vs-heir confusion is another: nomination gives access, succession law gives ownership, and
the will is the instrument that harmonises them. And the "family settlement at the meeting" with no
documentation is worth exactly the paper it is not written on.

## The bottom line

A woman's own money is a matter of statute and evidence: earnings are absolutely hers, stridhan is her
absolute property when it can be proved, daughters inherit equally as coparceners and Class-I heirs, and
family gifts have specific tax rules that are mostly favourable when documented. The paperwork — the marriage
inventory, the gift deeds, the bank trails, the will — is not distrust; it is the only language the law
speaks when the family's memory disagrees with itself.
