Money habits are installed between the ages of 6 and 16, mostly by accident. The child watches whether money
is a fight or a system, whether waiting is normal, and whether spending happens with a tap or a plan. A
deliberate pocket-money system is the cheapest financial education available — cheaper than any course, and
more effective than any lecture — because it pays the child to practise. The system below has three rules:
money arrives on schedule, it is split on arrival, and the child's mistakes are allowed to be cheap.

## The three-jar split, by age

The core mechanic: every rupee that arrives is split into **Spend, Save and Give** — in that order of the
child's choosing, but always three parts, never zero.

| Age   | Pocket money (per week, indicative) | Split the child controls  | Parent's only rule                  |
| ----- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| 6–9   | ₹50–100                             | Two jars: Spend, Save     | Save jar is physically visible      |
| 10–13 | ₹200–500                            | Three jars + bank account | Save jar moves monthly to the bank  |
| 14–16 | ₹1,000–2,000                        | Bank + first SIP          | Half of any gift money goes to Save |
| 17–19 | Monthly transfer                    | Own UPI, own tracking     | Own account pays own discretionary  |

The split ratio is the child's decision — 90:10 spend-save teaches nothing, but neither does a forced 50:50,
which just becomes a tax they resent. The lesson that lands is the _consequence curve_: the child who spends
everything on Sunday has nothing on Wednesday, and the lesson must be allowed to happen. Parents who rescue
the Wednesday gap with an advance have taught the only lesson advances teach: money arrives when feelings run
high, not when plans do.

## The bank-account handover at 10

Around age 10, the Save jar physically moves to a bank account in the child's name, with the parent as
guardian. Minor savings accounts are free at most banks, come with a modest debit card, and give the child a
statement — the statement matters more than the interest, which is a rounding error. The passbook ritual is
the compounding lesson: the balance that grew without the child doing anything. By 13 the child should be able
to answer three questions without help: how much is in the account, what went in this month, and what the
difference means. This is also the right age to name the difference between the bank account (safe, boring)
and the markets (growing, wobbly) — not with a lecture, but by showing one liquid fund and one index fund
statement side by side and letting the child notice the wobble themselves.

## The teen SIP and the ₹500 proof

At 14–16, start a small SIP — ₹500 a month in a Nifty 50 index fund in the parent's demat-and-folio structure
(a minor demat account with the parent as guardian, which SEBI mandates for minors). The amount is almost
irrelevant; the proof is not. A ₹500 SIP at 12% from 15 to 25 is roughly ₹1.4 lakh; from 25 to 35 the same
₹500 is roughly ₹1.2 lakh — **ten years earlier beats ten times later by a wide margin**, and the teenager who
watches the statement for a decade internalises the curve no短视频-reel can teach. Around the SIP, teach the
two adult behaviours: the statement is checked monthly (five minutes), and the fund is not sold because a
down month feels bad. The sell-the-wobble reflex is the most expensive habit in retail investing; better it is
unlearned on ₹500 than on ₹5 lakh.

## The UPI-era rules for 15-year-olds

The payments world has changed faster than parenting advice. A 15-year-old with UPI Lite or a family-linked
UPI can spend the entire monthly allowance in four taps, which makes the old envelope system more relevant,
not less. Workable rules for the phone era: the monthly amount arrives in the child's own account on a fixed
date (no top-ups, no advances); a screenshot of the month's spending is reviewed together at month-end — as a
five-minute ritual, not an audit; and one shared rule applies to everyone in the house including parents: no
purchases over ₹2,000 without a 24-hour wait. The waiting rule is the single most protective behaviour in
modern retail spending — it kills the influencer-coded impulse purchase dead, and modelling it as a parent
teaches more than enforcing it.

## Where schools and courses fit

Schools teach financial literacy as vocabulary — what is a fixed deposit — and stop before behaviour, which
is where the actual losses happen. The household's edge is the practice loop: money arrives on schedule, is
split by the child, is reviewed monthly, and its mistakes are cheap. By 17 the system's graduate can read a
bank statement, price a want against a goal, wait 24 hours, and hold a wobbling fund for a decade. That
skill set — not any certificate — is what the neighbour's kid with the tutoring classes will not have.

## The legality note

Nothing here is grey, but two compliance details matter. A minor's bank account operates on the
natural-guardian route until 18; at majority, the account must be converted and fresh KYC done in the child's
own right — banks send notices, families miss them, and the account freezes. Interest on a minor's savings
and FD income is the child's income, clubbed under Section 64(1A) with the parent's if it exceeds ₹1,500 a
year per minor — a limit so low that the family PAN should hold the corpus and the child's account should
hold only spending money. And the SIP on a minor demat account must be funded from documented, gift-routed
money — a bank transfer from the guardian's account with a gift letter on file — because the money is the
child's and the source must show it. The KYC and minor-account rules are laid out on
[rbi.org.in](https://www.rbi.org.in), and the clubbing provision itself is Section 64(1A) of the Income-tax
Act, readable on [incometax.gov.in](https://www.incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal).
