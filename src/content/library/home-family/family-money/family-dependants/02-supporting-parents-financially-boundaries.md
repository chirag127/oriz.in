Supporting parents is not a budget line for most Indian earners — it is a standing obligation that arrives
unpriced, unannounced and often in the same month as everything else. The households that handle it well treat
it like infrastructure: fixed, funded, insured against the medical shock, and split with siblings by explicit
policy rather than by whoever answers the phone. The households that handle it badly treat it as an emotional
account settled ad hoc — and pay for it in both money and sibling relationships. This is the infrastructure
version.

## The fixed transfer, not the random one

Convert parent support from a variable emotional expense into a fixed system: a standing transfer on salary
day, in a range both sides know.

| Parent situation                                 | Monthly transfer (indicative) | Notes                             |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Parents financially independent, occasional help | ₹0–5,000                      | Festival and medical gifts only   |
| Parents partly dependent, no pension             | ₹10,000–20,000                | Covers utilities, groceries, help |
| Parents fully dependent, tier-2/3 city           | ₹20,000–35,000                | Full household support            |
| Parents fully dependent, metro                   | ₹35,000–50,000                | Rent changes everything           |

The fixed number does three things the random transfer cannot: it lets parents plan (dignity is being able to
plan); it lets _you_ plan (support that is a line item stops cannibalising your SIPs); and it kills the
guilt-spend cycle, where a festive ₹50,000 transfer substitutes for months of unmanaged costs. Review the
number annually — inflation in a tier-2 household runs at 6–8%, so a transfer set in 2023 is quietly
insufficient by 2026.

## Medical insurance before the medical event

The single most valuable thing you can buy your parents is not a transfer — it is a senior-citizen health
policy bought _while they are healthy_. Premiums at 60 for a ₹10 lakh cover run ₹25,000–60,000 a year per
person; at 68 with a diabetes diagnosis, the same cover costs 40–80% more with waiting-period exclusions
baked in, and at 72 some doors close. The order is: policy first, then the transfer, then the earmarked
medical reserve (₹1–2 lakh liquid) for deductibles, consumables and the non-payable items policies exclude.
The financial shock that breaks Indian earners is almost never the monthly support; it is the ₹8 lakh
hospitalisation that arrives uninsured in the same quarter as a job review. The IRDAI senior-citizen
provisions — including the right to renewal and pre-existing disease waiting windows — are codified at
[irdai.gov.in](https://irdai.gov.in) and make the earlier purchase strictly better; every year of delay is
paid in premium and exclusion.

## The sibling problem, solved with policy

The unspoken fight in most Indian families: support is split by proximity, guilt and answering speed, not by
capacity. The fix is an explicit siblings' policy, stated once, in writing, ideally on a family call:

- **Capacity-proportional split:** each sibling contributes a share proportional to take-home, not equal
  amounts — the sibling earning ₹2 lakh funds more than the one earning ₹40,000, and both know it in advance.
- **The substitute rule:** a sibling who cannot pay can contribute time — appointments, paperwork, the
  hospital shifts. Time is money in a system where the earning sibling is buying exactly that.
- **The ledger, not the grievance:** one shared note records what was paid for what — hospital, repairs,
  travel. Not to police; to prevent the five-year-later version of the argument where memory has inflated
  everyone's own contribution.

Families that skip the policy do not avoid the fight; they schedule it for the worst possible week, after the
second hospitalisation, in a WhatsApp thread that never fully heals.

## The emergency top-up pool

Beyond the monthly transfer, keep a parents-emergency pool — ₹2–3 lakh in a liquid fund, replenished after
use — earmarked for the genuine shocks: hospital deductibles, an urgent repair, a suddenly needed attendant
and travel. This pool is what lets the true emergency be handled in 48 hours without touching the SIPs or
taking a personal loan. It sits outside the household emergency fund (which covers your own life) and is
reviewed at the same annual sitting. The pool's existence changes behaviour in the crisis: decisions get made
on medical merit, not on the balance in the account.

## The non-negotiable: your own retirement line

The rule that keeps the whole system solvent: **your retirement savings line survives every family demand.**
The 15–20% of take-home that funds your own EPF/VPP/NPS/equity corpus is not suspended because a parent
needed support — because in 25 years, the alternative is your own children funding an unfunded you, and the
cycle repeating with interest. The system that works is layered: the fixed transfer (predictable), the
policy-bought insurance (the shock absorbed), the emergency pool (the spike), the sibling policy (the split),
and your own line (the continuity). A support system that consumes the supporter is not support; it is a
relay race run without a baton.

## The paperwork that saves months later

Support paid to parents leaves a paper trail or it leaves a dispute. Three documents cost nothing now and
answer everything later: the standing-transfer mandate itself (which is its own record of regular support);
a simple one-page declaration signed by the parents stating that the monthly amount is voluntary support
from their children, not a loan — this single page defeats the most common property-era claim, that the
money was a recoverable advance against the house; and a nomination audit on everything the parents hold —
bank accounts, FDs, demat, PPF, property society records — with a copy of the list in your own files. The
worst week to discover that the father's FD had no nominee and the mother cannot produce a succession
certificate is the week after the funeral, when the family is least able to run an errand chain. Households
that do the audit call it morbid for an afternoon; households that skip it describe the succession process
for years.

## The hidden subsidies nobody prices

Two subsidies flow in families that are never priced: the parent who relocates to your city to run your
household (saving ₹15,000–25,000 a month in domestic help and childcare), and the parent whose pension or
rental income quietly funds their own expenses so your transfer is smaller than it should be. Both deserve
acknowledgment in the annual reset — the first by naming the childcare saving explicitly in the household
budget instead of letting it vanish into "we manage", the second by honestly increasing the transfer as
their income sources erode. A fixed deposit yielding 7% on ₹10 lakh generates ₹70,000 a year, which
inflation halves in real terms in a decade; the transfer that "was enough" in 2020 needs revisiting not
because generosity faded but because the arithmetic did. Naming these flows keeps the support system honest —
and keeps the household's real economics visible to the two people making its decisions.
