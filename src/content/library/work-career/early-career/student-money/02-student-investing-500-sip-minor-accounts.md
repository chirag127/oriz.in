A student investor's edge is not capital — it is the 45-year compounding runway and the mistakes made small.
The disadvantage is everything else: no salary, KYC friction, minor-account rules, and an internet full of
advice written for people with incomes. This article is the mechanics: what a student can legally open, what a
₹500 SIP actually becomes, and the five errors whose real cost is measured not in rupees but in years.

## What a student can actually open

| Account/Product              | Age/rules                        | Practical note                                |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Savings account (minor, 10+) | Operated by minor within limits  | Becomes the KYC anchor for everything else    |
| Minor demat ( guardian-held) | Parent/guardian operates till 18 | Converts to regular account at 18 — do it     |
| MF folio via minor account   | Guardian in folio                | SIPs stop at 18 unless converted              |
| Direct-plan MF (own PAN)     | 18+, own bank account            | The standard route from first year of college |
| PPF in own name              | Any age; ₹500 minimum            | The 15-year discipline instrument             |
| Crypto exchange (18+)        | KYC with PAN                     | Legal, taxed at 30% + 1% TDS — know the rules |

The 18th-birthday checklist matters more than most: a minor demat account _must_ be converted to a regular one,
SIPs registered under guardian folios pause, and bank accounts shift to self-operation. Students who skip the
conversion discover it when the SIP bounce notice arrives in the second year of college.

## The ₹500 arithmetic, honestly computed

₹500 a month at 11% (a reasonable long-term equity assumption, not a promise) for the 45-year runway:

| Start age | Monthly SIP | Corpus at 60 | Total invested |
| --------- | ----------- | ------------ | -------------- |
| 19        | ₹500        | ~₹1.05 crore | ₹2.46 lakh     |
| 25        | ₹500        | ~₹56 lakh    | ₹2.10 lakh     |
| 30        | ₹500        | ~₹33 lakh    | ₹1.80 lakh     |

The ₹500 is a training instrument: its financial value is real but modest; its habit value — the debits that
happen without decisions, the first folio statement read at 20, the first market dip survived with ₹50,000 and
not ₹50 lakh at stake — is the actual return. The step-up is where the money arrives: the same investor
stepping the SIP up 10% a year from ₹500 reaches roughly 4-5× the flat-SIP corpus, because the step-up
converts a symbol into a system.

## The tax of a student's investments

Students are taxpayers too, with the same slabs and the same rules:

- **Capital gains on redemptions** follow the standard rules: 12.5% long-term on equity above ₹1.25 lakh per
  year, slab for short-term. A student redeeming a ₹2 lakh folio with ₹80,000 of gains pays nothing if total
  income is under the basic exemption — but must still _file_ to claim the exemption properly when gains plus
  other income cross thresholds.
- **Minor's income (clubbing).** Income of a minor child is clubbed with the parent's under Section 10(32)/64
  — except the ₹1,500-per-child-per-year exemption and income the minor earns from _their own_ manual work,
  skill or talent (a 17-year-old's YouTube or tutoring income is their own; a 15-year-old's dividend income
  is the parent's).
- **The parent's PAN on everything before 18.** Guardian-held accounts, FDs and folios all report under the
  guardian's PAN — the clubbing rules apply, and the minor's own PAN matters from 18.

| Income type (student)          | Whose tax                    | Note                                           |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Tutoring/freelance at 17+      | Student's own (skill income) | Section 64's exception; ITR in own name        |
| Dividends/FD interest at 15    | Parent (clubbed)             | ₹1,500 exemption per child                     |
| Capital gains at 20, own folio | Student's own                | Standard CG rules, exemption available         |
| Scholarship money              | Exempt under Section 10(16)  | Genuinely for education; keep the award letter |

## The five mistakes that cost decades

1. **The crypto-heavy first portfolio.** A 19-year-old's ₹5,000 monthly split 80% into crypto means the first
   bear market teaches despair, not patience — 70% drawdowns at a stage where the habit should be forming.
   The index-fund core (covered in the investing track) with a small speculative satellite teaches more.
2. **The margin/F&O discovery at 20.** The leverage products' first-year math — the SEBI's own studies show
   the vast majority of retail F&O traders lose money — is a tuition fee when the position sizes are ₹5,000
   and a catastrophe when they are the whole folio.
3. **The insurance-cum-investment bought "for the future".** The endowment at 20 locks a 5% instrument for 15
   years at exactly the age when equity runway is worth the most. Term insurance arrives when dependants do.
4. **The demat-account zoo.** Five accounts opened for five referral bonuses create the maintenance-fee
   leakage and the consolidated-statement confusion that hides holdings for years. One broker, one folio set,
   nominations on everything.
5. **Not filing at all.** A student with FD interest, dividends and a freelance streak who crosses the
   basic-exemption boundary and does not file loses refunds, builds no ITR history (needed for visas and the
   first credit card), and inherits notices. Filing at 19 costs an afternoon.

## The legality note

Student investing is fully legal and lightly regulated in their favour — the protections are real (SEBI's
[RBI/SEBI investor-protection frameworks](https://www.sebi.gov.in/) govern the products, minor accounts are
guardian-supervised, and unlisted "student investment clubs" pooling money need care: pooling without a
structure can wander into unregistered collective-investment territory, with the SEBI CIS consequences the
alt-investments track describes). The grey edges students actually meet: "doubling" apps and referral-scheme
telegram groups promising fixed monthly returns — Ponzi mechanics with the standard consequences — and
trading accounts opened on a friend's KYC "because the KYC keeps failing", which is impersonation with
account-fraud consequences, not a workaround.

## The bottom line

Open the accounts once, correctly, with nominations; run a ₹500 SIP in a broad index fund as the habit
engine; know whose PAN and whose tax each rupee belongs to (yours after 18, clubbed before); file the return
even when small; and spend the tuition budget on small mistakes made early. The corpus that matters at 30 was
built by the system that started at 19 — and the system, at that age, is free.
