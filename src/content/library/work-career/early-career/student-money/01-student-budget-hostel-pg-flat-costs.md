Student budgeting is the only budgeting done without an income that reliably arrives, which changes its
mechanics entirely: the enemy is not insufficient income but irregular supply, cash-flow timing and the five
specific leaks that eat every Indian student's allowance in the same order. This article prices the housing
decision first — it is 40-60% of the budget — then the leaks, then the allocation that survives a bad month.

## The housing decision, priced

| Option            | Monthly cost (tier-1 city) | What is included            | Hidden costs                                  |
| ----------------- | -------------------------- | --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| College hostel    | ₹8,000-15,000 (with mess)  | Room, mess, utilities       | Semester-level advance, fines, mess hikes     |
| PG (with food)    | ₹12,000-22,000             | Room, 2-3 meals, Wi-Fi      | Deposit (2-3 months), food quality drift      |
| PG (without food) | ₹8,000-14,000              | Room, utilities             | Food adds ₹4,000-6,000                        |
| Shared flat (3-4) | ₹7,000-12,000 per head     | Rent share, utilities share | Brokerage, deposits, furnishing, conflict     |
| Own studio        | ₹18,000-35,000             | Everything                  | The solo premium — the budget's biggest swing |

The shared flat is usually the optimal cost-to-control ratio — cheaper per head than a PG with food, with a
kitchen that enables the single largest leak-repair (cooking even 8 meals a week saves ₹3,000-4,000). Its
costs are the hidden ones: brokerage (one month), deposits (2-3 months, often half-refunded), and the
furnishing ratchet where each flatmate buys one thing and four Somethings multiply. The hostel is the control
option: fixed costs, mess economics at scale, and the leak list shrinks to snacks and cabs.

## The leak list, in the order it eats the money

1. **Food delivery.** The average food-delivery order at ₹350 against a cooked meal at ₹70; three orders a
   week is ₹4,200 a month — often the largest line item after rent. The repair is not abstinence but a
   cap: a weekly order budget, pre-decided.
2. **Cabs.** The ₹120 last-mile rides that never appear in any ledger; four a week is ₹2,000. Campus-area
   routines (shared autos, cycles, the ₹15 bus) repair most of it.
3. **Subscriptions stacked across cards and UPI autopay.** ₹199 × 5 services nobody fully uses is ₹1,000 a
   month; the shared-family-plan route (one streaming account among flatmates, paid by rotation) cuts it 70%.
   Autopay mandates make these stickier than one's memory of them — the UPI Autopay controls article in the
   banking track is the companion read.
4. **The festival-travel spike.** Travel home twice a semester at ₹3,000-8,000 each, unallocated, paid from
   the same month's allowance. The repair is a named sinking fund: ₹1,000 a month, 8 months, travel paid
   without crisis.
5. **Cash that vanishes.** The ₹100-300 cash withdrawals with no destination. The repair is not tracking every
   ₹50 — it is a weekly 10-minute statement review that makes the pattern visible enough to cap.

| Leak                 | Typical monthly cost | Capped version        | Monthly saving               |
| -------------------- | -------------------- | --------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Food delivery        | ₹4,200               | ₹1,500                | ₹2,700                       |
| Cabs                 | ₹2,000               | ₹800                  | ₹1,200                       |
| Subscriptions        | ₹1,000               | ₹300                  | ₹700                         |
| Travel (unallocated) | ₹1,600 amortised     | ₹1,000 (sinking fund) | Cash-flow repair, not saving |
| Vanishing cash       | ₹1,500               | ₹500                  | ₹1,000                       |

## The allocation: what a ₹5,000 month actually looks like

| Line                    | Amount | Note                                        |
| ----------------------- | ------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| Food beyond mess/ration | ₹1,500 | The capped delivery + eating-out line       |
| Transport               | ₹800   | Daily commute + the capped cab line         |
| Phone + data            | ₹300   | The student plan; it is also the study tool |
| Subscriptions (shared)  | ₹200   | One shared streaming, one music, one cloud  |
| Social/entertainment    | ₹1,000 | The line that makes the budget survivable   |
| Sinking fund (travel)   | ₹700   | The festival-return ticket, funded monthly  |
| Buffer/emergency        | ₹500   | The ₹6,000-a-year first emergency fund      |

At ₹2,000 the structure shrinks proportionally — food ₹800, transport ₹400, everything else ₹800 with the
buffer becoming ₹200. The point is not the rupees; it is that every rupee has a line _before_ the month
begins, which is zero-based budgeting (the foundations track's longer treatment) applied at student scale.

## Shared-flat money systems that prevent the friendship damage

The flat's financial failures are mechanical, not moral: unequal usage, untracked common spends and
settlement delayed past memory. The systems that work:

- **One shared spreadsheet, not one shared wallet.** Every common purchase (groceries, cooking gas, cleaner,
  Wi-Fi) logged with amount, payer and date; settled every Sunday via UPI. A shared wallet or "whoever has
  cash pays" guarantees one flatmate finances the others at 0% interest and resents it.
- **Named expense owners with rotation.** The Wi-Fi bill, the gas cylinder, the cleaning — each owned by one
  flatmate per quarter, auto-debited, settled in the sheet. Rotation prevents the "I always pay" archive.
- **The deposit ledger.** Every flatmate's deposit amount, date and receipt photographed into the sheet. The
  move-out fight over deposits is the most common financial conflict of Indian student life, and the
  evidence wins it.
- **Personal spends stay personal.** The system tracks common costs only; nobody audits anybody's clothes.
  The boundary is what keeps the sheet alive.

## The legality note

Student money has few legal edges but they bite: renting involves deposit and notice terms — a written
agreement (even on plain paper with signatures and the deposit amount) is what makes a deposit dispute a
matter of evidence rather than volume; unregistered "PG" operators with arbitrary deposit forfeitures are
challengeable via the consumer forum route at
[consumerhelpline.gov.in](https://consumerhelpline.gov.in); and lending apps targeting students with
"instant ₹5,000" at effective rates above 60% operate in the RBI's regulated-lending framework —
unregistered recovery practices are reportable to the
[RBI](https://www.rbi.org.in/) Ombudsman, and a student loan from a friend is a contract too (get the terms
in writing if the amount exceeds a month's allowance).

## The bottom line

Price the housing decision before everything else — it is the budget's largest lever. Cap the five leaks with
pre-decided numbers rather than willpower, run the shared flat on a Sunday-settled sheet with a deposit
ledger, fund travel through a named sinking fund, and keep the ₹500 buffer that absorbs the month's one
surprise. A student budget's success is not measured in savings; it is measured in the absence of month-end
crises — which is the same skill the salary years will demand at 100× the scale.
