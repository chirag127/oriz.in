A health policy bought today does not cover everything from tomorrow. Three clocks start on day one — 30 days,
two years, four years — and the timing of your first claim depends on which clock governs your hospitalisation.
Most claim disputes that begin with "the insurer says the disease was pre-existing" are actually waiting-period
arithmetic done correctly by the insurer and understood late by the family. This is the clock structure, the
rules that change it, and how to plan around it.

## The three clocks

**1. The initial waiting period: 30 days.** Except for accidents, no claim is payable for any hospitalisation in
the first 30 days of a new policy. The rationale is anti-selection: people do not buy health insurance the
afternoon before a planned surgery. The 30-day period is standard, hard, and applies to every fresh policy,
including ported ones in some cases (portability carries credits — see below).

**2. Specific-disease waiting periods: 1-2 years.** A defined list of conditions and procedures carries its own
longer clock: hernia, hydrocele, cataract, tonsillectomy, sinusitis, piles, fistula, joint replacement,
kidney stones, gynaecological procedures, and similar. The logic: these are conditions that often exist
asymptomatically before purchase, so the insurer excludes them for the first one or two years. The list is in
the policy wording — it is a _named list_, not a discretionary judgment.

**3. Pre-existing disease (PED) waiting period: up to 4 years.** Any condition that existed before the policy
started — diagnosed, treated, or with symptoms that a reasonable person would have investigated — is excluded
for the first 48 months of continuous coverage. After 48 months of continuous cover with the same insurer (or
with credited period under portability), the PED becomes covered.

| Waiting period       | Duration        | Applies to                        | Can it be reduced?                                 |
| -------------------- | --------------- | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Initial              | 30 days         | All illness hospitalisations      | No                                                 |
| Specific diseases    | 1-2 years       | Named list in the wording         | Some insurers offer 1-year variants                |
| Pre-existing disease | Up to 48 months | Anything existing before purchase | Portability credits; some products offer 2-3 years |
| Maternity            | 24-48 months    | Childbirth and related            | Usually fixed by product                           |

## What counts as pre-existing

The IRDAI definition, standardised across insurers: any condition, ailment, injury or related complication
existing before the policy start, that was **diagnosed, received advice or treatment, or whose symptoms were
present** — the symptom clause is the one that generates disputes. Diabetes diagnosed three years before the
policy is a PED with a 4-year clock. So is hypertension managed with medication for years. So, in the
insurer's argument, is the knee pain that existed six months before the policy and became a meniscus surgery
in month 20.

The complication: the **disclosure question**. PED must be disclosed at proposal; a policy issued after
disclosure carries a stated PED waiting period and — after IRDAI's 2024 master circular on protecting
policyholders — insurers must specify the PED _by name_ where they can, rather than leaving "any known
condition" open-ended. Non-disclosure of PED is the misstatement route (three-year contestability in life
insurance has no exact parallel in health, but insurers can repudiate for fraud or material non-disclosure),
while disclosed PED runs its clock and becomes covered. Disclosure is the better branch of that fork by a wide
margin.

## The rules that beat the clocks

**Portability and the credited period.** If you move from one insurer to another (or between products with the
same insurer) without a break, the time served counts. A PED clock of 36 months served at Insurer A, ported to
Insurer B, leaves 12 months of the 48. IRDAI's portability regulations require the new insurer to underwrite
based on the old insurer's claims and underwriting data, and the credit is of the _waiting period served_, not
of the premium. Portability requests must be initiated 45 days before renewal — the mechanics matter, because a
request on renewal day does not complete in time and the policy lapses in between.

**Moratorium rules.** Under IRDAI's standardisation, after **8 years of continuous coverage** with the same
insurer (including ported years), the insurer cannot contest claims on the ground of non-disclosure or PED
waiting periods, except for proved fraud — the "moratorium period". The 2024 master circular reaffirmed this:
after moratorium, the burden of proof shifts decisively to the insurer. This is the strongest reason to pick
one insurer young and stay: the 8-year mark converts a health policy from a contested contract into a durable
asset.

| Years of continuous cover | PED status                                                        | Non-disclosure contestability           |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| 0-2                       | Waiting period runs                                               | Full contestability                     |
| 2-4                       | Named PEDs become covered as clocks expire                        | Contestability subject to rules         |
| 4-8                       | PEDs covered; new PEDs continue to be subject to their own clocks | Reducing in practice                    |
| 8+ (moratorium)           | All PEDs covered                                                  | Insurer cannot contest except for fraud |

**Day-1 and shorter-PED products.** A recent generation of products covers named conditions from day 1 or
waives PED waiting after 2 years — priced 20-60% higher. For a 60-year-old buying a first policy (where the
4-year PED clock is nearly useless in practice), these products are often the honest answer.

## The planning moves

**Buy before you have a diagnosis.** The single most valuable insurance decision available to a young earner:
a policy bought at 25 with no PED carries a 48-month clock that expires by 29, and the moratorium at 33. The
same person buying at 45 with a diagnosis faces a 4-year clock on that diagnosis and never reaches the
moratorium without discipline. The premium difference between 25 and 45 is large; the _insurability_ difference
is larger.

**Never let it lapse.** Continuous coverage is the unit of account. A 3-month gap can restart clocks — portability
requires no break beyond 30 days (with grace provisions), and the moratorium clock resets on lapse in most
wordings. Auto-renewal with a funded account is the entire discipline.

**Port, do not lapses-and-rebuy.** Upgrading cover at a new insurer with a PED: portability preserves the served
clock; buying fresh resets it. The request timing (45 days) and the underwriting outcome (the new insurer can
accept with conditions, load the premium, or impose its own exclusions) should be known _before_ the old policy
renewal date.

## The legality note

Waiting periods are contractual and IRDAI-regulated; they are legal, standardised and enforceable. The
regulatory boundary is around _how_ they are applied: the master circular on protection of policyholders'
interests (2024) requires that PED be defined at underwriting, that exclusions be stated specifically, that
claims cannot be denied for want of an avenue that the policy wording does not contain, and that the moratorium
provisions are honoured. What carries consequences on the other side: **concealing** a diagnosed condition at
proposal is material misrepresentation, and the insurer can repudiate the claim for fraud — the policyholder
loses the premium and the cover, and a forged discharge summary in support of a claim is criminal forgery with
prosecution exposure. Disputes that are honest disagreements about wording go to the insurer's grievance
officer, then the Bima Bharosa portal, then the insurance ombudsman — a free, time-bound forum with the power
to award up to ₹50 lakh (₹1 crore for claim disputes in some categories). The regulations are at
[irdai.gov.in](https://irdai.gov.in).

## The clock map, in one paragraph

Buy early, disclose everything, never lapse, and treat the first 48 months of any policy as the audition: claims
in that window are governed by the clocks, and the insurer is entitled to enforce them. From month 49 to month
96, the policy is maturing — PEDs are covered, specific-disease lists have expired, and the only live risk is
non-disclosure contestability. At month 97, the moratorium makes the policy what most people believe they
bought on day one: a contract that pays when the hospital bills, without a forensic argument first.
