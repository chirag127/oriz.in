The most expensive sentence in Indian health insurance is a sub-limit nobody reads at purchase: "room rent
capped at 1% of sum insured per day". It looks like a small administrative detail. It is not. Because Indian
hospital billing is priced _off the room_, a room-rent cap can turn a ₹5 lakh policy into a policy that pays
₹2.1 lakh of a ₹3.4 lakh bill — and the shortfall arrives as a co-payment-sized deduction you never agreed to.
This is the mechanic, the arithmetic, and the fix.

## The cascade: how one room drives the whole bill

Hospital tariffs are tiered by room category: general ward, twin sharing, single private, deluxe, suite. Each
tier carries its own schedule — not just for the room, but for the surgeon's fee, the anaesthetist, the
operation theatre, consultation charges and nursing. The surgeon operating in a deluxe suite charges more than
the same surgeon operating in a twin-sharing ward, because the hospital's tariff card says so.

Most Indian health policies, especially older ones and the budget tiers of modern ones, contain a clause that
does one of two things: **caps the room rent** at a percentage of sum insured (1% is the common number) or a
rupee amount, or **caps the room category** (e.g., "single private room only"). The critical wording — the one
that costs money — is the next sentence: _associated medical expenses shall be payable in the proportion that
the actual room rent bears to the eligible room rent_.

That proportionality clause is the cascade. If you are entitled to a ₹3,000 room and you take a ₹6,000 room,
the policy does not simply pay the room at ₹3,000 and let you pay the ₹3,000 difference. It pays **50% of the
entire associated bill** — surgery, surgeon, diagnostics, OT — because you occupied a room at twice the eligible
rate.

## The arithmetic, worked

Consider a ₹5,00,000 sum-insured policy with a 1% room cap (₹5,000/day) and the proportionality clause. A
patient has surgery; the hospital bills:

| Billing head             | Twin-sharing (eligible) | Single AC (chosen)   | Deluxe suite (chosen) |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------- | -------------------- | --------------------- |
| Room rent, 4 days        | ₹8,000 (₹2,000/day)     | ₹20,000 (₹5,000/day) | ₹36,000 (₹9,000/day)  |
| Surgeon and anaesthetist | ₹90,000                 | ₹1,20,000            | ₹1,60,000             |
| OT and consumables       | ₹70,000                 | ₹85,000              | ₹1,00,000             |
| Diagnostics, pharmacy    | ₹40,000                 | ₹45,000              | ₹50,000               |
| Nursing and others       | ₹22,000                 | ₹30,000              | ₹38,000               |
| **Total bill**           | **₹2,30,000**           | **₹3,00,000**        | **₹3,84,000**         |
| Eligible room rent       | ₹2,000/day              | ₹5,000/day           | ₹5,000/day            |
| Actual ÷ eligible ratio  | 100%                    | 100%                 | 55.6%                 |
| **Insurer pays**         | **₹2,30,000**           | **₹3,00,000**        | **₹2,13,300**         |
| **Patient pays**         | ₹0                      | ₹0                   | ₹1,70,700             |

The deluxe-suite patient paid for a room at ₹9,000 against an entitlement of ₹5,000 — a 1.8× ratio — and lost
44% of the entire bill, ₹1.7 lakh, not the ₹16,000 the room differential alone would suggest. Note the second
row: the single AC at exactly the cap is fully payable. The cliff is at the ratio, not at the rupee amount.

## What IRDAI fixed, and what it did not

The IRDAI's 2024 master circular on the protection of policyholders' interests directed insurers to define
clearly what a sub-limit applies to, to avoid ambiguous terms, and mandated that any reduction in claim due to
room-rent-related proportional deductions be communicated transparently — it also pushed insurers to state
room-category eligibility rather than percentage caps where possible, and to settle claims with a written
explanation of every deduction. The circular improved _transparency_, which matters at claim time. It did not
abolish sub-limits: a policy bought today can still contain them, and the older book — including group covers
and grandfathered retail policies — is full of them.

The structural fix available since 2020 is the **"room rent: no capping"** or "single private room" variants
most retail insurers now offer at higher premium — typically 15-30% more for the same sum insured. On a ₹25,000
premium, that is ₹4,000-7,500 a year to remove the single clause that produces the largest deductions in Indian
health claims. For a family with a ₹10 lakh cover and any realistic hospitalisation expectation, it is the
highest-value rider-equivalent in the market.

| Policy structure                        | Room entitlement                    | Claim behaviour              | Who it suits                      |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| 1% cap with proportionality             | ₹5,000/day on ₹5L                   | Cascade deductions above cap | Nobody, knowingly                 |
| Fixed rupee cap, no proportionality     | Room capped, rest payable at actual | Room differential only       | Budget buyers who know the clause |
| Room category cap ("single private AC") | Any room within category            | Clean                        | Most families                     |
| No room-rent capping                    | Any room type                       | Clean, priced 15-30% higher  | The default recommendation        |

## How to check your own policy in five minutes

Find the policy wording (not the brochure — the wording). Search for "room rent" and "proportionate" or "pro
rata". Three outcomes:

1. **"Room rent up to 1% of sum insured per day... associated medical expenses shall be payable in the same
   proportion"** — the cascade is present. Every hospitalisation above the eligible room will be prorated.
2. **"Room rent up to ₹X per day"** with no proportionality sentence — you pay the room difference only.
3. **No room-rent sub-limit, or "single private room"** — clean.

Then check the same wording in the _network-hospital_ context: some policies apply the cap only at non-network
hospitals, and some insurers' cashless authorisation letters state the eligible room in advance — read that
letter at admission, not at discharge, because the tariff decision is made at admission.

## The practical playbook at admission

When hospitalised, the TPA's pre-authorisation letter is the document that governs the room. It states the
eligible room category or amount. Showing that letter to the hospital's insurance desk before choosing the room
converts an abstract clause into a concrete choice: "your policy covers a single private AC room at ₹5,000; the
deluxe suite at ₹9,000 will trigger proportional deductions on the entire bill". Families who make this choice
at admission with full information pay less and dispute nothing. Families who learn the clause at discharge pay
the differential and write grievance emails that change nothing, because the clause was always there.

## The legality note

Room-rent sub-limits are legal and are a matter of policy wording — the contract you accepted at purchase.
The regulatory obligations sit on the insurer's side: IRDAI's protection-of-policyholders regulations and the
2024 master circular require that sub-limits be stated clearly, that claim deductions be explained in writing,
and that repudiation or partial settlement carry reasons with the grievance-escalation path stated. When an
insurer applies a proportional deduction _the policy does not contain_, or misstates the eligible room in the
pre-authorisation letter, that is a claim deficiency, and the remedies are the insurer's grievance officer,
then the IRDAI Bima Bharosa portal (Bima Bharosa — the integrated complaint platform), then the insurance
ombudsman, then consumer commission — in that order, and the ombudsman route is free and usually fast.
What has no remedy is the clause you did not read: the policyholder's consequences flow from the wording, and
the wordings are public. The master circular and the regulations are at
[irdai.gov.in](https://irdai.gov.in).

## The decision, stated plainly

Buy the no-room-cap variant if the premium difference is under 30%. If budget forces a capped policy, choose
the fixed-rupee-cap-without-proportionality version and know the number at admission. If you already own a
capped policy, check the wording today, note the exact eligible room for each family member's sum insured, and
decide whether upgrading at the next renewal is worth the premium delta — for most households holding ₹5-10
lakh covers, it is the single cheapest reduction in financial risk available anywhere in their insurance
portfolio.
