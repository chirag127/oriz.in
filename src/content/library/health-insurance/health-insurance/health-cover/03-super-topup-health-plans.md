A ₹50 lakh individual health policy from a mainstream insurer costs a 35-year-old roughly ₹25,000-40,000 a
year. A ₹5 lakh base policy plus a ₹45 lakh super top-up covering the same person costs roughly ₹18,000-25,000
for the pair — and provides nearly identical protection against the catastrophic bills that actually bankrupt
families. The saving is real, the mechanism is simple, and the one structural detail — the deductible — is
where buyers get hurt. This is how super top-ups work, what they cost, and how to structure them.

## What a super top-up actually is

A top-up policy pays expenses **above a threshold called the deductible**. A super top-up differs from a plain
top-up in one essential way: the deductible can be met by _any_ means — your own pocket, a base insurance
policy, an employer group cover, or a previous claim's exhaustion. A plain top-up only counts expenses _within
a single hospitalisation_ beyond its deductible, which makes it nearly useless for large multi-event years.

The mechanics: a super top-up with a ₹1,00,000 deductible and ₹49,00,000 sum insured pays nothing until
₹1,00,000 of eligible expenses are incurred in the policy year (across hospitalisations), and pays beyond that
up to the total. When the deductible is met by an underlying base policy, the base policy's payment counts
toward the deductible.

| Structure                                      | Sum insured          | Deductible | Approx. annual premium (35-y/o, family floater) |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------- | ---------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Standalone ₹50 lakh base policy                | ₹50,00,000           | —          | ₹30,000-45,000                                  |
| Base ₹5 lakh + super top-up ₹45 lakh           | ₹50,00,000 effective | ₹5,00,000  | ₹20,000-30,000                                  |
| Employer group ₹5 lakh + super top-up ₹45 lakh | ₹50,00,000 effective | ₹5,00,000  | ₹12,000-18,000 (top-up alone)                   |
| Base ₹10 lakh + super top-up ₹40 lakh          | ₹50,00,000 effective | ₹10,00,000 | ₹22,000-32,000                                  |

Premiums are indicative retail ranges; the _ratio_ — a super top-up costing 40-60% of an equivalent base policy
— is the stable fact across insurers.

## The claims arithmetic, worked

**Event 1: a ₹4,20,000 hospitalisation** with a base policy of ₹5 lakh and a super top-up of ₹45 lakh
(deductible ₹5 lakh).

- The base policy pays ₹4,20,000.
- The super top-up sees eligible expenses of ₹4,20,000 against a ₹5,00,000 deductible: ₹4,20,000 − ₹5,00,000 =
  nothing to pay (the deductible is not exhausted because expenses did not reach it).
- Net out-of-pocket: ₹0 (assuming the base policy covers the full bill).

**Event 2, same year: a ₹9,00,000 hospitalisation.**

- Base policy has ₹80,000 of sum insured left (₹5,00,000 − ₹4,20,000). It pays ₹80,000.
- The super top-up counts cumulative expenses: ₹4,20,000 + ₹9,00,000 = ₹13,20,000. Deductible ₹5,00,000 is
  crossed. It pays ₹13,20,000 − ₹5,00,000 = ₹8,20,000, of which the ₹80,000 the base paid is not double-counted —
  depending on the wording, the super top-up pays the _unpaid_ eligible expense above the deductible:
  ₹8,20,000, adjusted for what the base already paid.

The precise treatment of what the base policy paid is a wording detail: most super top-ups pay eligible expenses
_in excess of the deductible that are not payable by any other policy_. Read that sentence in the wording
before purchase; the difference is one hospitalisation's worth of ambiguity.

| Scenario               | Base pays           | Super top-up pays     | You pay                             |
| ---------------------- | ------------------- | --------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| One bill of ₹4.2 lakh  | ₹4,20,000           | ₹0 (below deductible) | ₹0                                  |
| One bill of ₹6.5 lakh  | ₹5,00,000           | ₹1,50,000             | ₹0                                  |
| Two bills: ₹4.2L + ₹9L | ₹4,20,000 + ₹80,000 | ₹8,20,000             | ₹0                                  |
| One bill of ₹60 lakh   | ₹5,00,000           | ₹45,00,000 (SI limit) | ₹10,00,000 — beyond total structure |

The last row is the honest limit: a ₹50 lakh effective structure does not pay a ₹60 lakh bill; it pays ₹50
lakh. No retail structure does better without a different product class.

## Why the pricing gap exists

Insurers price large base covers for the risk that the _entire_ sum insured is drawn at once — a ₹50 lakh
cancer treatment is a real scenario they underwrite from the first rupee. A super top-up's exposure begins
only after the deductible, so the expected loss per policy is a fraction of a base policy's. The saving is
therefore not a discount or a gimmick; it is actuarially consistent. What you give up: the first ₹5 lakh of
claims must come from somewhere (a base policy or your pocket), and the super top-up's sum insured is a real
ceiling, not a marketing number.

## The employer-cover trap, and the honest structure

The most common configuration is: employer group cover of ₹3-5 lakh, super top-up on top of it, no personal
base policy. It works — until the year you change jobs, are between jobs, or retire. The employer cover
vanishes; the super top-up's deductible (say ₹5 lakh) is now met only by cash. The family with a ₹9 lakh
hospitalisation in the year after a job change pays the first ₹5 lakh from savings, not because the cover
failed but because its foundation was an employer benefit.

The robust structure for anyone with dependants and a working career ahead:

| Layer                                                           | Purpose                                             | Notes                                        |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Personal base policy ₹10-25 lakh (family floater or individual) | Pays first; survives job changes                    | Bought early, kept for moratorium years      |
| Super top-up ₹40-90 lakh above it                               | Catastrophic layer, cheap                           | Deductible = base sum insured                |
| Emergency fund                                                  | Pays deductibles, non-payables, waiting-period gaps | The article on emergency funds covers sizing |

The layered structure costs less than a single big base policy at every age we priced, and it survives
employment changes by design. It also survives the insurer: if the base insurer misbehaves at claim time, the
super top-up's exposure to that dispute is bounded.

## What to check in the wording before buying

1. **Deductible definition** — per policy year (good) or per hospitalisation (bad: makes multi-event years
   painful).
2. **What counts toward the deductible** — expenses paid by other policies (yes), out-of-pocket (usually yes),
   non-medical items (no).
3. **The "not payable by other policy" clause** — how it treats what the base paid.
4. **Room rent and sub-limits** — the super top-up can carry its own room-rent capping (see the room-rent
   article); the cascade applies.
5. **Waiting periods and PED** — the super top-up has its own 30-day/2-year/4-year clocks, independent of the
   base policy's served time; portability credits do not transfer between different insurers' base and
   top-up contracts.
6. **Restore/multiply features** — whether the sum insured restores after exhaustion, and whether restoration
   applies to the top-up layer.

## The legality note

Super top-up products are regulated retail health products — legal, standardised in nomenclature by IRDAI's
guidelines, and sold with the same protection-of-policyholders rules as base policies. The regulatory traps
are the familiar ones: mis-selling at point of sale (an agent selling a super top-up _as if_ it were a full
health policy, without explaining the deductible, is a complaint the Bima Bharosa portal takes seriously and
the ombudsman awards on), and claim-time deductions not supported by the wording. The consequences for
insurers of unjustified deductions or misrepresentation by their intermediaries run through IRDAI's penalty
framework and the ombudsman's awards; for policyholders, the risk is not legality but suitability — buying a
super top-up alone, without any base or employer cover, leaves the deductible exposed to cash, which is a
planning error, not an illegal one. IRDAI's product regulations and the grievance framework are at
[irdai.gov.in](https://irdai.gov.in).

## The bottom line

For most households, the cheapest honest path to a ₹50 lakh-class cover is a modest personal base policy plus
a super top-up with a deductible equal to the base — bought young, kept continuously, with the employer cover
treated as a bonus layer and never as the foundation. The premium saved against a single big base policy runs
to ₹10,000-20,000 a year at ages 30-45, which, invested in a health contingency fund or simply kept, more than
pays for the occasional deductible exposure. The structure fails only when one of its layers is assumed to
exist and does not — so write down which layer pays what, before the hospital asks.
