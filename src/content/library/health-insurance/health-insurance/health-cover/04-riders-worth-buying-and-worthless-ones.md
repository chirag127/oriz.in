A rider is an add-on benefit attached to a base policy, priced separately, and — per IRDAI's 2022 guidelines —
capped so that all riders together cannot exceed a share of the base premium (for term life products, the
aggregate of rider premiums is capped at 30% of the base policy's premium; health riders in life products have
their own caps). Some riders are the cheapest insurance you will ever buy. Others are duplicate cover sold to a
buyer who does not know what the base policy already includes. The difference is a reading of the definition
clauses, which this article does for you.

## The riders worth buying

**1. Critical illness (CI) rider.** Pays a lump sum on diagnosis of a listed condition — cancer, heart attack
with defined severity, stroke, kidney failure, major organ transplant, bypass surgery, usually 15-40 conditions
per IRDAI's standardised definitions. It is not a hospitalisation cover; it is _income and out-of-network
protection_: money that arrives while you are not earning, to pay for treatment the base health policy does not
cover (consumables, travel, second opinions, experimental care) and for the household bills that continue.

The arithmetic: on a ₹1 crore term policy at age 32, a CI rider of ₹25 lakh costs roughly ₹4,000-8,000 a year.
A standalone ₹25 lakh critical-illness policy costs ₹6,000-12,000. The rider is cheaper and rides on a policy
you already maintain. The definition caveat: CI riders pay on _survival past a stated period_ (usually 28-30
days after diagnosis) and on conditions meeting severity definitions — a heart attack that kills instantly pays
the death benefit, not the CI benefit. Both can pay if both conditions are met; that is the point of holding
both.

**2. Accidental death and disablement (AD&D) rider.** Adds a lump sum for death by accident and graded payouts
for permanent disablement (loss of limb, loss of sight, total permanent disablement). Cost: ₹1,500-3,000 per
₹50 lakh of cover at age 30-40. For occupations with road exposure — delivery, sales, two-wheeler commutes —
this is honest pricing for a real risk. The caveat: the definition of "accident" (violent, visible, external,
involuntary) and the disability schedule's percentages decide everything at claim time.

**3. Waiver of premium (WoP) rider.** On becoming totally and permanently disabled (or on diagnosis of a listed
CI, in some wordings), future premiums are waived and the policy continues. This is the rider that protects the
_policy itself_ — a term plan lapses exactly when the family can least afford it. Cost: ₹1,000-3,000 a year on
a ₹1 crore term policy. Buy it on any long-term policy you expect to hold for decades; the annual cost is
trivial against the cover it preserves.

**4. Hospital cash / daily hospitalisation benefit (conditionally).** Pays a fixed daily amount (₹1,000-3,000)
per day of hospitalisation, often after an initial deductible of 2-3 days. It is not a health policy; it is a
small income-replacement for the non-medical costs of hospitalisation — travel, attendant, lost daily wages.
For a household with a single earner and no sick-leave buffer, it is honest value at ₹1,000-2,000 a year. For a
salaried person with paid leave, it is decoration.

**5. Accidental hospitalisation / surgical benefit riders (for specific exposures).** Schedules a lump sum per
surgery category. Value depends entirely on the schedule's generosity versus the premium; the 2022 guidelines
pushed these toward standardisation. Buy when the schedule covers the surgeries your family profile actually
risks, and skip otherwise.

## The riders that are marketing

**1. Return of premium (RoP) "term" as a rider or variant.** A term plan that returns all premiums if you
survive the term costs 1.5-2.5× a pure term plan. The extra premium invested in even a liquid fund at 6.5%
post-tax outperforms the returned amount in most scenarios — the insurer's return is your own money back at 0%
nominal yield. As a _rider_ attached to base term, it is the same deal in a smaller package.

**2. Riders duplicating employer group cover.** Accidental death riders bought by employees whose employer
already provides group personal-accident cover of ₹10-20 lakh. The employer's cover vanishes at job change;
the rider's whole rationale is permanence — which the duplicate destroys. Check the employer's cover before
buying; buy the rider only for the gap.

**3. Premium-waiver riders on single-premium or short-term products.** Waiver of premium makes sense over a
20-year term. On a 5-year policy, the probability that the waiver triggers and the remaining premium is
meaningful is small; the price is not.

**4. "Health" riders in endowment and ULIP products.** IRDAI's 2019 and 2022 changes reined these in, but
legacy wordings attach health riders to investment-linked policies where the base product's own charges make
the rider's effective cost opaque. Riders belong on protection products, not on savings products.

**5. Income benefit riders.** Pay 1% of sum insured monthly for 10 years after death, in place of (or alongside)
the lump sum. The embedded return is usually a bond-ish 5-6% — lower than what the family can get from a bond
ladder or annuity purchased at claim time with the full lump sum. The exception: a family that has demonstrated
inability to manage lump sums, for whom annuitisation is a feature, not a tax.

## The structural test for any rider

Three questions, in order:

1. **Does the base policy already cover this?** A health base policy covers hospitalisation; a hospital cash
   rider adds income, not coverage. A term base pays death; a CI rider pays living costs after diagnosis. If
   the answer to "does it duplicate?" is yes, the rider is a second purchase of the same risk.
2. **Is the trigger definition precise?** Read the definition clause: "heart attack of specified severity" with
   the troponin and ECG criteria listed, "total permanent disablement" with the schedule of losses. Riders with
   vague definitions are claims disputes waiting for a family.
3. **Is the price defensible against a standalone?** The rider must be cheaper than the equivalent standalone
   product, or it is mispriced convenience. CI riders pass; RoP fails everywhere.

## The legality note

Riders are regulated products: IRDAI's 2022 guidelines on product design fixed the cap on aggregate rider
premiums (30% of base premium for term products; the caps differ for other product classes), required
standardised definitions for common riders, and mandated that the policy document state each rider's terms
clearly and separately. Mis-selling a rider — selling a rider without explaining the trigger, or bundling
riders into a quoted "premium" without disclosure — is a complaint that the insurer's grievance officer, the
Bima Bharosa portal and the insurance ombudsman all entertain, and awards can include the refund of premium
with interest. The free-look period (15 days, 30 for digital sales and for policies sold to senior citizens)
applies to riders as part of the policy; use it to read the definitions and cancel what you do not want. What
carries consequences for the _buyer_: a rider claim built on a misstated health history at proposal is
repudiable for material non-disclosure, and the stakes are the rider's benefit — disclosure duties apply to
riders exactly as to base policies. The guidelines are at
[irdai.gov.in](https://irdai.gov.in).

## The habit to build

When a policy is quoted, ask for the base premium and each rider priced separately, in writing. Compare each
rider to (a) what the base already covers and (b) the cheapest standalone equivalent. Buy only the riders that
pass both tests. The typical result on a ₹1 crore term policy: two riders kept, four declined, and roughly
₹15,000 a year redirected from decoration to a health policy's sum insured — where it buys something that
actually pays a claim.
