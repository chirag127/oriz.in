Motor insurance is the one policy every Indian household must buy by law — and the one where the retail
decision is almost always made at the dealer's desk in ten minutes. The standard choice presented: comprehensive
or "zero depreciation". The dealer pushes zero-dep because it pays more commission. The insurer's price for it
is roughly 30-50% more on the own-damage premium. Whether it is worth it is not a matter of opinion; it is
arithmetic on your car's age, the parts that get replaced in a claim, and the depreciation schedule the insurer
applies when it does not.

## The depreciation schedule, which is the whole story

When a comprehensive policy pays for damaged _parts_, it deducts depreciation based on the part's age. The
schedule for fibreglass and rubber-plastic components (bumpers, headlights, wipers, tyres' outer sections) is
fixed by the Indian Motor Tariff framework; metal parts depreciate by vehicle age.

| Component                                                     | Depreciation applied without zero-dep                |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Paint work                                                    | 50% on material cost                                 |
| Rubber, plastic, nylon parts (bumpers, brushes, tyres' tubes) | 50%                                                  |
| Fibreglass components                                         | 30%                                                  |
| Battery                                                       | 40% (at 4+ years), 30% (2-4), 25% (under 2)          |
| Metal parts by vehicle age                                    | 0-5 yrs: 5-40% escalating; over 10 yrs: by agreement |
| Tyres (replacement)                                           | 50%                                                  |

A zero-depreciation (zero-dep or bumper-to-bumper) add-on waives these deductions — the insurer pays the full
replacement cost of the parts, subject to the sum insured and the policy's own limits (typically: a limit on the
number of zero-dep claims per year, usually 2, and exclusions for consumables unless separately covered).

## The claim, worked

A 3-year-old compact SUV, insured declared value (IDV) ₹9,00,000, hits a wall. Repairs: front bumper and headlamp
replacement (₹28,000 plastic/fibreglass), one door metal panel (₹18,000), paint work (₹12,000), tyre (₹9,000),
battery (₹8,000), labour and consumables (₹15,000).

| Head                        | Without zero-dep            | With zero-dep          |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Bumper + headlamp (₹28,000) | −50% = ₹14,000 paid         | ₹28,000                |
| Door metal panel (₹18,000)  | −20% (3-yr metal) = ₹14,400 | ₹18,000                |
| Paint (₹12,000)             | −50% = ₹6,000               | ₹12,000                |
| Tyre (₹9,000)               | −50% = ₹4,500               | ₹9,000 (where covered) |
| Battery (₹8,000)            | −30% = ₹5,600               | ₹8,000 (where covered) |
| Labour/consumables          | ₹15,000                     | ₹15,000                |
| **Total payable**           | **₹59,500**                 | **₹90,000**            |
| Deductible (compulsory)     | ₹2,000                      | ₹2,000                 |
| **Net received**            | ₹57,500                     | ₹88,000                |

The zero-dep claim pays ₹30,500 more on this single repair. The premium difference for zero-dep on this car:
roughly ₹5,000-7,000 a year (own-damage premium of ~₹12,000 becomes ~₹18,000). One claim in two years pays for
the add-on twice over. The add-on's break-even is close to one claim every two years — and Indian claim
frequency for cars up to five years old is close to that in urban areas.

## When zero-dep stops making sense

The depreciation deductions are computed on _parts prices_, which do not shrink with the car's value. On an
8-year-old hatchback with IDV ₹2,20,000, the zero-dep premium delta is still ~₹3,000-4,000, but the maximum
additional payout per claim is bounded by the parts prices of a cheap car — typically ₹10,000-15,000 — and
insurers often stop offering zero-dep beyond 5-7 years of car age (varies: some go to 10, some cap at 3 for
certain models).

| Car age     | IDV (illustrative) | Zero-dep premium delta | Typical extra payout per claim | Verdict                                 |
| ----------- | ------------------ | ---------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| New-2 years | ₹12,00,000         | ₹6,000-9,000           | ₹35,000-50,000                 | Take it                                 |
| 3-5 years   | ₹7,00,000          | ₹5,000-7,000           | ₹25,000-40,000                 | Take it                                 |
| 5-7 years   | ₹4,00,000          | ₹3,000-5,000           | ₹12,000-20,000                 | Marginal; claim frequency matters       |
| 7-10 years  | ₹2,00,000          | ₹2,000-4,000           | ₹8,000-15,000                  | Usually skip; some insurers won't offer |

The second variable is the _type of driving_. Zero-dep pays off in the dent-and-replace pattern of city driving
(bumpers, lamps, panels). On highway-heavy usage with fewer, larger claims, the metal-part deductions dominate
and the add-on's edge narrows.

## The IDV trap, which is bigger than the add-on decision

The IDV — insured declared value — is the policy's maximum payout and roughly the car's market value. At
renewal, insurers sometimes offer a lower IDV to cut the premium (and sometimes the dealer's online quote
simply plugs in a low IDV). A car worth ₹6,00,000 insured at ₹4,50,000 saves ₹800 of premium and hands up to
₹1,50,000 of claim settlement away — the insurer pays the lower of repair cost and IDV in constructive-total-loss
situations (repair cost over 75% of IDV is the conventional total-loss threshold). Check the IDV at every
renewal against the market value of your model on used-car platforms; it should decline roughly 10-15% a year,
not 25%.

**The third-party component is fixed by law.** The third-party premium for private cars is set by the IRDAI
annual notification (a ₹1,000-1,600 band for cars under 1,000cc to above 1,500cc, plus the raises by year). You
cannot choose it; you can only add the own-damage and add-on layers. Long-term third-party bundles (3-year for
new cars) are mandatory for new registrations since September 2018 — the dealer's "3-year insurance" figure is
mostly third-party plus the first year's own-damage, and the arithmetic of bundling should be checked against
buying own-damage separately.

## The other add-ons, quickly priced

| Add-on                          | What it does                                    | Worth it?                                          |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Zero depreciation               | Pays parts at full cost                         | Yes up to 5 years; marginal after                  |
| Engine protect                  | Covers hydrostatic lock, oil-leak engine damage | Yes for monsoon-city cars and automatics           |
| Consumables                     | Nuts, bolts, lubricants, AC gas                 | Yes, cheap (₹500-1,000), always deducted otherwise |
| Roadside assistance             | Towing, fuel, battery jump                      | Cheap; useful for old cars                         |
| NCB protect                     | Preserves no-claim bonus after one claim        | Worth it if NCB is 25-50% (see math)               |
| Key and lock, tyre protect, RTI | Marginal covers                                 | Skip unless priced under ₹500                      |
| Return to invoice (RTI)         | Pays original invoice price on total loss       | Yes for cars under 3 years old                     |

**The NCB arithmetic, because it beats everything.** The no-claim bonus accumulates 20% (year 1), 25%, 35%, 45%,
50% (year 5) off the own-damage premium — permanently, as long as no claim is made. On a ₹12,000 own-damage
premium, a 50% NCB saves ₹6,000 a year. Making a ₹9,000 claim burns the NCB: the lost bonus compounds at ₹6,000
a year for years. The rule of thumb: if the claim amount is under roughly 1.5× your annual NCB saving, pay it
yourself and preserve the bonus.

## The legality note

Driving uninsured is illegal: the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 (Section 146 read with the 2019 amendments and the
Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita's successor provisions for enforcement) mandates third-party liability cover for every
vehicle on a public road, and the penalties for non-insurance are fine and, on repeat offence, disqualification
of licence. Third-party claims are adjudicated by the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal, and insurers' third-party
liability is effectively unlimited for death and injury — which is why the third-party component is not a place
to economise. What is legal and routine: choosing zero-dep, engine protect, consumables and NCB protect — all
regulated add-ons whose terms IRDAI's product-filing norms standardise. What carries consequences: a claim
where the driver was unlicensed or drunk can be _settled with recovery_ — the insurer pays the third party and
recovers the amount from the insured — which is a contractual consequence, not a criminal one. The Motor
Vehicles Act and the IRDAI notifications are at
[irdai.gov.in](https://irdai.gov.in).

## The decision, in one line

Take zero-dep and consumables up to year five of a car you intend to keep, check the IDV at every renewal, pay
small claims in cash to preserve the NCB, and treat the dealer's finance desk as a sales channel rather than an
advisor — the arithmetic above is fifteen minutes, and it is the whole decision.
