Every surplus rupee between the EMI and the salary credit faces the same fork: send it to the loan account and
save 8.5-9 per cent guaranteed, or invest it and hope for 11-12 per cent. The framing is usually presented as a
personality test — "aggressive investors prepay nothing, safety-lovers prepay everything" — which is a way of
avoiding the arithmetic. The arithmetic is tractable, and it depends on four numbers: your loan rate, your tax
regime, your investment mix and your liquidity position.

## The return comparison, post-tax

A prepayment saves you the loan's interest rate, tax-free and risk-free — the loan rate is the return. An
investment earns its return, then tax. The honest comparison is therefore post-tax loan rate versus post-tax
investment return:

| Instrument / action         | Nominal return            | Tax treatment                | Post-tax return (30% slab / new regime) |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Prepay loan at 8.5%         | 8.5%                      | None (no interest to deduct) | **8.5%**                                |
| Index fund, equity          | ~12% (Nifty TRI long-run) | LTCG 12.5% above ₹1.25 lakh  | ~10.5%                                  |
| Debt fund / target maturity | ~7.5%                     | Slab on gains                | ~5.3%                                   |
| Senior citizen FD           | 7.5-8.3%                  | Slab on interest             | ~5.3-5.8%                               |
| EPF / PPF                   | 8.25% / 7.1%              | Exempt                       | 8.25% / 7.1%                            |
| NPS Tier 1 (E+G mix)        | ~10-11% blended           | Deferred; annuity taxed      | ~10% pre-exit                           |

Two rows change the standard answer. First, if you are on the old regime and claiming the Section 24(b) interest
deduction, the prepayment saves you _post-tax_ interest — the deduction reduces your effective loan rate, so
prepaying is worth less than it looks. A 30 per cent slab borrower with ₹2 lakh of deductible interest on an 8.5
per cent loan has a post-tax cost of roughly 6.0 per cent on the deductible portion, and prepaying that portion
earns the same 6.0 per cent post-tax. Second, under the new regime there is no 24(b) deduction at all, so the full
8.5 per cent is the prepayment's return — and it beats debt funds, FDs and most bonds outright.

| Regime                           | Effective loan cost (8.5% loan) | Prepayment "return" | Equity needs to beat it by |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Old regime, 30% slab, 24(b) used | ~6.0-7.2%                       | ~6.0-7.2%           | ~3.3-4.5 pts               |
| Old regime, 24(b) cap exhausted  | 8.5%                            | 8.5%                | ~2.0 pts                   |
| New regime                       | 8.5%                            | 8.5%                | ~2.0 pts                   |

## The liquidity argument, which is the real one

Prepayment is an irreversible exchange: money sent to the loan account cannot be retrieved without a top-up loan at
a higher rate, or an overdraft facility negotiated in advance. Investment is reversible. This asymmetry, not the
return, decides most cases:

| Surplus use                                    | Reversible?          | Emergency value     | Risk                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Regular prepayment                             | No                   | None                | Locks you in                               |
| Prepay into a loan-overdraft (offset) facility | Yes                  | Full                | Rate premium of 20-40 bps on some products |
| Equity SIP                                     | Yes, at market value | Subject to drawdown | Volatility, sequence risk                  |
| Debt fund / FD                                 | Yes                  | High                | Interest-rate and credit risk              |

The offset (overdraft-linked home loan) is the instrument that dissolves the dilemma for disciplined borrowers:
you park surpluses in the linked account, the balance reduces the interest-charging principal, and you can withdraw
it back. The cost is a slightly higher rate and the discipline trap in reverse — money you can withdraw is money
you will withdraw. Where the facility exists and the borrower has both surplus and irregular income (business
owners, consultants), it dominates plain prepayment.

## The decision rules that actually hold

1. **Emergency fund first.** No prepayment from money you might need within 24 months. The saving is a guaranteed
   8.5 per cent; the cost of an emergency loan at 10-14 per cent (top-up, personal loan) or a distress equity exit
   is worse.
2. **Compare against the portfolio's fixed-income leg.** If your asset allocation calls for 40 per cent debt, the
   marginal surplus belongs in the loan or debt instruments, whichever is higher after tax. Prepaying a 9 per cent
   loan while holding a 7 per cent FD is a 2 point loss you are choosing every month.
3. **Prepay early-year principal; invest late-year.** In the last third of the tenure, the interest component is
   small and the EMI is mostly principal you are retiring anyway — the marginal prepayment now saves interest over
   only a few remaining years, and equity's expected premium over the loan rate widens in your favour with a
   shorter horizon of exposure.
4. **Rate regime matters.** At 9.5 per cent floating, prepayment is a strong 9.5 per cent risk-free return and beats
   most debt outright. At 7 per cent (as in 2020-21), the case tilts to equity unless you are otherwise
   risk-averse. The RBI's repo path, published at [rbi.org.in](https://www.rbi.org.in/), is the upstream variable
   both your loan reset and your debt fund returns follow.
5. **The psychology clause.** A borrower who will lose sleep over the outstanding is not making an investment
   error by prepaying; the behavioural return of a cleared liability is real and unmeasured in every spreadsheet
   in this article. Just do it with eyes open about the return foregone.

## The hybrid that usually wins

For a salaried household with a stable income and a funded emergency buffer, the defensible standing instruction is
a **split**: direct 50-70 per cent of every surplus to the loan via a step-up (raise the EMI 5-10 per cent a year,
which clears a 20-year loan in roughly 14-15 years) and put the balance into an equity SIP that you touch only for
goals, never for topping up the loan. The step-up is the underrated instrument — a 10 per cent annual EMI increase
on a ₹50 lakh, 20-year, 8.5 per cent loan finishes it around year 13 and saves roughly ₹17-18 lakh of interest,
with no lump-sum discipline required.

| Strategy on ₹50 lakh / 20 yr / 8.5%     | Interest paid                         | Loan-free by                                |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Standard EMI                            | ₹54.1 lakh                            | Year 20                                     |
| 10% annual EMI step-up                  | ~₹36 lakh                             | Year ~13                                    |
| ₹5,000/month extra prepayment           | ~₹42 lakh                             | Year ~16                                    |
| ₹5,000/month into equity at 12% instead | Loan runs full term; corpus ~₹49 lakh | Year 20 (corpus ₹49 lakh vs ₹17 lakh saved) |

The last row is the honest equity case: ₹5,000 a month at 12 per cent for 20 years is about ₹49 lakh, against
₹17 lakh of interest saved plus the reduced principal if the same money had gone to the loan. On pure arithmetic
the equity path wins _if_ 12 per cent arrives and you never interrupt the SIP; the loan path wins _guaranteed_.
Which probability you should accept depends on the horizon and the purpose of the house — a home you will hold
through any market is a different collateral risk than one you might need to sell in a bad year.

## What the loan agreement says about prepayment

Zero prepayment penalty applies to floating-rate loans to individuals under RBI's rules on individual borrowers,
and most large lenders now advertise it. Verify three clauses anyway: whether part-prepayment triggers an EMI
recomputation (specify tenure reduction instead), whether the lender caps the number of free prepayments per year,
and whether an offset facility exists and what it costs. The RBI's framework on fair practices in lending and the
key fact statements lenders must provide are the reference documents here — read the KFS before assuming the
branch officer's verbal summary is the contract.

## The ORIZ worked example

The [home loan prepayment calculator](/tools/loan-prepayment-calculator/) models a fixed rate, a constant extra
monthly payment, and the same EMI. For a ₹50,00,000 loan at 8.5% with 240 months remaining, the baseline EMI is
about ₹43,391 and total interest is about ₹54,13,879.

| Extra per month | Total interest | Interest saved | Payoff | Months saved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ₹0 | ₹54,13,879 | — | 240 months | — |
| ₹5,000 | ₹40,24,629 | ₹13,89,250 | 187 months | 53 |
| ₹10,000 | ₹32,35,158 | ₹21,78,721 | 155 months | 85 |
| ₹20,000 | ₹23,46,759 | ₹30,67,120 | 116 months | 124 |

The ₹10,000 case pays off in 155 months, or 12 years and 11 months, instead of 20 years. The extra payments are
principal, not a fee: the household is converting cash into home equity earlier. The real comparison is between
that equity, the liquidity given up, and the after-tax return the same money could earn elsewhere.

The calculator does not include prepayment fees, changing floating rates, lender rounding, or tax effects. Confirm
the lender's part-prepayment rules and request the revised amortisation schedule in writing.
