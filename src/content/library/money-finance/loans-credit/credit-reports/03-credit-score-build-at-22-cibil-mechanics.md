A credit score is the first financial asset most Indians build without noticing, at exactly the age when its
future pricing power is being decided. A 22-year-old with a three-year-old card paid in full, utilisation
under 30% and no enquiries has a file that will price a home loan at 8.35% instead of 9.1% a decade later —
a difference worth lakhs. This article is the mechanics: what the bureaus actually compute, how a thin file
becomes a thick one, and the repair timeline for the mistakes that young earners make predictably.

## What the score is and what moves it

| Factor             | Weight (bureau-typical) | What young earners do wrong                              |
| ------------------ | ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Payment history    | ~35%                    | One missed EMI from a BNPL line reported late            |
| Credit utilisation | ~30%                    | ₹90,000 spent on a ₹1 lakh limit in a travel month       |
| Credit age         | ~15%                    | Closing the first card "because it has no rewards"       |
| Credit mix         | ~10%                    | Only BNPL; never a card or a small instalment            |
| Hard enquiries     | ~10%                    | Five card applications in a month for the sign-up offers |

Four bureaus operate in India (CIBIL, Experian, Equifax, CRIF); the score each reports is its own model on
the same underlying data, and lenders pull whichever they prefer. The annual free report from each bureau is
a statutory right — the CIBIL report is accessible via its
official portal, and disputes filed there are the formal correction route.

## The thin-file problem: how the first three years are spent

At 22 with no loans, the file is thin — the bureau cannot score what does not exist, and "no history" prices
almost as badly as "bad history" for a first home loan. The climb:

| Month | Action                                          | File effect                             |
| ----- | ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| 0     | Secured card against FD or entry unsecured card | First tradeline opens; age clock starts |
| 6     | Small purchase monthly, paid in full            | Payment history accrues                 |
| 12    | A consumer-durable or two-wheeler instalment    | Mix improves; age 12 months             |
| 24    | Limit enhancement requested (not a new card)    | Utilisation headroom improves           |
| 36    | Score crosses 750 on most models                | Unsecured approval becomes routine      |

The three years of light, punctual usage are the entire investment. The alternative — "I'll build credit when
I need the home loan" — means the loan at 30 meets a two-year-old file and a 8.9% rate instead of 8.35%, which
on a ₹50 lakh 20-year loan is roughly ₹1,700 a month, ₹4 lakh over the tenure.

## Utilisation: the number that moves the score within a billing cycle

Utilisation is the statement balance over the credit limit, reported per cycle. The mechanics young earners
miss:

- **The statement date, not the due date, is what gets reported.** Spending ₹80,000 and paying in full before
  the due date still reports 80% utilisation for that cycle if the statement cut first. Pay before the
  statement for the low number, or pay after and accept a one-cycle blip.
- **The 30% line is per card and aggregate.** A ₹50,000 limit split as ₹15,000 on card A and ₹20,000 on card
  B (limits ₹60,000 and ₹40,000) is 25% and 50% — the 50% card drags.
- **Limit enhancement is the cheapest fix.** A limit raise with unchanged spending mechanically lowers
  utilisation; a new card application adds a hard enquiry instead.
- **A big planned spend (laptop, travel) is manageable:** pay mid-cycle before the statement cut, and the
  reported balance stays low.

## The closed-card myth and the age you are throwing away

Closing the first entry-level card "because it has no rewards" deletes the file's oldest tradeline and drops
average age — a 3-5 year-old card is worth more as a line on the report than as 1% cashback. The correct
pattern: keep the first card active with one small subscription, autopaid in full, and let its age compound.
The cards that die from _issuer_ inactivity are a different matter — a card unused for years may be closed by
the issuer, so the one-small-transaction rule keeps both sides alive. Cards closed in good standing remain on
the report for years anyway (the bureau retains closed-account history), which is why the "closing hurts
immediately" belief is half-myth — what hurts is the age recomputation and the utilisation headroom shrinking.

## The repair timeline for the classic early mistakes

| Mistake                         | Damage                          | Repair timeline                                    |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Missed card payment (30 days)   | Score −50 to −80 points         | 6-12 months of clean history                       |
| Missed EMI (90+ days, NPA flag) | Score −100+; loan approval pain | 18-36 months clean                                 |
| Maxed-out card month            | Score −40 to −70                | 1-2 cycles of low utilisation                      |
| BNPL default                    | Bureau-reported after the delay | 12-24 months; check the report to confirm          |
| Settlement ("settled" status)   | Flags future lenders for years  | 3-5 years, or upgrade to "closed" via full payment |
| Multiple rejections in a month  | Enquiry cluster, small drop     | 6 months of no applications                        |

The repair mechanism is uniform and dull: punctual payments, low utilisation, no new applications. Paid-in-
full behaviour on a restructured EMI, a written settlement that says "closed" rather than "settled" where the
lender agrees, and the annual report check that catches reporting errors (wrong closure dates, duplicate
accounts, unpaid-after-paid lines) — disputed via the bureau's own portal with the bank's proof attached.

## The legality note

Credit reporting in India is regulated under the CICRA framework; the bureaus' own dispute mechanisms are the
statutory route, and the
[RBI](https://www.rbi.org.in/) integrated ombudsman escalates unresolved complaints. The grey and illegal
edges young earners meet: "credit repair" services promising score jumps for fees (nothing they do is beyond
the free dispute process — pay for nothing); buying a "score boost" via added-name tradelines from strangers
(an unregulated market with fraud and KYC-misuse exposure); and the friend's-loan-on-your-name favour, which
makes you the borrower in law regardless of who spends the money — the bureau consequence and the recovery
consequence are both yours.

## The bottom line

The file you build at 22-25 prices every loan you take at 30-40. One card held long and used lightly, the
30% utilisation line with the statement-date mechanic understood, no application clusters, an annual free
report read, and the early mistakes repaired on the standard timeline rather than "fixed" by a paid service.
The score is the rare financial asset that costs nothing but three years of boring behaviour — and it is
worth more than the card's cashback by an order of magnitude.
