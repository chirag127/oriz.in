When money is needed in days, the two fastest doors in Indian finance are the personal loan and the gold loan. They
look interchangeable from outside — both are unsecured in feel, both disburse quickly — but they are priced from
opposite premises: one is underwritten on your salary and score, the other on the resale value of the metal in the
locker. The right choice is usually decided by which premise you can satisfy, and the cost difference is larger
than most borrowers expect.

## The rate and cost comparison

| Cost element       | Personal loan                                 | Gold loan                                  |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Interest rate band | 10.5-24% p.a.                                 | 9-16% p.a. (NBFCs to ~24% on small-ticket) |
| Typical sanction   | ₹50,000-₹40 lakh                              | ₹10,000-₹1 crore+                          |
| Processing fee     | 1-3% of loan (often 2%)                       | 0.25-1% (often flat ₹250-₹1,000)           |
| Documentation      | Income proofs, bank statements, bureau pull   | PAN + Aadhaar; property of gold verified   |
| Disbursement speed | Same day to 3 days                            | 30 minutes to same day                     |
| Prepayment penalty | 2-5% on some products; nil at several banks   | Usually nil or nominal                     |
| Collateral         | None                                          | Pledged gold held in bank/NBFC custody     |
| Risk on default    | Recovery process, bureau damage, legal notice | Auction of pledged gold after notice       |

Two rows deserve expansion. First, the personal loan's headline rate is quoted for the best borrowers; the rate a
650-scorer actually gets from an NBFC app is 18-24 per cent, and the processing fee takes a bite at disbursement —
a ₹2 lakh loan with a 2 per cent fee means ₹1.96 lakh received and ₹2 lakh owed. Second, the gold loan's rate looks
uniform but is priced by loan-to-value and tenor; short-tenor bullet loans (interest paid at maturity) at 9-11 per
cent dominate the market, and the effective cost of a 12-month bullet at 10 per cent on ₹2 lakh is ₹20,000 plus a
small fee — cheaper than nearly any unsecured alternative.

## How LTV caps the gold loan

The RBI's LTV framework for loans against gold jewellery caps the loan at **75 per cent of the value of the gold
pledged** (the standalone NBFC framework tightened this further for small lenders in stages), with the value
determined by the lender's own valuation at the day's prevailing rate. The practical arithmetic for a household:

| Gold pledged (22K equivalent) | Valuation @ ₹74,000/10g | Maximum loan at 75% LTV |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------- |
| 20 grams                      | ₹1.48 lakh              | ₹1.11 lakh              |
| 50 grams                      | ₹3.70 lakh              | ₹2.77 lakh              |
| 100 grams                     | ₹7.40 lakh              | ₹5.55 lakh              |
| 200 grams                     | ₹14.8 lakh              | ₹11.1 lakh              |

(Prices are illustrative for 2025-26; the valuer's deduction for wastage, stones and purity bands means the
realisable loan is typically 10-15 per cent below the naive weight × price figure.) Note the asymmetry the table
hides: a rise in the gold price after you pledge does not raise your sanction, but a fall before you repay changes
the auction maths — which is the source of the gold loan's distinctive risk, discussed below.

## The risk profiles are not comparable

A personal loan's risk is a bureau and legal risk: missed EMIs produce DPD entries, recovery calls, and eventually
a legal notice or a civil suit; the exposure is financial and reputational, never custodial. A gold loan's risk is
custodial: the metal sits in the lender's vault, insured, and on default the lender issues notice and auctions the
pledge to recover the principal and interest. The consumer-protection question — whether the auction is conducted
fairly and whether surplus over the dues is returned — is where disputes concentrate.

The regulatory framework here is the RBI's Fair Practices Code for lenders and, for gold-loan NBFCs specifically,
the 2021-2024 regulatory framework requiring transparent auction notice periods, documented auction processes and
return of any surplus to the borrower, with auction conducted through a transparent mechanism and the borrower
given reasonable notice. Read the actual circulars at [rbi.org.in](https://www.rbi.org.in/) rather than relying on
the lender's verbal summary; the framework is what your **consequences** on default are built from — an auction
below the dues leaves you owing the difference, and an auction notice you ignored is not a defence.

There is also a documentation grey zone that borrowers should understand before pledging: ornaments with no
purchase invoice are accepted on the lender's own valuation and self-declaration, and households pledging
**inherited or scrap jewellery have historically been used as a channel for unaccounted gold**. The lender's KYC
and the Income Tax Act's cash-transaction limits apply to the loan, but the provenance of the metal is nobody's
problem until it becomes somebody's — a pledged-then-auctioned chain of unaccounted jewellery creates
**consequences** for the pledgee's own books, and the cleanest answer is to pledge only metal whose acquisition
you can document. The grey legality here is about provenance, not the loan itself: gold loans against properly
documented family gold are entirely legal and RBI-regulated.

## Which loan for which situation

| Situation                                           | Better fit                  | Why                                         |
| --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Salaried, 750+ score, ₹3-5 lakh need                | Personal loan from a bank   | Rate under 11-12%, no collateral friction   |
| Thin credit file or low score, gold at home         | Gold loan                   | Score matters little; LTV is the constraint |
| Very short tenor (1-6 months), known repayment date | Gold loan bullet            | Single-digit rate, interest at maturity     |
| Emergency needing money in hours                    | Gold loan                   | Physical verification is the only gate      |
| Small ticket, ₹30,000-₹1 lakh                       | Either; compare all-in cost | Fee structure decides the margin            |
| No gold at home                                     | Personal loan               | The constraint is dispositive               |

The all-in comparison must include the processing fee amortised over the tenor: a personal loan at 11 per cent with
a 2 per cent fee repaid over 12 months costs effectively about 12.6 per cent; a gold loan at 10 per cent with a
0.5 per cent fee costs about 10.5 per cent. On a ₹2 lakh, one-year loan, that is a ₹4,200 difference — real money,
worth the half-day a gold loan's documentation takes.

## One operational warning

Gold loan interest compounding is where borrowers lose money unknowingly. Bullet loans accrue interest monthly and
demand it at maturity; a borrower who lets two or three tenors lapse into penalty interest (the usual structure is
1-2 per cent per month on overdue amounts, plus the lender's right to auction after 90 days of default) can see
the effective cost climb from 10 per cent to 20-plus. The clean structure is: match the tenor to the certainty of
the repayment source, pay interest on schedule even if principal rolls over, and never let a gold loan drift past
its notice period into auction. A gold loan treated carelessly converts family jewellery into a lender's
inventory — and the auction recovery rate for the borrower's sentimental stakes is zero.
