The credit card is the cheapest loan in India — under 6 per cent effective if you use the grace period fully — and
one of the most expensive, at 40-46 per cent annualised the moment you revolve. Both facts are true of the same
card in the same month, and the switch between them is a single missed deadline or one month of paying the minimum
due. The mechanics below are what the statement abbreviations actually mean.

## The cycle, the statement and the grace period

Every card runs on three dates: the **statement date** (the cycle's cut-off, when all spends are frozen into a
bill), the **payment due date** (typically 15-20 days after statement), and the **grace period** — the
interest-free window from a purchase's posting date until the due date, _available only if the previous statement
was paid in full_.

| Element                      | Typical Indian issuer                      | Consequence if missed                    |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- |
| Statement cycle              | 30 days (some 28-31)                       | Next bill shifts accordingly             |
| Due date gap                 | 15-20 days after statement                 | Late fee ₹100-₹1,300 by bill size        |
| Grace (interest-free) period | Up to 45-50 days from earliest transaction | Lost entirely on revolving               |
| Minimum due                  | 5% of total due (floor ₹100-₹200)          | Keeps account current; triggers interest |

The minimum due is the single most misunderstood number on the statement. Paying it avoids the late fee and a
default report to the bureau — it keeps the account "current" — but it does _not_ buy the interest-free period.
From the moment you pay less than the total due, interest accrues on the **entire outstanding, from the date of
each transaction**, not on the balance remaining. This is the RBI-mandated method and it is why one month of
minimum-due payment can cost more interest than the card's annual fee.

## The interest arithmetic, worked

Take a card at 42 per cent annualised (3.5 per cent monthly — the rate printed as "3.5% p.m." in the MITC document).
You spent ₹60,000 through the month, the statement shows ₹60,000 total due and ₹3,000 minimum due. You pay ₹3,000.

| Item                                                                                    | Interest charge                                     |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| ₹60,000 outstanding, interest from each transaction date, ~25 days average to statement | ~₹1,460                                             |
| Same balance carried to next statement (~30 more days)                                  | ~₹1,750                                             |
| New purchases in the following cycle: no grace period                                   | Interest from day of each spend                     |
| Cash withdrawal, regardless of bill payment                                             | Interest from day one, plus 2.5-3.5% withdrawal fee |
| Total to keep ₹57,000 revolving for one cycle                                           | **~₹3,200+ (≈₹3,200/57,000 ≈ 5.6% for the month)**  |

Annualised, revolving costs not 42 per cent but effectively more once you stack the loss of grace on new spends.
The RBI's 2022 framework on fair practices in credit card issuing forced issuers to state this in plain words in
the MITC (Most Important Terms and Conditions): interest on revolving credit applies from the transaction date to
the payment date on the full outstanding, and failure to pay the total due kills the interest-free period on new
transactions as well.

## The RBI rules that changed the statement

Since the July 2024 circular tightening card issuance, and the 2022 master direction framework before it, several
abuses are gone: issuers cannot bill interest on interest on revolved amounts in the same cycle through
capitalisation tricks without disclosure, must obtain explicit consent before activating a card, must bill
unbilled instalments transparently on EMI conversions, and must publish the grace period's conditionality plainly.
The full texts live at [rbi.org.in](https://www.rbi.org.in/) under the Master Direction – Credit Card and Debit
Card – Issuance and Conduct Directions; the paragraph that matters to a revolver is the interest-charging
methodology one, and it is worth reading once.

What the rules do _not_ protect you from: the compounding that legitimately follows a genuine revolver, the cash
advance fee and its immediate interest (there is no grace period on ATM cash, by design), and the EMI-conversion
upsell at checkout, where a "no-cost EMI" often carries a hidden discount foregone (the merchant's offer price
without EMI is frequently lower) plus GST on the interest component.

## The discipline that makes the card free

| Habit                                                   | What it preserves                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Pay the **total** due, not the minimum, by the due date | Grace period on all future spends                    |
| Pay by autopay for the full amount, not the minimum     | Removes the human deadline failure                   |
| Keep utilisation under 30% per statement                | Score effect, and intra-cycle reporting              |
| Avoid cash withdrawals except emergencies               | No grace period on cash, 2.5-3.5% fee                |
| One card, one cycle, one statement date                 | Every card's grace period is independently breakable |

The last row is the quiet one: grace periods do not pool across cards. A household with three cards from three
issuers maintains three due dates and three revolve traps; the discipline load triples. Consolidating to one or two
cards with different cycle dates (early-month and mid-month statements) spreads large spends across cycles and
keeps both grace periods alive with one autopay each.

## When revolving is the rational choice — rarely

There are legitimate cases: an emergency in a month with no liquidity, where 3.5 per cent monthly on a card
undercuts a personal loan's approval timeline, or a one-cycle cash-flow gap in a business you can prove will close.
Even then, the correct structure is to convert the outstanding to an EMI at the moment of revolving — most issuers
offer 6-12 month conversion at 14-18 per cent annualised, which is dramatically cheaper than open-ended 42 per cent
revolving — and to pre-plan the payoff month. The 42 per cent open revolver is a bridge between statements, not a
financing instrument; a borrower who carries a card balance for more than two consecutive cycles should refinance
it with anything — a personal loan, a top-up on a home loan, even a gold loan — that prices under 20 per cent.

## The credit report angle

The minimum due keeps the bureau record clean (DPD '0' on the report), which is why it is a trap and not a
default: you can pay it for two years, never miss a due date, keep a 780 score — and still hand the issuer ₹3,000
a month in interest that the score never reflects. The score measures discipline, not cost. The interest bill is
measured on your statement, and the two numbers are only related by whether you read both.
