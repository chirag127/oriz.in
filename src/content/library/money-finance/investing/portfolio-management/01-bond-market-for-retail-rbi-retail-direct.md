India's retail bond market stopped being theoretical in 2021, when the RBI opened government-securities
participation to individuals through the Retail Direct platform — a free, direct account with the central bank's
own dealing window. Yet most Indian portfolios still contain zero bonds outside FDs, because the space looks
impenetrable. This article is the penetration: what the instruments are, what the yields mean, and where each
belongs.

## The instruments, one by one

| Instrument                            | What it is                                          | Typical yield (FY 2025-26, indicative) | Tenure                    |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Treasury bills (91/182/364-day)       | Discount instruments — no coupon, issued below face | ~6.0–6.7%                              | Up to 1 year              |
| New G-Secs (10-year benchmark etc.)   | Coupon-paying sovereign bonds                       | ~6.5–7.0%                              | 5–40 years                |
| State Development Loans (SDLs)        | State government bonds, sovereign-backed            | G-Sec + 30–80 bps                      | 10+ years                 |
| Sovereign Green Bonds                 | G-Secs earmarked for green spend                    | Near G-Sec yields                      | Long                      |
| Corporate bonds (AAA/PSU)             | Company-issued, rated                               | G-Sec + 50–120 bps                     | 1–10 years                |
| Corporate bonds (AA and below)        | Higher credit risk                                  | G-Sec + 150–400 bps                    | Various                   |
| RBI Floating Rate Savings Bonds, 2020 | Government bond, rate = NSC + 0.35%                 | ~8.05% as of FY 2025-26                | 7 years, non-transferable |
| Listed bond-market ETFs / debt MFs    | Fund wrappers over the above                        | Varies                                 | Liquid                    |

The G-Sec yield curve is the risk-free baseline for everything else; the spreads above it are payment for credit
risk, liquidity risk, or both. RBI Retail Direct publishes live yields at
[rbidirect.rbi.org.in](https://www.rbidirect.rbi.org.in/) — and the RBI's own site
([rbi.org.in](https://www.rbi.org.in/)) carries the auction calendar and results.

## RBI Retail Direct: the mechanics

One-time, free registration with PAN, Aadhaar, mobile and a bank account. The account gives: participation in
primary auctions (non-competitive bidding — you get the cut-off yield), secondary-market trading through the
NDS-OM platform, a direct gilt securities account (no demat needed for these), and mobile/Demat conversion
options. Settlement is T+1. There are no account fees, no transaction charges, and the interest is credited
straight to your bank account.

| Feature            | Detail                                                           |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Account            | Free, one per PAN                                                |
| Bidding            | Non-competitive: up to ₹2 crore per auction at the cut-off yield |
| Primary allocation | Guaranteed at cut-off — no bid-price risk                        |
| Secondary trading  | NDS-OM, retail segment, T+1                                      |
| Interest           | Direct credit; coupon taxable at slab                            |
| Custody            | Gilt account with RBI (or convert to demat)                      |

Non-competitive bidding is the retail superpower: you receive the auction's cut-off yield exactly, without
pricing a bid. The consequence is that primary participation in a 10-year G-Sec at 6.9% is a _known_ yield at a
_known_ credit quality — the two properties FDs pretend to offer.

## The yield math, honestly

Government bonds trade near risk-free yields, so the comparison with FDs and with inflation must be run before
any allocation:

| Instrument                     | Yield                                           | Tax treatment      | Post-tax at 30% | Real at 6% inflation |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | --------------- | -------------------- |
| 364-day T-Bill                 | ~6.4%                                           | Slab (STCG/deemed) | ~4.5%           | −1.5%                |
| 10-year G-Sec held to maturity | ~6.9%                                           | Slab               | ~4.8%           | −1.2%                |
| G-Sec held > 36 months, listed | LTCG 12.5% without indexation (post-2024 rules) | 12.5% on gains     | —               | —                    |
| FD, major bank 3-year          | ~7.1%                                           | Slab               | ~5.0%           | −1.0%                |
| FR Savings Bond 2020           | ~8.05%                                          | Slab               | ~5.6%           | −0.4%                |
| AAA corporate 5-year           | ~7.6–7.9%                                       | Slab               | ~5.4%           | −0.6%                |

Two honest conclusions. First, at slab taxation, bonds are _not_ return-maximisers — they are the part of the
portfolio whose job is certainty of outcome, priced at −1% real. Second, the _listed_ route with 36-month-plus
holding gets 12.5% LTCG treatment without indexation (post-July 2024 rules), which at these yields is meaningfully
better than slab taxation for high earners — one of the few remaining tax-efficient fixed-income structures for
retail.

## The risks, named

**Interest-rate risk.** A 10-year G-Sec bought at 6.9% falls in price if yields rise to 7.6%; the marked-to-market
loss on a 10-year paper for a 70 bps move is roughly 5–6%. Hold to maturity and the risk vanishes — the coupon
and principal arrive exactly as booked. **Liquidity risk.** The retail NDS-OM segment is thinner than the
institutional one; exiting a large position before maturity can mean accepting a wider spread. Size positions
assuming you _will_ hold to maturity. **Reinvestment risk.** Coupons arrive periodically at whatever yields then
prevail — the ladder's benefit and its curse. **Credit risk.** Zero for G-Secs/SDLs (sovereign), real for
corporates: the AA and below universe has produced genuine defaults — the IL&FS and DHFL episodes taught the
market that "rated" is not "safe". Corporate bond allocation should stay with AAA/PSU names and short tenures
until you can read a credit report.

## Where each instrument belongs

| Portfolio slot               | Best instrument                              | Why                                         |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Emergency fund layer 3       | 91/182/364-day T-Bill ladder                 | Sovereign, liquid, dated                    |
| Goal 1–3 years away          | T-Bills and short G-Secs                     | Certainty of value at the date              |
| Goal 3–10 years, high slab   | Listed G-Secs/SDLs held 36+ months           | 12.5% LTCG without indexation               |
| The "retirement debt sleeve" | Laddered G-Secs 5–10 yr + FR Savings Bonds   | Known coupons, floating-rate ballast        |
| Yield chase                  | AAA corporate bonds, short tenure            | The only spread worth taking at retail size |
| Never                        | NCDs of unrated or A-below issuers for yield | Credit risk mispriced by retail             |

## The legality note

Retail Direct and the bond market are fully regulated — the RBI governs G-Secs and the Retail Direct framework;
SEBI governs listed corporate bonds and debt funds ([sebi.gov.in](https://www.sebi.gov.in/)). The consequences
live in the credit universe: bonds of issuers below investment grade carry documented default history with
recoveries taking years and haircuts measured in tens of percent; unlisted or obscure "high-yield" paper sold
through WhatsApp-grade platforms is where retail bond losses concentrate. Stick to the sovereign curve, the
listed AAA tier, and the RBI's own products, and the legality note becomes one line: everything described here
is plain vanilla, regulated, and — for the sovereign part — backed by the same government that prints the rupee.
