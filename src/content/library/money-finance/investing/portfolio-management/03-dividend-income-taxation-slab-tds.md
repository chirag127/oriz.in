Dividends changed character in the 2020 Budget: for a quarter century they were tax-free in the shareholder's
hands because companies paid dividend distribution tax; from 1 April 2020 the DDT went away and dividends
returned to the shareholder's own tax slab, TDS-deducted and AIS-reported. A ₹3 lakh dividend stream is now
₹3 lakh of ordinary income. Everything about dividend investing — which account holds the stocks, whether the
"high dividend yield" portfolio makes sense for your bracket, how the TDS flows — follows from that single
structural change.

## The current rules, stated plainly

| Rule                       | Detail (FY 2025-26)                                                                                |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tax head                   | Income from other sources (unless you hold stock as trading stock)                                 |
| Rate                       | Your slab — nothing special                                                                        |
| TDS (resident)             | 10% under Section 194 above ₹5,000 per company per year                                            |
| TDS threshold (FY 2025-26) | ₹5,000 aggregate per company; companies may net-claim across filings                               |
| Surcharge                  | Applies as per income slabs (15%/25%/37% at high incomes, capped at 15% on capital gains for some) |
| foreign dividends          | Slab rate; foreign tax credit under DTAA/section 91 with Form 67                                   |
| Expense deduction          | Up to 20% of dividend income (interest on loans to buy shares, etc.)                               |

The ₹5,000 TDS threshold is per company: a shareholder of one company receiving ₹6,000 a year meets 10% TDS;
the same ₹6,000 spread across six companies meets no TDS. TDS is not extra tax — it is prepayment, claimed as
credit in 26AS at filing — but a retiree whose total income is below the taxable limit files Form 15G/15H to
stop deduction at source, and a shareholder whose slab is 5% lends the government 5% of dividends at zero
interest until refund.

## The bracket arithmetic that decides whether dividend stocks fit

Dividends are slab-taxed, so their after-tax yield depends on who holds them:

| Holder's slab         | Gross yield | Tax  | Net yield |
| --------------------- | ----------- | ---- | --------- |
| Nil (below exemption) | 3.5%        | 0%   | 3.5%      |
| 5% (new regime band)  | 3.5%        | 5%   | 3.33%     |
| 20%                   | 3.5%        | 20%  | 2.80%     |
| 30% + surcharge       | 3.5%        | 31%+ | 2.42%     |

The same company's retained earnings compound inside the stock tax-free until sale, then face capital-gains
tax (12.5% long-term above ₹1.25 lakh under the post-July 2024 rules). For a 30%-slab holder, the comparison
is 2.4% net dividend income today versus deferred 12.5% capital gains later — which is why high-dividend
portfolios in India have always been a lower-bracket or tax-advantaged-account idea, and why growth compounding
beats yield-chasing at high brackets. The dividend decision is a tax decision first.

## The 20% expense deduction and its narrow uses

Section 57 allows a deduction of up to 20% of dividend income for expenses _laid out to earn it_ — chiefly
interest on money borrowed to buy the shares. It is not a general basket for brokerages, advisory fees or
internet bills, and the deduction applies only against dividend income taxed as other sources. A shareholder
who borrowed ₹5 lakh at 9% to build a dividend portfolio spends ₹45,000 a year in interest and may deduct up
to 20% of the dividend income received — which, at ₹3 lakh of dividends, is the full ₹45,000 (the cap is
₹60,000). The arithmetic only works when dividend yield comfortably exceeds borrowing cost after tax;
financing dividends with personal loans at 11-13% is the classic way this turns negative.

## Foreign dividends and the credit machinery

Dividends from foreign stocks (via international brokerages or as a shareholder of US-listed companies) are
taxed at slab in India, with the foreign country's withholding — the US's 25% default for non-treaty
residents, 25%→15%/25% under the India-US treaty via W-8BEN — credited under DTAA rules or Section 91 where
no treaty exists. The mechanics: file Form 67 within the due date of the return, keep the foreign broker's
tax slips and bank remittance trail, and claim the credit limited to Indian tax on that income. Foreign
dividend streams also meet the ₹20 lakh LRS remittance context (already covered in the NRI track's LRS
article); the tax point is simple — gross-up, claim credit, keep documents.

| Source                     | Foreign withholding     | India tax | Credit route           |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------- | --------- | ---------------------- |
| US stocks (W-8BEN, treaty) | 15% (qualified, treaty) | Slab      | DTAA + Form 67         |
| US stocks (no W-8BEN)      | 25-30%                  | Slab      | DTAA + Form 67         |
| UK, Singapore (treaty)     | 10-15%                  | Slab      | DTAA + Form 67         |
| No-treaty jurisdiction     | Local rate              | Slab      | Section 91 (FTC rules) |

## The portfolio decisions the tax change drives

**Account placement matters more than stock choice.** A 30%-slab earner holding dividend payers in the
personal account and growth stocks in the same account makes no distinction — India has no tax-sheltered
brokerage wrapper — but the _composition_ decision changes: at 30%, prefer companies that retain and compound
over those that distribute, unless the cash flow itself is the goal (retirees at nil-to-5% brackets are the
natural dividend holders). **The dividend-only income idea has a tax ceiling:** ₹12 lakh of dividends at the
30% slab is ₹3.6 lakh of tax plus surcharge possibilities; the same companies' price appreciation would face
12.5% only at sale. **TDS discipline:** companies deduct on the register-holder; holding shares across
family members splits the ₹5,000 thresholds and slabs — the joint-holding structure is a legitimate split of
income by ownership, not an avoidance scheme, provided the money was genuinely the holder's.

## The legality note

Everything in this article is plain law: dividends are taxed at slab since 1 April 2020, TDS under Section
194 applies above ₹5,000, and Section 57 allows the 20% expense deduction with documentation. The failure
modes: not reporting dividends at all (the AIS lists them by company — concealment penalty 200% under Section
270A), claiming the 20% deduction without genuine expense links (disallowance plus 270A misreporting), and
gifting shares to family and treating their dividends as one's own non-income (income from gifted assets is
clubbed under Section 64 for spouse and minor children — legitimate transfers with genuine ownership transfer
are legal; transfers in name only are clubbing, and the clubbing provision's consequences include reopening
assessments). Current thresholds and forms:
[incometax.gov.in](https://incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/).

## The bottom line

Dividends are ordinary income again, and every dividend-investing decision should be re-run through that
fact: the ₹5,000 per-company TDS line, the slab arithmetic, the 20% expense cap, and the foreign-credit
machinery. Hold dividend payers where their yield is taxed least and their cash flow is needed; prefer
compounding at high brackets; and file 15G/15H when genuinely eligible to stop the deduction that creates
refund paperwork for no reason.
