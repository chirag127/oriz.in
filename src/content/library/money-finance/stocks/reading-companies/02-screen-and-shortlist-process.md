The difference between investors who improve and investors who repeat the same decade is not intelligence or
information. It is having a _process_ — a fixed sequence of filters that turns the universe of Indian listed
stocks into a small, defensible shortlist, and a journal that records why each decision was made so that the
record can be argued with later. This article is that process, written as something you can actually run: four
stages, each with numeric gates, each producing a smaller list, ending in a portfolio sized by rules rather than
conviction.

## Stage 1: the universe and the screen

Roughly 5,000 stocks trade on Indian exchanges; about 1,000 have enough institutional coverage and disclosure
quality to be analysable by an individual. Start by eliminating what you cannot analyse rather than by ranking
what looks best. A defensible baseline screen, using the most recent annual data:

| Filter                             | Threshold                             | Rationale                                               |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Market capitalisation              | Over ₹1,000 crore                     | Liquidity, disclosure depth, less manipulation          |
| Sales growth (3-yr CAGR)           | Over 8%                               | Business is not shrinking                               |
| Earnings growth (3-yr CAGR)        | Over 10%                              | Profit engine works                                     |
| ROCE (3-yr average)                | Over 12%                              | Capital earns more than its cost                        |
| Debt / equity                      | Under 1.0                             | Survival first; excludes financials, handled separately |
| Interest coverage                  | Over 3×                               | Debt serviceable even in a bad year                     |
| Promoter holding + pledge          | Pledge under 25% of promoter stake    | The classic collapse precursor                          |
| Receivables growth vs sales growth | Receivables not outpacing sales 2 yrs | Accounting quality first pass                           |

Applied honestly, this screen usually leaves 150-300 names. That is the point: the screen's job is not to find
winners, it is to _discard_ what cannot be defended. Every filter above has a legitimate exception — banks and
NBFCs are debt-financed by design and need their own screens (asset quality: gross NPA, provisioning coverage,
PCR); commodities fail the ROCE filter in a trough. Make the exceptions explicit and small.

## Stage 2: the evidence pass, 30 minutes per company

Each surviving candidate gets a fixed 30-minute pass, in the same order every time. Fixed order matters: it
prevents enthusiasm about a story from deciding what you look at.

1. **The last annual report, first hour** — auditor's report, related-party note, cash-flow-versus-profit over
   five years (the full method is in the annual report article in this track).
2. **Shareholding pattern, 8 quarters** — promoter trend, pledge trend, institutional trend, and whether any
   single entity controls more than 60% of the votes with 25% economic interest.
3. **The last two years of quarterly numbers** — not to forecast, but to check for pattern breaks: margin
   inflections, sudden "other income", receivable spikes in the March quarter (the Indian financial year-end,
   where revenue recognition games cluster).
4. **A competitor with the same lens** — any thesis of the form "this company is good" needs a comparator.
   Margin 4 points above the nearest peer for three years is either a moat or a misstatement; find out which.
5. **Promoter background and litigation search** — past ventures, defaults, SEBI orders (search the name on
   SEBI's orders page), and pending cases. Ten minutes; has killed more theses than any spreadsheet.

Output: a one-page note per company with five numbers you verified (revenue, margin, ROCE, debt/equity, cash
conversion), two things you could not verify, and the price at which the company would be interesting. Typically
this pass eliminates half the survivors, leaving 60-150.

## Stage 3: the thesis and the kill conditions

For anything left, write the thesis in three sentences: what the company does, why it will earn more in five
years than today, and what the market is mispricing. If you cannot write it in three sentences without a
spreadsheet, you do not have a thesis; you have a mood.

Then write **kill conditions** — the observable developments that would prove the thesis wrong:

- Margin falls below X% for two consecutive quarters → thesis invalid.
- Promoter stake falls by more than Y percentage points, or pledge rises above Z% → exit.
- Auditor resignation, qualified opinion, CFO departure inside 12 months of appointment → exit first, ask later.
- Receivables grow faster than sales for two years → accounting quality review, default exit pending.
- The specific growth driver (a plant, a contract, a regulatory approval) does not materialise by its stated
  date → thesis has no fuel.

The discipline is not the conditions themselves — it is writing them _before_ you buy, when you are still
capable of objectivity. Kill conditions written after the price falls are rationalisations.

| Thesis type                           | Typical kill condition                         | Typical holding horizon |
| ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Quality compounder at fair price      | ROCE sustained under 12% for 2 years           | 5-10 years              |
| Cyclical at trough                    | Capacity additions by peers exceed demand      | 2-4 years               |
| Turnaround with new management        | Debt not reduced by year 2 milestone           | 3-5 years               |
| Special situation (demerger, buyback) | Event completes without the expected re-rating | 6-18 months             |

## Stage 4: sizing, execution and the journal

**Position sizing by conviction and evidence, not by price.** A working rule: full position is 6-10% of the
equity portfolio; entry is in two or three tranches; no single illiquid small-cap exceeds 5% until it has been
held for four quarters. Two positions at 8% with kill conditions written is a portfolio. Twenty positions at 3%
without theses is a hobby that mimics an index fund with extra fees.

**Liquidity discipline.** Check the average daily traded value before sizing: a position that would take 15
trading days to exit at normal volumes is a position you may not be able to exit when the kill condition trips.
Cap any such position at 3% of the portfolio.

**The journal.** For every buy, record: date, price, thesis (3 sentences), kill conditions, expected holding
period, and — crucially — what you expect to be true in 12 months that the market does not yet believe. For
every sell, record which condition fired (or why none did). Once a year, read your own journal and count:
how many theses were right, how many kill conditions were correct and ignored, and what your average
held-period was versus the planned one. This is the only feedback loop in investing that reliably improves the
investor rather than the market timing.

## The legality note

Nothing in a research process carries legal risk until the information you use stops being public. Acting on
non-public price-sensitive information — an unannounced acquisition, unpublished results, a forthcoming order
win — is insider trading under the SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015, with penalties
including disgorgement, trading bans and prosecution for serious cases; SEBI has applied this to retail
investors and analysts, not only company insiders. Public filings, annual reports, exchange disclosures,
concall transcripts and management interviews are safe. Forward-looking claims made by companies on social media
are subject to SEBI's disclosure norms — if a company states something material informally, the exchange
filing must follow; if it does not, the claim was not compliant and you should discount it. The framework is at
[sebi.gov.in](https://www.sebi.gov.in).

## What the process cannot do

No funnel finds the next multi-bagger. What a process does is narrower and more valuable: it makes your
mistakes _repeatable and inspectable_, it prevents the majority of disasters that come from weak balance sheets
and stained promoters, and it frees your analytical attention for the few companies where it can actually
compound. Most of the return of a disciplined stock-picker over a decade comes from the stocks they refused to
buy and the ones they exited on schedule. The screen gets the credit; the journal does the work.
