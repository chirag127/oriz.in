A sinking fund is a savings line with a name and a deadline. Not "savings" — a named pool for an expense you can
see coming: the school-fee quarter, the insurance renewal, the festival month, the car's 40,000 km service, the
laptop that will die in year four. The expense is not a surprise; only its _timing_ surprises people who never
pre-funded it. The entire mechanism is arithmetic — annual estimate ÷ months remaining — but the effect is
disproportionate: it converts the four worst months of the Indian financial calendar into non-events.

## Why "miscellaneous" fails and named lines work

Household budgets fail on irregular expenses for a predictable reason: monthly budgets model monthly expenses, and
irregular expenses arrive as shocks. A family earning ₹1.2 lakh a month, spending ₹1.1 lakh monthly, feels fully
funded — until March brings ₹45,000 of school fees, April brings two insurance renewals totalling ₹28,000, and
October brings Diwali plus a wedding gift season. Each shock gets met with a credit card, a personal loan, or a
withdrawal from investments at the worst possible moment. The money was always going to be spent; the sinking fund
is simply the decision to _spread it in advance_ instead of financing it after.

| Irregular expense                   | Typical annual cost | Monthly set-aside | Shock it prevents               |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------------------- |
| School fees (2 children, quarterly) | ₹1,20,000           | ₹10,000           | The ₹40,000 quarter             |
| Health + term insurance premiums    | ₹36,000             | ₹3,000            | The renewal that lapses         |
| Festivals and gifts                 | ₹40,000             | ₹3,300            | The Diwali credit-card hangover |
| Vehicle service + insurance         | ₹30,000             | ₹2,500            | The workshop surprise           |
| Home maintenance and repairs        | ₹36,000             | ₹3,000            | The geyser/AC/plumbing month    |
| Travel (one family trip)            | ₹60,000             | ₹5,000            | The "we'll figure it out" trip  |
| Electronics replacement cycle       | ₹48,000 every 3 yrs | ₹1,300            | The dead-laptop emergency       |

Total monthly set-aside in that table: ₹28,100 — on an income of ₹1.2 lakh, about 23%. That number startles people
who have never run the arithmetic, and it explains a great deal about why months feel tight despite "reasonable"
spending. The expenses were real all along; they were merely unassigned.

## The sizing method, in four steps

**One — list every irregular expense you know of.** Annual and periodic: insurance, fees, subscriptions billed
annually, festivals, travel, maintenance, gifts, license renewals, property tax, the society's painting fund.
**Two — attach a real number to each.** Last year's actuals are the best estimator; where you have none, estimate
high and adjust after year one. **Three — divide by the months remaining** until each expense falls due; the
school-fee line eight months from the quarter is annual ÷ 12, but the insurance renewal three months away needs
the total ÷ 3 this year, ÷ 12 thereafter. **Four — automate the transfer** on salary day into a pool account, with
a simple ledger (a spreadsheet tab is fine) tracking each named line's balance.

The ledger matters. The pool is a single account; the _names_ live in the ledger. Without the ledger, the pool
becomes a general-purpose balance that the month's emergencies quietly annex — and by the time school fees arrive,
the fee money has become a phone.

## Where the pool lives

The sinking-fund pool should be liquid, boring, and slightly inconvenient to raid.

| Parking option                  | Access                   | Return (FY 2025-26, indicative) | Verdict                            |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Savings account (separate bank) | Instant                  | 2.5–3.5%                        | Fine for lines due within 6 months |
| Liquid mutual fund              | T+1 redemption           | 5–6.5% pre-tax                  | Best for lines 6+ months out       |
| Short-duration FD               | Premature penalty 0.5–1% | 6.5–7.5%                        | Lines due at a known date          |
| Sweep-in FD linked to savings   | Instant-ish              | ~FD rate minus nothing          | Good compromise in one bank        |
| Equity anything                 | Volatile                 | High variance                   | Never — horizons too short         |

Split by horizon: money needed within the quarter sits in savings or sweep-in; money for next year's fees sits in
a liquid fund. The interest-rate optimisation is worth pennies compared to the behavioural win of the pool not
being visible in the salary account.

## The annual-cycle version: the calendar of truths

After one full year of running named sinking funds, you own something most households never build: a _calendar of
known expenses_ — the true annual cost of your family's operating model. The second year's version gets sharper
(the estimates meet actuals), and the surplus lines — the ones that over-collected — get swept to investments in
December rather than absorbed into spending. The point is not the ₹28,100; it is that your budget stops
pretending your life has twelve identical months.

## Common objections, answered with numbers

**"I can't spare ₹28,000 a month."** You are already spending it — in the four ugly months, with interest attached.
The sinking fund changes _when_ you pay, not _whether_ you do. **"What if the line never gets spent?"** Then the
balance rolls forward or sweeps to investments — money was saved either way; there is no loss scenario except
under-funding. **"Credit cards can absorb the shocks."** At 36–42% annualised on revolving balances, a ₹40,000
Diwali on a card paid over eight months costs roughly ₹5,000–6,000 in interest — the festival's true price is then
₹46,000, pre-funded or not. **"My expenses are too irregular to predict."** They are irregular in timing, not in
existence; annual insurance does not become optional because it arrives in April.

## The legality note

Sinking funds are plain legal, but two adjacent notes matter. First, if a pool is held in another family member's
name — a parent's account, a spouse's folio — remember that interest earned is _their_ income, taxable in their
hands, and large movements between accounts should be explainable as gifts or loans with a paper trail. The
Income-tax Act taxes income in the hands of the earner, and clubbing provisions apply to spousal transfers
([incometax.gov.in](https://www.incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/)). Second, small informal "chit fund" style
rotating pools among friends and family occupy a legal grey zone unless registered; the risks — default, no
documentation, no regulator — are absorbed entirely by participants, and disputes have no cheap remedy. A named
sinking fund in your own account achieves the same discipline with none of the ambiguity.
