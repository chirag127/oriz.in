The money that survives the month is not the money left over — it is the money removed on day one. This is the
least negotiable idea in personal finance: salary arrives, and within the hour, a planned split sends portions to
places the debit card cannot casually reach. Everyone knows the theory. Almost nobody has a _sequence_, which is
why the month begins at ₹60,000 and ends at ₹800 and the SIP bounce notification arrives with the usual shame. This
article is the sequence.

## The T+0 sweep, defined

T+0 means the transfer happens the same day the salary credit posts — before rent apps, before Swiggy, before
anything. The split is decided by your budget (the zero-based article in this track), but the _execution_ is
mechanical: standing instructions and auto-debits timed to the salary date, not to calendar dates you keep
forgetting.

| Order | Move                    | Where                          | Why this order                       |
| ----- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| 0     | Salary lands            | Salary account                 | The holding pen, nothing more        |
| 1     | Insurance premiums ÷ 12 | Sinking-fund account           | Contractual, non-negotiable, small   |
| 2     | Emergency-fund top-up   | Liquid fund / separate savings | Until the 6-month target is met      |
| 3     | SIP auto-debits         | Mutual fund folios             | Scheduled 2–3 days after salary date |
| 4     | EMI auto-debits         | Loan accounts                  | Never bounced, never late-fee'd      |
| 5     | Rent and shared bills   | Landlord / roommates           | Due early, pay first                 |
| 6     | Sinking-fund deposits   | Separate account               | Fees, insurance, festivals, repairs  |
| 7     | What remains            | Spends from salary account     | Guilt-free — this is the point       |

The order is not aesthetic. Emergency fund before discretionary spend because the fund is the thing that lets you
be brave everywhere else. SIP before EMI in the _scheduling_ sense only because EMIs are auto-debited by the bank
on their own date regardless; the SIP instruction must be set _after_ the salary credit date but _before_ the
spending season begins — typically salary +2 days.

## The account structure that does the enforcement

Three accounts, each with one job. The structure matters more than the banks.

| Account              | Holds                                                   | Card/UPI enabled?                  |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Salary account       | Month's variable spend — groceries, fuel, food delivery | Yes, this is the daily wallet      |
| Sinking-fund account | Annual and irregular sums accumulating                  | No card; transfer out deliberately |
| Long-horizon account | Investments, emergency-fund layers                      | No card, no UPI, no ATM            |

The single behavioural win: the daily-wallet account shows a number that is _already your spendable money_. What
you see is what is safe to spend. Every salary-day transfer is invisible by design — the eye cannot mourn money
it never saw settle.

## What the first ₹10,000 should fund

Take a ₹60,000 take-home and a first-₹10,000 slice on salary day. The split is a statement of priorities, in
order:

| Slice | Amount | Destination                                             |
| ----- | ------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | ₹1,500 | Health + term premium pool (annual ÷ 12)                |
| 2     | ₹3,000 | Emergency-fund top-up (liquid fund)                     |
| 3     | ₹3,000 | SIP installments (index fund, direct plan)              |
| 4     | ₹1,500 | Sinking funds — school fees, insurance, travel, repairs |
| 5     | ₹1,000 | PPF or NPS contribution                                 |

Note what is absent: nothing discretionary. The first ₹10,000 out the door is the month's _infrastructure_ — the
moves that make every other decision cheaper. Only after this sweep does the remaining ₹50,000 face groceries,
rent, and the weekend. A ₹10,000 first-hour discipline on a ₹60,000 salary is a 20% structural savings rate
counting only these five lines; add the EMI (which builds an asset) and the real committed rate is higher still.

## When the salary is variable

Freelancers, consultants, commission earners: the sweep still applies, but the _anchor_ changes. Budget the sweep
off a **baseline income** — the lowest monthly income of the last 12 months, not the average. Months above baseline
fill the sinking-fund account first, then investments, in that order. The rule that changes everything: **the
sinking-fund account is the shock absorber, not the lifestyle**. A ₹2 lakh month after four ₹80,000 months does
not raise your standard of living; it fills the buffer that makes the next ₹60,000 month survivable.

| Income scenario                | Sweep %             | Notes                                       |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Fixed salary                   | 25–35% of take-home | Automate on salary date +2                  |
| Variable, 12-month floor known | 35% of the floor    | Top up from surplus months                  |
| Fully variable / freelance     | 40% of every credit | First ₹10,000 of _every_ credit, not salary |
| Bonus or one-time income       | 50% minimum         | Before it touches the spend account         |

## Common failure modes, and the fix

**The salary date drift.** Companies pay on the 1st, the 3rd, "by the 5th". Set auto-debits against the _earliest
credible_ date and keep one month's expenses as a checking buffer in the salary account once the emergency fund
is done. **The "I'll transfer manually" plan.** Manual transfers lose to manual months. Standing instructions beat
intention every time they are tried head-to-head. **The shared-salary couple split.** Each partner sweeps their
own income into the joint structure on their own salary day; the sweep is per-person, not per-household, and the
personal no-questions allowance survives every joint decision.

**The mid-month raid.** The sinking-fund account must be _annoying_ to access — no card, a separate bank, ideally a
bank requiring a small transfer wait. Friction is a feature here, the same way convenience was a feature when
moving money _into_ it.

## The legality note

Nothing in salary-day routing is grey, but two compliance points belong here. First, if part of your income is
cash — tips, freelance, family business — the sweep must route it through a bank account anyway; cash deposits
above ₹10 lakh a year in savings accounts are reported by banks to the tax department under
[reporting requirements](https://www.incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/), and unexplained credits are exactly what
notices are made of. Declare the income, pay the tax, then sweep it. Second, salary accounts sometimes carry
"reimbursement" components — fuel, telephone, internet. Route reimbursements against actual bills, not as
tax-free-sounding extras; a reimbursement claimed without a bill is taxable perquisite income, and the employer's
TDS return will eventually disagree with your assumption. Pay the tax that is due; the sweep works just as well
on post-tax money.

## The compounding nobody posts screenshots of

Run the first-₹10,000 rule for ten years at a 12% equity return — ₹10,000 monthly for 120 months is about
₹23 lakh, of which ₹12 lakh is principal. Run it for twenty and the corpus crosses ₹99 lakh on ₹24 lakh invested.
Neither number is extraordinary. What is extraordinary is that the alternative — spending first and saving later —
has a documented average: whatever the month's enthusiasm permits, which is to say, usually nothing. The sweep is
not a sacrifice. It is the difference between a plan that executes itself and an intention that requires you to
be a better person every month for thirty years.
