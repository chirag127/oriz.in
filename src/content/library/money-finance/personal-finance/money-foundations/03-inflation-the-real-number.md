Inflation is the only force that acts on every rupee you own, every year, with no exceptions and no lock-in
period. It is also the number most people quote without ever having looked up. India's headline CPI inflation has
run between roughly 4% and 6.5% through most of the last decade — the RBI's own target band is 2–6% with a 4%
midpoint. But the printed number is an average across a consumption basket you do not have. Your personal
inflation rate is the one that decides whether your money plan is working, and it is almost always different.

## How the official number is built

The Consumer Price Index (Combined), published by the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation
([mospi.gov.in](https://www.mospi.gov.in/)), weights a fixed basket of goods and services consumed by an average
household. The weights matter more than the headline:

| Category                    | CPI weight (approx.) | 10-year typical trend    |
| --------------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Food and beverages          | ~45%                 | Volatile: 2–11% swings   |
| Housing                     | ~10%                 | 3–5%, sticky             |
| Transport and communication | ~8%                  | Fuel-linked, spiky       |
| Health                      | ~5%                  | 8–12%, consistently high |
| Education                   | ~4%                  | 8–10%, consistently high |
| Clothing, fuel, misc        | ~28%                 | Mixed                    |

Read the table again with your own month in mind. A household with school-age children and elderly parents has
health and education running at 8–12% while the food lines may be flat. A retiree with no food inflation exposure
but heavy medical spend faces a rate well above the printed 5%. The average is accurate for the average household
and wrong for yours, in whichever direction your basket differs.

## Your personal inflation rate

The calculation is one line: list what you actually spend monthly today, and what the same list cost five years
ago, and annualise the ratio. Or shortcut it with the categories that dominate your budget:

| Expense type                        | Typical inflation   | Your exposure             |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Rent (metro, renewals)              | 5–10% per year      | High if renting           |
| School and coaching fees            | 8–10%               | High with children        |
| Private hospitalisation (uninsured) | 10–12%              | Catastrophic if uninsured |
| Groceries (staples)                 | 3–6%, volatile      | Medium                    |
| Eating out, services                | 6–8%                | Medium                    |
| Electronics, gadgets                | 0–3% (deflationary) | Small share of budget     |

Two structural observations follow. One: the things that inflate fastest — health, education, housing — are the
things a middle-class Indian budget spends most on. Two: the things that deflate or stay flat — electronics — are
the things budgets spend least on. The basket-weighted CPI will tell you 5%; the household running the top four
rows of that table lives closer to 7–8%.

## What inflation does to fixed sums

The compounding works quietly and without appeals. The same ₹1 lakh, at various inflation rates:

| Years | At 4%   | At 6%   | At 8%   |
| ----- | ------- | ------- | ------- |
| 10    | ₹67,600 | ₹55,800 | ₹46,300 |
| 20    | ₹45,600 | ₹31,200 | ₹21,500 |
| 30    | ₹30,800 | ₹17,400 | ₹9,900  |

The third row is the retirement mathematics nobody enjoys: ₹1 crore at retirement, spent evenly over a 30-year
retirement at 6% inflation, buys roughly what ₹17 lakh buys today — unless the corpus keeps growing while it is
being spent. That is the whole argument for equity in long-horizon money, and it does not require a bull-market
opinion, only an inflation assumption.

## The hurdle rate: what your money must beat

Real return is nominal return minus inflation, and the arithmetic is not merely subtraction — the correct form is
(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1 — but subtraction is close enough at these magnitudes and kinder to mental
math. The hurdle rates as of FY 2025-26:

| Instrument                         | Typical nominal return | Real return at 6% inflation             |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Savings account                    | 2.5–3.5%               | −2.5 to −3.5%                           |
| Liquid fund                        | 5–6.5% pre-tax         | ~0% post-tax at 30% slab                |
| Fixed deposit (1–3 yr, major bank) | 6.5–7.5%               | 0.5–1.5% pre-tax                        |
| PPF (FY 2025-26)                   | 7.1%                   | ~1% pre-tax                             |
| EPF (FY 2025-26)                   | 8.25%                  | ~2% pre-tax, and tax-free within limits |
| Nifty 50 TRI, long-run average     | 11–13%                 | 5–7%                                    |

The table's uncomfortable column is the negative one: money parked in a savings account at 3% while inflation runs
6% is a guaranteed, permanent, compounding loss of purchasing power. It is not "safe". It is a slow, certain loss
with no paperwork. Safety of principal is not safety of value, and confusing the two is the most expensive
category error in Indian household finance.

## Where inflation planning goes wrong

**Insuring against the wrong inflation.** Term life cover set at "10× salary" from a 2015 salary ignores a decade
of income growth; the sum assured must be sized against future family expenses, which inflate. **Gold as the
hedge.** Gold's long-run return roughly tracks or slightly beats CPI, with decade-long flat stretches — it is a
store of value with high variance, not an inflation-busting engine. **The FD retirement.** A retiree drawing
₹50,000/month from an FD corpus at 7% while inflation runs 6% holds nominal principal constant and runs a real
drawdown; in twenty years the corpus buys barely half of what it did. **Salary negotiation against the wrong
base.** A 5% appraisal in a 6% CPI year is a pay cut, politely framed.

## What to actually do with the number

Three moves, none of them exotic. **One:** compute your own basket's inflation once — one evening, last year's
expenses versus this year's — and use _that_ number in every retirement and education calculation instead of the
CPI headline. **Two:** match instruments to horizons — money needed within three years does not belong in equity
regardless of the inflation argument, because sequence risk eats small horizons; money needed in fifteen years
does not belong in savings accounts regardless of the safety argument. **Three:** re-check the real return on the
fixed-income sleeve annually. The RBI's rate cycles move FD and small-savings rates ([rbi.org.in](https://www.rbi.org.in/)
publishes the reference rates and the monetary policy reports); a portfolio built in a 9% FD world does not
automatically reprice itself in a 7% one.

The realistic summary: the number that matters is not the one printed in the news, it is the one your own receipt
drawer produces — and the return your portfolio must clear is that number plus the tax on the return, plus a
margin. Everything else in personal finance is easier once this hurdle is written on a sticky note and believed.
