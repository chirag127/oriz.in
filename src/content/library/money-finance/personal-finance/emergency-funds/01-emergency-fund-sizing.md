The emergency fund is the least glamorous instrument in personal finance and the only one that will certainly be
used. Everything else — equity, PPF, crypto — is a bet on the future. The emergency fund is a bet on your own life
being messy, and it pays out on schedule. This article fixes three things most guides get wrong: the size, the
parking place, and the rules of use.

## Why six months of _expenses_, not salary

The common advice is "save six months of salary." That is wrong in both directions. Salary includes the savings and
investments you would stop during an emergency anyway; billing six months of salary makes the fund look
unreachable. Six months of _essential expenses_ is the correct unit — rent, EMIs, utilities, groceries, school fees,
insurance premiums, the commute you would still pay for.

| Household situation                                 | Essential monthly expenses | Fund target (months) | Fund size     |
| --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | -------------------- | ------------- |
| Salaried, stable PSU/government job                 | ₹40,000                    | 4                    | ₹1.6 lakh     |
| Salaried, private job, single income                | ₹60,000                    | 6                    | ₹3.6 lakh     |
| Salaried, private job, single income, home loan EMI | ₹80,000                    | 7–8                  | ₹5.6–6.4 lakh |
| Freelancer / consultant / sales on variable pay     | ₹60,000                    | 9–12                 | ₹5.4–7.2 lakh |
| Business owner with fixed overheads                 | ₹1,00,000                  | 12                   | ₹12 lakh      |

Two adjustments cut the number _down_ honestly. First, anything already insured — a health policy with a ₹5 lakh
cover, term life for the earning years — means the fund must absorb the deductible and the waiting months, not the
full hospital bill. Second, money that is accessible but not liquid (a PPF you are 4 years from maturity, a parent's
FD they have mentally earmarked for you) is a second layer, not the first. The first layer must be reachable in
**72 hours without begging anyone**.

## The three-layer parking structure

Do not keep the whole fund in one instrument. Keep it in three layers with increasing yield and decreasing
instant-access.

**Layer 1 — the float (1 month of expenses).** Savings account, ideally the same bank the EMIs auto-debit from.
It exists so that a medical bill on the 28th does not bounce a SIP debit on the 2nd. Yield is irrelevant; the
savings account is a parking bay, not an investment.

**Layer 2 — the buffer (2–4 months).** A liquid or overnight mutual fund with **instant redemption facility**
(SEBI allows ₹50,000 or 90% of the folio per day, whichever is lower, credited same-day). This is the workhorse:
2–4% post-tax-equivalent yield with same-day access beats a savings account's 2.7–3.5% by a wide margin.

**Layer 3 — the deep reserve (the remaining months).** An FD ladder: three to four FDs of equal size split across
maturities (say 6, 9, 12, 18 months), each breakable. A single 5-year tax-saver FD is _not_ an emergency fund
component — it is locked for the full term. Banks charge 0.5–1% penalty for premature withdrawal of regular FDs;
that cost is the price of insurance-like liquidity, and it is worth it.

```text
Fund of ₹3.6 lakh (6 × ₹60,000), single private-sector earner:
  Layer 1  ₹60,000   savings account (same bank as EMIs)
  Layer 2  ₹1,60,000  liquid fund, instant redemption enabled
  Layer 3  ₹1,40,000  FD ladder: ₹50k @ 6m · ₹45k @ 9m · ₹45k @ 12m
```

## The two places not to park it

**Equity, in any form.** Emergency funds get demanded at exactly the moments equity falls — layoffs coincide with
market crashes. Pulling ₹3 lakh from a 20%-down portfolio converts a temporary drawdown into a permanent loss. The
fund's job is to _prevent_ that sale.

**Long-locked products.** PPF (15 years, loans only from year 3), NPS (locked to 60), ELSS (3-year lock per
installment), small-finance bank tax-saver FDs. These are investment decisions, not liquidity. Mixing them is how
people end up with ₹8 lakh of "savings" they cannot touch during an actual emergency.

## Building it without feeling it

Automate a standing instruction the day salary lands: 15–20% of take-home into the liquid fund until the target is
hit. At ₹60,000 of essential expenses and a ₹3.6 lakh target, ₹18,000 a month finishes the job in 20 months. Two
accelerators halve that: the annual bonus (route 50% of it), and tax refunds. Do not route windfalls into equity
_before_ the fund is complete — a complete fund is what lets you stay invested through the next crash.

## The spending and refilling rules

An emergency fund has a definition, and "new phone because the old one is slow" is not it. A genuine emergency has
three properties: **unexpected** (not annual insurance renewal), **necessary** (not a bargain that expires), and
**urgent** (not a holiday that could be budgeted for). Job loss, medical deductible, urgent home or vehicle repair
that affects safety or income, family support in a crisis — those qualify.

When money leaves, the fund goes into **replenishment mode**: contributions restart at the higher of the regular
amount or 25% of take-home until the target is restored, and no new discretionary investment commitments until it
is. The fund is not "set and forget"; it is a standing army that must be re-manned after every war. Review the size
whenever expenses change materially — a new EMI, a child, a move to a costlier city all raise the target.

## The legality note

Nothing here is grey. Park the fund anywhere legal and disclosed — savings account, liquid fund, FD. Two hygiene
points that keep it that way: keep the liquid fund in your own PAN-registered folio (not a relative's), and if you
park large cash in savings accounts, remember that interest above ₹10,000 a year is fully taxable at slab (the old
80TTA deduction is ₹10,000; the new regime's 80TTB applies only to senior citizens, up to ₹50,000). TDS on FD
interest kicks in when annual interest crosses ₹40,000 (₹1,00,000 for senior citizens) — banks deduct at 10% with
PAN on file, 20% without. Fill Form 15G/15H only if your total tax is genuinely nil — the forms and the
eligibility conditions are on [incometax.gov.in](https://www.incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal); filing it falsely
is a misdeclaration, and the tax is recoverable with interest.

## What this buys you

A complete fund converts every other financial decision. You can take equity risk without panic-selling, because
the next layoff will not force a sale. You can buy insurance with a real deductible instead of paying premiums to
shrink it. You can negotiate — with an employer, a hospital, a car dealer — from a position that does not need the
transaction. The returns on the fund itself are modest by design. The returns on _having_ it show up everywhere
else.

A worked household example:

| Essential monthly outflow | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Rent and maintenance | ₹22,000 |
| Groceries and household supplies | ₹12,000 |
| Utilities, internet, and mobile | ₹3,500 |
| Commute and essential fuel | ₹4,500 |
| School fees and transport | ₹8,000 |
| Home and vehicle EMIs | ₹14,000 |
| Insurance premiums, monthly provision | ₹3,000 |
| Medicines and regular care | ₹2,000 |
| **Total** | **₹69,000** |

Six months of cover is ₹4,14,000. Add a ₹50,000 buffer for deductibles and repairs, and the target becomes
₹4,64,000. If the household already holds ₹1,60,000, the gap is ₹3,04,000; at ₹25,000 a month, it closes in
about 13 months. Change the assumptions and the answer changes, which is the point of using a calculator rather
than a slogan.

## Where to keep the reserve in India

| Option | Access | Risk | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cash at home | Immediate | Theft, loss | Keep only a few days of expenses |
| Savings account | Same day | Bank risk, inflation | Convenient; interest is taxable at slab |
| Sweep-in FD | Same or next day | Bank risk | Check the minimum-balance and sweep terms |
| Short-term FD | At maturity or with penalty | Bank risk | Match maturity to access needs |
| Liquid or overnight fund | Typically next business day | Market-linked, not guaranteed | Check exit load and settlement |
| Long-term or tax-saver FD | Locked | Bank risk, opportunity cost | Not emergency money |
| Equity, crypto, property | Variable | High | Not suitable for this layer |

Deposits in scheduled commercial banks are insured by the DICGC up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank, subject to
conditions. Spread very large balances across banks rather than treating every instrument as insured. A practical
split is a small cash layer, one to three months in a savings or sweep account, and the remainder in a short-term
deposit or liquid fund whose settlement timeline you have actually tested.

## A short spending rule

Use the fund when the expense is unexpected, necessary, and urgent, and when the realistic alternative is
expensive debt or missed essential care. A planned vacation, a sale purchase, or a wedding belongs in a sinking
fund, not here. After a withdrawal, rebuild the first month of cover before increasing discretionary investments;
a fund that is never replenished is a one-time emergency loan to yourself.
