Every January, the same question arrives with the tax-proof deadline: should the 80C money go into PPF or an ELSS
fund? The honest answer is that they are not really competitors — one is a government-guaranteed debt instrument
with a 15-year clock, the other is an equity fund with a three-year lock-in — and the right answer depends on which
of those two gaps in your portfolio actually needs filling. Here is the arithmetic, without the usual sermon.

## The instruments, side by side

| Feature               | PPF                                                | ELSS                                                 |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| What it is            | Small-savings debt scheme, sovereign backing       | Diversified equity mutual fund, Section 80C-eligible |
| Tenure                | 15 years, extendable in 5-year blocks indefinitely | Open-ended; lock-in only 3 years per instalment      |
| Current rate / return | 7.1% (unchanged since Q1 2020-21)                  | Market-linked; long-run Nifty TRI ~11-12%            |
| Annual limit          | ₹1.5 lakh (₹500 minimum)                           | Shares the ₹1.5 lakh 80C ceiling                     |
| Tax on deposit        | Deduction under 80C, old regime                    | Deduction under 80C, old regime                      |
| Tax on gains          | Fully exempt; proceeds tax-free                    | Equity LTCG above ₹1.25 lakh at 12.5%; STCG 20%      |
| Liquidity             | Partial withdrawal from year 7; loans from year 3  | Full redemption after 3 years per unit               |
| Risk                  | Zero nominal; reinvestment risk on rate resets     | Full equity volatility                               |

The rate column hides the most important fact about PPF: 7.1 per cent has held since April 2020 because small-
savings rates were frozen by a 2020 decision to keep them stable through the rate cycle. Every other year, the
Shyamala Gopinath committee formula would have moved them with G-sec yields. So PPF today is a sovereign instrument
paying 7.1 per cent tax-free while a comparable 10-year G-sec yields over 6.5 per cent taxable — the tax exemption
is doing real work. But treat 7.1 as the _floor_ you are accepting for 15 years, not as a guaranteed permanent
feature; the government resets rates quarterly-eligible and has both raised and cut small-savings rates in living
memory.

## The tax maths, slab by slab

Both instruments give the same 80C deduction, so the entry is identical. The exit is where they diverge:

| Investor                                  | PPF: tax on ₹1.5 lakh/year × 15 yrs @7.1% | ELSS: tax on same at 12% CAGR                                  |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 30% slab, old regime, ₹22.5 lakh invested | ₹41.9 lakh maturity, ₹0 tax               | ₹47.0 lakh value; LTCG on gains above ₹1.25 lakh/year at 12.5% |
| 20% slab, old regime                      | Same ₹41.9 lakh, ₹0 tax                   | Same growth; gains taxed at 12.5% on redemption                |
| New regime (no 80C)                       | No deduction, but corpus still tax-free   | No deduction; LTCG rules apply                                 |

Let us do the arithmetic properly. ₹12,500 a month (₹1.5 lakh a year) into PPF at 7.1 per cent for 15 years
compounds to roughly **₹40.7 lakh** — the standard PPF multiplication, since contributions are assumed in-year.
The same ₹1.5 lakh a year into ELSS at an 11 per cent net CAGR gives about **₹52 lakh**; at 8 per cent, about
₹41.6 lakh. So the honest comparison is: PPF guarantees ₹40.7 lakh; ELSS delivers between ₹41.6 and ₹52 lakh
depending on how equities behave over your specific 15 years, and the LTCG tax shaves something off the top — at
12.5 per cent on gains above the annual ₹1.25 lakh exemption, a ₹52 lakh redemption with ₹37 lakh of gains costs
roughly ₹4.4 lakh in tax if taken in one year, less if you redeem across financial years to use the exemption each
time.

At which point the question becomes: is a 25-65 per cent upside worth accepting equity risk on money you have
labelled "retirement"? For most people with 15 years to deploy, yes. For someone whose retirement portfolio is
already equity-heavy, PPF is the better risk-adjusted addition because it is the _fixed-income_ leg.

## The 15-year licence: what the rules actually allow

PPF is not a sealed box for 15 years. The rules under the Public Provident Fund Scheme, 1968:

| Year                            | What you can do                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Year 3-6                        | Loan up to 25% of the closing balance of the preceding year; interest 1% above PPF rate (2% if repaid late) |
| Year 7+                         | Partial withdrawal up to 50% of the balance at the end of year 4, or the preceding year, whichever is lower |
| After year 15                   | Extend in 5-year blocks, with or without fresh contributions                                                |
| Extension with contributions    | Continue as if a fresh 5-year block; withdrawal up to 60% of the opening block balance                      |
| Extension without contributions | Withdraw any amount, once a financial year; balance keeps earning                                           |

The detail almost everyone misses: an extension _without_ contribution keeps the whole balance earning 7.1 per cent
with annual withdrawal rights — which makes mature PPF an accidental post-retirement income instrument, tax-free,
sovereign-backed, with no lock-in and no annuity. The extension rules also mean the "15-year licence" is really a
licence to run a tax-free debt portfolio for as long as you like, in 5-year renewal windows, with the current
balance's rate applying. Every PPF account holder should know this before letting a bank clerk close the account at
maturity by default.

## Who should pick which

The decision tree is short:

1. **You are on the new tax regime.** The 80C deduction is gone for you, so the "tax-saving" label is moot; both
   instruments are just investments now. Choose by portfolio gap — if your fixed income is thin, PPF's 7.1 per cent
   tax-free is hard to beat; if your equity allocation is low for your horizon, ELSS is the better default.
2. **You are on the old regime and the ₹1.5 lakh is genuinely tax-driven.** Split it: ELSS for the years you are
   more than five from a goal, PPF for the retirement leg. A 60/40 or 70/30 ELSS/PPF split inside 80C is a
   reasonable standing instruction.
3. **You are risk-averse and this money is your retirement core.** PPF, extended in 5-year blocks, is a legitimate
   debt allocation for the whole of retirement — sovereign, tax-free, and operationally simple.
4. **You are already maxing EPF and have a healthy emergency fund.** The marginal 80C rupee has less work to do in
   PPF (you may already be overexposed to EPF's identical risk profile); the case for ELSS as the equity portion
   strengthens.

Two operational notes. First, PPF contributions have a deadline geometry: deposit before the 5th of the month to
earn interest for that month, and a lump sum deposited in April earns for the full year — the cheapest return
optimisation available. Second, ELSS SIPs run through a tax-planning quarter still create a three-year lock-in per
instalment; if you stop the SIP after January, you still carry staggered redemption dates into three years of
future liquidity planning. Check the fund's lock-in schedule before assuming you can exit when you want.

## Where people go wrong with both

The recurring PPF error is treating it as a deposit account: irregular contributions, missing the April lump-sum
window, closing it at 15 years because the bank told them to, or taking year-7 withdrawals for consumption instead
of letting the balance compound. The recurring ELSS error is choosing the fund by its 80C label rather than its
portfolio — many 80C-labelled ELSS funds have held positions indistinguishable from their large-cap benchmarks,
and the tax advantage over a plain index fund is the same 12.5 per cent LTCG treatment any equity fund gets; ELSS
is not tax-privileged _as an investment_, only as an 80C entry point.

still holds in any given quarter.
