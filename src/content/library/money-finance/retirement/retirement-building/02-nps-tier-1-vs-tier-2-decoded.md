The National Pension System has two accounts, and most confusion about it comes from treating them as versions of
the same product. They are not. Tier 1 is a retirement account with a lock-in, a withdrawal regime and a tax
incentive built around it. Tier 2 is an investment account that happens to sit on NPS rails — closer to an open-
ended mutual fund than to a pension. Understanding which one a given rupee belongs in matters more than the fund
manager you pick.

## The structural differences, in one table

| Feature                  | Tier 1                                                 | Tier 2                               |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| Purpose                  | Retirement; mandatory annuitisation                    | Flexible savings                     |
| Lock-in                  | Until 60 (with limited exits)                          | None                                 |
| Minimum contribution     | ₹500 per transaction, ₹1,000/year                      | ₹250 per transaction (₹2,000/year)   |
| Withdrawals              | Partial: 25% of own contribution, 3 times, rules apply | Anytime, no limit                    |
| Equity exposure (Active) | Max 75%                                                | Max 75% for Scheme E (from 2023)     |
| 80CCD(1) deduction       | Up to ₹1.5 lakh within the 80C ceiling                 | None                                 |
| 80CCD(1B) extra ₹50,000  | Yes                                                    | No                                   |
| Employer 80CCD(2)        | Yes                                                    | No                                   |
| Tax on withdrawal        | 60% lump sum exempt; annuity income taxable at slab    | LTCG/STCG as per capital gains rules |

That last row is the quiet divide. Tier 1's exit at 60 pays you 60 per cent as a tax-free lump sum and forces the
remaining 40 per cent into an annuity whose income is taxed as ordinary salary. Tier 2 is simply a portfolio:
equity gains held over a year are long-term and taxed under the applicable capital-gains rates, debt gains at your
slab if held short. Nothing is forced, nothing is exempt beyond the ordinary rules.

## What Tier 1 actually locks up

The lock-in is softer than the label suggests, but it is real. Under PFRDA rules, before age 60 you may withdraw
up to **25 per cent of your own contributions** (not the employer's, not the gains), no more than three times over
the whole subscription, only for specified purposes — higher education of children, specified illnesses, a house,
marriage expenses. The earliest any such withdrawal can happen is three years after account opening. Full exit
before 60 is allowed only for a corpus under ₹2.5 lakh (where you can take it all as lump sum), or on
superannuation, death, or specific categories including terminal illness and permanent disability.

The 60-plus exit is the one to plan around: 60 per cent lump sum, 40 per cent annuity mandatory, with a de-minimis
exception if the entire corpus is ₹12 lakh or less (₹5 lakh for APY subscribers), where full lump sum is permitted.
Post-2022 changes allow phased withdrawals from the lump-sum portion up to age 75 and annual annuity re-shopping
after age 65, which softens the old complaint that you were locked into one insurer's quote for life.

## The tax benefit, with the return on it

The ₹50,000 extra deduction under Section 80CCD(1B) is the headline reason salaried people open Tier 1. The honest
accounting:

| Tax regime                        | Deduction level                     | Tax saved at 30% (₹50,000 extra) | Effective first-year gain              |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Old regime                        | 80C ₹1.5 lakh + 80CCD(1B) ₹50,000   | ₹15,000                          | ~30% on the day one ₹                  |
| New regime (FY 2025-26, 12% slab) | 80CCD(2) employer contribution only | Slab rate on employer part       | Employer contribution is pre-tax money |

Under the new regime, the individual 80CCD(1B) deduction is not available; what survives is 80CCD(2), the employer
contribution, deductible up to 14 per cent of salary. If your employer contributes to NPS, that is free structure
and should be used before anything else. If not, and you are on the new regime, the ₹50,000 tax incentive that
justifies Tier 1 in most articles simply does not apply to you — what remains is the investment case, which is
stronger or weaker depending on what you would otherwise hold.

## Costs and fund choice

NPS is cheap by any standard. The PFRDA-prescribed charges: 0.30 per cent of assets for scheme management under
Tier 1 (0.50 per cent under the newer models where allowed), plus fund manager compensation at 0.05 per cent of
contributions, a ₹60 annual service charge, and a ₹380 annual transaction charge under the Tier 1 model. Tier 2
adds ₹50 per contribution transaction. Equity in Scheme E tracks the Nifty 50 and Nifty 100 TRI benchmarks; the
system now offers several fund managers with a 25 per cent cap on the money each holds, and you may switch managers
once a financial year at a nominal fee.

The comparison that matters is against what you would otherwise own:

| Instrument            | Cost band          | Equity cap | Liquidity                                   | Tax                 |
| --------------------- | ------------------ | ---------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| NPS Tier 1 (E+G)      | ~0.10-0.35% all-in | 75%        | Locked to 60, 25% own-contribution advances | 60% lump sum exempt |
| NPS Tier 2 Scheme E   | ~0.30-0.50%        | 75%        | Full                                        | Capital gains rules |
| Index fund            | 0.10-0.20%         | 100%       | Full                                        | Capital gains rules |
| Active flexi-cap fund | 0.60-1.20%         | 100%       | Full                                        | Capital gains rules |

If you are a do-it-yourself investor who already holds index funds, Tier 1 is a supplement — its auto-rebalancing
and the G-scheme's fixed-income mandate do work you would otherwise do by hand. If you are someone who would
otherwise own an expensive active fund, Tier 1 is an upgrade in cost discipline.

## Where Tier 2 earns its keep — and where it does not

Tier 2's honest use cases are narrow. It works as a disciplined parking lot when you want NPS-style fund
management without lock-in — for example, a Scheme C (debt) bucket for a known expense five years out. It works
for people who want to mirror their Tier 1 asset allocation with liquidity for the last five years before 60,
building the lump-sum portion they will draw at exit. And it is the only way to keep money on NPS rails once the
Tier 1 contribution target is met and you have spare cash you do not want in equity.

Where it does not earn its keep: as a general mutual fund substitute. The Scheme E universe tracks indices you can
own through an index fund at similar or lower cost with fewer transaction charges; the Scheme C universe is a
government-bond and gilt tilt that competes with target-maturity funds. And there is a quiet trap: money in Tier 2
earns nothing tax-favoured, so using it as a "retirement account" without Tier 1 means you have taken on NPS's
admin (PIN, KYC, annuity at 60 applies only if Tier 1, but the account is still on NPS rails) for none of its
benefits.

## The practical setup sequence

One PRAN, two accounts: open Tier 1 through the CRA portals at [npscra.nsdl.co.in](https://npscra.nsdl.co.in/) or
KFin, e-KYC with Aadhaar and PAN, a nominal ₹500 first contribution, then activate Tier 2 from the same login if
you need it. Choose Active or Auto lifecycle at Tier 1 opening; Active caps equity at 75 per cent, Auto runs the
age-based glide path (LC-25, LC-50, LC-75 tiers) and can be switched once a year. Tier 2 fund choice is separate
and can be set differently. Both accounts draw from the same NPS Trust infrastructure; switching schemes, managers
or allocations costs a small fee and is done on the CRA portal, not by paperwork.
