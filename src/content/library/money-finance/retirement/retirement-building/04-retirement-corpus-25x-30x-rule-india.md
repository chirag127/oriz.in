"Save 25 times your annual expenses" is the most portable rule in personal finance: it crossed from the American
Trinity study into Indian Instagram finance without losing its shape, and without anyone checking what it means
when the currency is the rupee, the inflation is 6 per cent and the retirees are 45. The rule is not wrong — it is
just doing a different job here than it did in Texas. This article works out what a 25x or 30x corpus actually
buys in India, and where the multiple needs adjusting.

## Where the multiple comes from

The 25x rule is the inverse of the 4 per cent safe withdrawal rate: if you can sustainably withdraw 4 per cent of
a corpus in year one and then inflation-adjust it annually, you need 25 times your first-year withdrawal as the
starting corpus. The 4 per cent figure comes from the 1994 Bengen study and the 1998 Trinity study on US equity and
bond data — 30-year retirements, US market history, US inflation. The 30x rule (a 3.33 per cent withdrawal rate) is
what you get when you either extend the horizon to 40-plus years or take a more cautious view of sequence risk.

Neither study was calibrated for India. What we have for Indian markets is shorter history and different behaviour:
equity returns that have been strong in nominal terms (the Nifty TRI has compounded at roughly 12-14 per cent over
the past two decades) with inflation that eats 6 per cent of it, leaving a real return of 5-7 per cent rather than
the US-style 6-7 per cent real. The arithmetic consequence: an Indian 25x is safer than an American 25x at the
same horizon, but an Indian 40-year retirement needs more than an American 30-year one.

## What 25x and 30x mean in actual rupees

| Current monthly expenses | Annual expenses | 25x corpus  | 30x corpus |
| ------------------------ | --------------- | ----------- | ---------- |
| ₹50,000                  | ₹6 lakh         | ₹1.5 crore  | ₹1.8 crore |
| ₹75,000                  | ₹9 lakh         | ₹2.25 crore | ₹2.7 crore |
| ₹1,00,000                | ₹12 lakh        | ₹3 crore    | ₹3.6 crore |
| ₹1,50,000                | ₹18 lakh        | ₹4.5 crore  | ₹5.4 crore |

Now the inflation correction, which is where Indian versions of the rule usually fail. If you retire at 45 and
need ₹1 lakh a month in today's money at 60, at 6 per cent inflation that figure has already multiplied:

| Years to retirement | Multiplier at 6% inflation | ₹1 lakh/month becomes | 30x corpus on that |
| ------------------- | -------------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------ |
| 10                  | 1.79x                      | ₹1.79 lakh            | ₹6.4 crore         |
| 15                  | 2.40x                      | ₹2.40 lakh            | ₹8.6 crore         |
| 20                  | 3.21x                      | ₹3.21 lakh            | ₹11.6 crore        |
| 25                  | 4.29x                      | ₹4.29 lakh            | ₹15.4 crore        |

This is the table that kills most casual retirement plans. A person earning ₹2 lakh a month today, retiring in 20
years, is not aiming for a ₹3.6 crore corpus; they are aiming for one that funds ₹3.2 lakh a month from day one,
which at 30x is eleven-plus crore. Whether that number is achievable is a different question from whether it is
true — the honest answer is that the corpus requirement compounds as fast as the expenses do, and there is no
version of the rule that makes a late start cheap.

## The withdrawal maths, year by year

Take a ₹5 crore corpus at 60, expenses of ₹12 lakh a year (a 2.4 per cent withdrawal rate to start), inflation at
6 per cent, and a portfolio of 50 per cent equity and 50 per cent fixed income earning a blended 10 per cent
nominal. The sequence:

| Year | Withdrawal (inflation-adjusted) | Corpus before growth | Corpus after 10% growth |
| ---- | ------------------------------- | -------------------- | ----------------------- |
| 1    | ₹12.0 lakh                      | ₹4.88 crore          | ₹5.37 crore             |
| 5    | ₹15.15 lakh                     | ₹5.28 crore          | ₹5.81 crore             |
| 10   | ₹21.5 lakh                      | ₹5.62 crore          | ₹6.18 crore             |
| 20   | ₹38.6 lakh                      | ₹5.06 crore          | ₹5.57 crore             |
| 30   | ₹69.0 lakh                      | ₹3.90 crore          | ₹4.29 crore             |

The corpus grows in early decades and only begins to deplete around year 25-30 under this specific path. Change the
return assumption to 8 per cent blended and the depletion point arrives near year 22; change it to 12 per cent and
the corpus survives 40 years with a legacy. This sensitivity is the whole point: the multiple is a rough-cut
planning device, and the portfolio's _sequence_ of returns in the first decade matters as much as its average.
A bear market in years 1-3 with the same average return leaves a materially smaller corpus at year 30 than the
same average distributed differently.

## Three adjustments the multiple needs in India

**Healthcare is the first.** Medical inflation in India runs at 10-12 per cent, above general CPI, and the
household expense basket that the multiple is based on rarely includes the post-60 reality: insurance premiums that
rise steeply with age (a ₹10 lakh family floater for a 65-year-old costs several multiples of what it does at 45),
out-of-pocket costs, and long-term care — IRDAI's premium and age-band rules, published at
[irdai.gov.in](https://irdai.gov.in/), are why renewal premiums climb so sharply after 60. A defensible adjustment
is to add a second, ring-fenced healthcare corpus — say 15-20 per cent of the headline number — rather than
pretending the expense base grows uniformly.

**Expense shape is the second.** The 4 per cent rule assumes expenses rise smoothly with inflation. Real Indian
retirements are U-shaped: travel and leisure in the 60s, healthcare in the 70s and 80s, and frequently a fall in
discretionary spending after the death of one spouse. Planning a flat inflation-indexed withdrawal when the real
curve is U-shaped means overfunding the middle and underfunding the end — the safer pattern is to bucket the
portfolio by time horizon rather than to trust one blended withdrawal rate.

**Debt and equity mix is the third.** The multiple implicitly assumes a portfolio that can outearn inflation by
5-6 per cent. A retiree who parks everything in FDs at 7 per cent with 6 per cent inflation has a real return of
under 1 per cent, and 25x depletes in under 20 years at any meaningful withdrawal rate. The instrument mix — not
the multiple — is what makes the number safe.

| Portfolio                   | Real return (nominal − 6% inflation) | Years 25x lasts at 4% real-need withdrawal |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| 100% FD at 7%               | ~1%                                  | ~20 years                                  |
| 50/50 debt/equity at 10%    | ~4%                                  | 30+ years                                  |
| 65/35 equity-heavy at 11.5% | ~5.5%                                | 35+ years                                  |

## What to do with the number

The multiple's real function is not prediction but sizing feedback: it tells you in one line whether your current
savings rate is in the right universe. A quick calculation: to hit 30x of today's ₹12 lakh annual expenses in 20
years at an 11 per cent CAGR requires a monthly SIP of roughly ₹85,000 — which is the honest cost of a comfortable
retirement for a household spending ₹1 lakh a month now, and a number most plans quietly fudge by assuming
post-retirement expense collapse. Whether you then choose to spend more now and retire later, or save at that
intensity, is a preference. Pretending the target is ₹2 crore is not.

The practical build is a three-bucket structure — a 3-year expenses buffer in liquid and short-term debt, a
medium bucket in hybrid and target-maturity funds for years 4-10, and the long bucket in equity for the rest —
rebalanced annually and reviewed at every life event. The multiple gets you in the range; the bucketing is what
stops the first bad decade from breaking the plan.
