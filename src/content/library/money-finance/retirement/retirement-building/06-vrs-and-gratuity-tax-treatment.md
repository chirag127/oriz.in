The last paycheque of a career arrives with more line items than any earlier one: gratuity, leave encashment, VRS
compensation if you took it, notice pay if you did not serve notice. Each is taxed on its own rule, and the
difference between the rules is worth lakhs. This article works through them in the order they appear on your
full-and-final statement.

## Gratuity: the exemption and its ceilings

Gratuity under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 is tax-free up to a ceiling for employees covered by the Act. The
exemption is the least of three numbers: the actual gratuity received; ₹20 lakh (the ceiling since 2018, aligned
with the 7th Pay Commission); and the statutory formula of **15 days' salary × completed years of service**. The
formula, for Act-covered employees, uses last-drawn basic plus DA, with 15 days calculated as salary × years ÷ 26 ×
15, and any service beyond six months in the final year counts as a full year.

| Last basic+DA | Years of service | Formula gratuity    | Tax-free up to |
| ------------- | ---------------- | ------------------- | -------------- |
| ₹40,000       | 10               | ₹2,30,769           | ₹2.30 lakh     |
| ₹60,000       | 20               | ₹6,92,307           | ₹6.92 lakh     |
| ₹80,000       | 25               | ₹11,53,846          | ₹11.54 lakh    |
| ₹1,00,000     | 30               | ₹17,30,769          | ₹17.31 lakh    |
| ₹1,20,000     | 33               | ₹20,00,000 (capped) | ₹20 lakh       |

Once gratuity exceeds the formula or the ceiling, the excess is taxed as salary income. Two ceilings stack and
should be understood separately: the ₹20 lakh is the _lifetime_ exemption under Section 10(10) for
Act-covered private-sector employees, while government employees enjoy a separate ₹25 lakh lifetime ceiling under
the same clause. If you have received gratuity from more than one employer across a career, the lifetime cap
applies in aggregate — the second employer's gratuity eats what is left of the exemption.

One frequently-missed edge: employees _not_ covered by the Gratuity Act (some small establishments, certain
contract arrangements) get the "least of three" test with a different half-month formula — salary × years ÷ 30 × 15
— and the same ₹20 lakh cap, but only where the employer's scheme is an approved one. The distinction shows up in
the exemption computation and is worth verifying before assuming the Act formula applies.

## VRS: the Section 10(10C) exemption

A voluntary retirement scheme is taxed as salary income unless it satisfies the conditions of Section 10(10C),
which exempts up to **₹5 lakh** of VRS compensation in a lifetime, subject to the scheme itself meeting statutory
conditions: it applies to an employee who has completed 10 years of service or is 40 or older; it covers employees
of a company, cooperative society, PSU, or specified authority; and it is a scheme in which the employee is
voluntarily retired, not redeployed to a subsidiary.

| Component                                   | Tax treatment                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| VRS compensation up to ₹5 lakh (lifetime)   | Exempt under 10(10C)                                                                                 |
| VRS compensation beyond ₹5 lakh             | Taxed as salary in the year received                                                                 |
| Unused leave encashment                     | Exempt up to ₹25 lakh for non-government employees (₹3 lakh+ per government rules for public sector) |
| Notice pay in lieu (not served)             | Fully taxable as salary                                                                              |
| Retention / joining bonus from new employer | Fully taxable as salary                                                                              |
| Employer's PF balance                       | Tax rules as per EPF withdrawal rules (5-year exemption logic)                                       |
| Employer's NPS contribution                 | Taxable at withdrawal unless annuitised; 80CCD(2) deduction already claimed at contribution          |

The ₹5 lakh VRS exemption is a lifetime figure — take two VRS payouts across a career and only the balance of the
₹5 lakh is available the second time. And the exemption's practical value depends on your slab in the exit year: if
the VRS lands in a year when you also draw salary for six months, the excess over ₹5 lakh compounds your marginal
rate; deferring the exit date into a year of lower income is the single most effective planning lever, and it is
legal and ordinary.

An important trap: the 10(10C) exemption applies to _compensation for voluntary retirement_, not to a severance
package dressed as such. Payments labelled golden handshake, retrenchment compensation or outplacement support are
taxed under different clauses (Section 10(10B) for retrenchment, with its own formula of 15 days' average pay per
completed year). The label on the cheque matters less than the character of the payment in the employer's books —
ask for the computation sheet and check which clause the payroll team is claiming.

## The relocation-of-income question: spread or not

Section 89(1) allows relief when income arrears or lump sums push you into a higher slab in one year, but for VRS
the law goes further: where the exemptible VRS is received along with salary, some employers spread the taxable
portion over the years of service under Rule 21A(2) mechanics. In practice, the choice between claiming relief
under Section 89 and letting the exemption do the work is a computation, not a principle:

| Scenario                                                    | Better route                                                                                  |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| VRS ₹5 lakh, salary also received in same year              | Claim 10(10C) exemption; no relief needed                                                     |
| VRS ₹12 lakh, 25 years of service, low prior-year income    | Exempt ₹5 lakh; Section 89 relief on the ₹7 lakh spread over service years often saves a slab |
| VRS ₹12 lakh, high prior-year income, high exit-year income | Exempt ₹5 lakh; taxable ₹7 lakh at current slab, relief usually marginal                      |

The calculation is mechanical once you have the Form 16 numbers, but employers frequently get it wrong in the F&F
sheet — payroll teams default to taxing the full amount and leave the employee to claim the refund in the annual
return. File the return with the Section 89 computation (Form 10E is mandatory before claiming the relief online)
and the refund follows; the IRD's filing portal at
[incometax.gov.in](https://www.incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/) computes the relief automatically once Form 10E is
uploaded.

## Leave encashment and the residual items

Leave encashment at retirement is exempt up to ₹25 lakh for non-government employees (the ceiling was raised from
₹3 lakh to ₹25 lakh by notification effective from FY 2023-24 assessments), computed on last-drawn basic plus DA
where the employer's rules include it, times unavailed leave days as defined by the employer's leave policy capped
at 30 days per completed year of service. Government employees have a separate, higher exemption. The caseload of
disputes here is small but real: encashment of leave _beyond_ the employer's sanctioned leave balance, or leave
encashment received as part of a VRS package rather than separately, is taxed as salary without the exemption —
the point on which the tax department has won several appeals.

Notice pay recovered from the employee (that is, salary not paid because notice was not served) reduces taxable
salary; notice pay _paid_ by the employee to exit early is not deductible. Severance for retrenchment under
Section 10(10B) has its own exemption formula — 15 days' average pay for every completed year of service, capped
at ₹10 lakh — and applies to "workman"-category employees under the Industrial Disputes Act, not to managers.

## The grey zone

The compliance reality around VRS in Indian industry is that schemes are frequently announced as voluntary and
administered as targeted: the employer selects who may apply, applies pressure on the marginal performers, and
records the exit as voluntary. For the individual employee, this grey zone rarely creates direct tax exposure —
the 10(10C) conditions are on the scheme and the service profile, not on the voluntariness of your decision — but
it can create **consequences** where a scheme is challenged: if the tax department or a labour authority treats the
exit as compulsory retrenchment, the VRS compensation can be recharacterised, the exemption denied, and interest
levied on the under-withheld tax, along with the employer's obligations under the Industrial Disputes Act being
reopened. Employees rarely bear the recharacterisation risk directly, but the **penalt**y risk sits with the
employer, and a contested scheme can freeze the payout. Ask whether the scheme has been filed with the regional
PF and labour authorities, and keep the acceptance letter that records the exit as voluntary — it is the document
that resolves the ambiguity if it is ever raised.
