India's residential rental yields are among the lowest in the world's major property markets — 2 to 3.5 per cent
gross in the large metros — and the fact is quoted everywhere without its arithmetic. This article does the
arithmetic: what the gross yield is by city and property type, what survives vacancy, maintenance, property tax
and brokerage to become the net yield, and what the number means against a fixed deposit or a REIT.

## The gross yield, by city and type

Gross rental yield is annual rent divided by property value. Indian residential figures, using representative
2025-26 prices and rents:

| Market                                    | Typical property value | Monthly rent | Gross yield |
| ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------ | ----------- |
| Mumbai (suburb 1BHK)                      | ₹1.2 crore             | ₹35,000      | 3.5%        |
| Bengaluru (mid-market 2BHK)               | ₹1.0 crore             | ₹30,000      | 3.6%        |
| Gurugram (mid 2BHK)                       | ₹1.3 crore             | ₹40,000      | 3.7%        |
| Pune (mid 2BHK)                           | ₹80 lakh               | ₹25,000      | 3.75%       |
| Hyderabad (mid 2BHK)                      | ₹90 lakh               | ₹26,000      | 3.5%        |
| Chennai (mid 2BHK)                        | ₹85 lakh               | ₹22,000      | 3.1%        |
| Delhi NCR (older 2BHK)                    | ₹1.1 crore             | ₹28,000      | 3.05%       |
| Kolkata (mid 2BHK)                        | ₹65 lakh               | ₹16,000      | 2.95%       |
| Tier-2 (Indore, Coimbatore, Lucknow 2BHK) | ₹55 lakh               | ₹13,000      | 2.8%        |
| Commercial office (Grade A, metro)        | ₹2 crore               | ₹1,00,000    | 6.0%        |
| Commercial retail (high street)           | ₹1.5 crore             | ₹75,000      | 6.0%        |

The residential band sits at 2.8-3.75 per cent gross. Commercial properties yield 5.5-7 per cent gross — nearly
double — which is the entire reason REITs exist as a retail instrument for commercial exposure. And the
comparison instrument is already visible: a bank fixed deposit in 2025-26 pays 6.5-7.5 per cent, senior-citizen
FDs higher still, and a liquid debt fund yields near the overnight-to-6-month curve.

## From gross to net: the cost stack

Gross yield is a marketing number. The costs between rent received and money in hand:

| Cost                                                                              | Typical                                | Effect on ₹80 lakh property renting at ₹25,000 |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Property tax (municipal)                                                          | 0.1-0.3% of value/yr                   | ₹8,000-₹24,000                                 |
| Maintenance / society charges (paid by owner when vacant or if rent is inclusive) | ₹2-₹4/sq ft/month                      | ₹20,000-₹45,000/yr                             |
| Repairs and refurbishment between tenants                                         | 0.3-0.5% of value/yr averaged          | ₹25,000-₹40,000                                |
| Vacancy (1 month/yr typical; 2-3 months on premium)                               | One month's rent                       | ₹25,000                                        |
| Brokerage on re-letting                                                           | 0.5-1 month's rent per new tenant      | ₹12,500-₹25,000                                |
| Property management (if outsourced)                                               | 5-8% of rent                           | ₹15,000-₹24,000                                |
| Income tax on rent                                                                | 30% slab, after 30% standard deduction | ~₹50,000 on ₹2.7 lakh net annual rent          |

The arithmetic on the ₹80 lakh Pune 2BHK: gross annual rent ₹3 lakh. Costs of roughly ₹90,000-₹1.2 lakh (tax,
maintenance share, repairs, vacancy, brokerage) leave ₹1.8-2.1 lakh before tax; the 30 per cent slab on rental
income after the statutory 30 per cent standard deduction takes about ₹50,000. **Net yield after tax: roughly
1.6-2.0 per cent.** The pre-tax net yield sits around 2.4-2.6 per cent — half the gross figure — and the after-tax
number is what competes with a debt fund's post-tax 5.3 per cent for a 30 per cent slab investor.

| Return measure           | Residential 2BHK (Pune example) | Commercial office | FD at 7% |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------- | ----------------- | -------- |
| Gross yield              | 3.75%                           | 6.0%              | 7.0%     |
| Net pre-tax yield        | ~2.5%                           | ~4.8%             | 7.0%     |
| Net after-tax (30% slab) | ~1.8%                           | ~3.4%             | ~4.9%    |

## The appreciation question, answered honestly

The defence of Indian residential property has always been appreciation, not yield — and the defence is
historically sound for the right asset: metro residential values compounded at 8-12 per cent through the 2000s
and early 2010s, and at 5-8 per cent in the last decade with wide variance by micro-market. The honest framing:

| Total return component                         | Conservative case              | Base case                      |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Net rental yield                               | 2.0%                           | 2.5%                           |
| Price appreciation                             | 5%                             | 8%                             |
| **Total return, pre-tax**                      | **7.0%**                       | **10.5%**                      |
| Stamp duty + registration + brokerage at entry | 6-8% one-time                  | 6-8% one-time                  |
| Exit costs (brokerage 1-2%, LTCG tax)          | 12.5% on gains above exemption | 12.5% on gains above exemption |

A total return of 10.5 per cent pre-tax beats debt comfortably, but the transaction costs at both ends — 6-8 per
cent in, 1-2 per cent out, plus LTCG at 12.5 per cent on gains — mean the holding period must exceed 8-10 years
for the arithmetic to work against liquid alternatives. The tax department's capital gains rules for property
are published in the Finance Act texts and the IT department's guidance at
[incometax.gov.in](https://www.incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/); the Section 54/54F reinvestment exemptions are the
planning lever most sellers underuse.

## What the rent actually depends on

Rental income is a business with concentration risk in one asset and one tenant. The variables that move it:

- **Tenant quality**: corporate-leased or embassy-leased tenants pay 5-15 per cent premiums with reliable
  escalation clauses; individual tenants at market rates carry the vacancy and brokerage cycle.
- **Furnishing**: a furnished unit rents 15-30 per cent higher and turns over faster; the capex is recovered in
  2-3 years in the right micro-market.
- **Escalation clauses**: a 5 per cent annual escalation is standard in corporate leases and rare in individual
  renewals (where the negotiation resets to market each time) — the clause is worth more than the headline rent.
- **Micro-market**: the same pincode's premium tower and the adjacent builder floor differ 30 per cent in rent for
  similar carpet area; the yield difference follows the _value_ more than the rent.
- **Regulatory regime**: the model Tenancy Act has been adopted by several states, but the rent-control regimes
  that survive in some jurisdictions affect old leases, not new market-rate ones — a new commercial lease is
  negotiated under contract law and is enforceable as written.

## REITs: the same exposure, priced correctly

A REIT unit is a claim on a portfolio of Grade A commercial properties, with SEBI's minimum-distribution rule
requiring at least 90 per cent of net distributable income to be paid out. Indian REITs (Embassy, Mindspace,
Brookfield, Nexus for retail) have distributed 5.5-7.5 per cent gross yields, taxed as ordinary income on the
rental component, with the unit's price tracking the underlying asset values. The comparison with direct
commercial ownership:

| Feature           | Direct commercial property       | REIT unit                            |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Ticket size       | ₹1-2 crore                       | ₹300-₹400 per unit                   |
| Vacancy risk      | Yours                            | Pooled across portfolio              |
| Liquidity         | Months                           | Same day                             |
| Tenant management | Yours                            | Professional manager                 |
| Yield             | 5.5-7% gross, ~4-4.5% net        | 5.5-7.5% distributed                 |
| Diversification   | One building, one tenant profile | Multiple buildings, multiple tenants |

For the investor whose actual question is "how do I earn rent from real estate without running a rental business",
the REIT is the honest answer at retail scale; the direct commercial unit wins only with scale, a good location
and the willingness to manage tenant cycles.

## The verdict on yield

Indian residential real estate is a leveraged, illiquid, single-asset equity position that pays a 2-3.5 per cent
gross coupon — a bond-like income stream attached to an equity-like asset, bought with 6-8 per cent of
transaction friction and held through management work. The 2 per cent net yield is not a reason to avoid property
if the appreciation case and the use-value (living in it) are the actual reasons for purchase. It is, however, a
decisive reason to stop describing a rented-out flat as "passive income": the yield after costs, vacancy and tax
is nearer a fixed deposit's post-tax return than the rent number on the listing site, and the effort is nearer a
part-time job than an investment.
