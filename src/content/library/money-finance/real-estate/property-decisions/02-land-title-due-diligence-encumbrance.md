Land in India is the asset class where the buyer's diligence matters more than the seller's honesty. A flat in a
RERA project has a regulatory wrapper; a plot of land has only its documents, its registration history and the
revenue records of the district it sits in — and the gap between "the seller has papers" and "the title is clean"
is where most land disputes in Indian courts are born. This is the working checklist, in the order a due-diligence
lawyer runs it.

## The chain of title: 30 years, not 13

The core document is the chain of conveyances from the current seller back to the origin of title — typically 30
years of documents (13 years is the older banker's convention; 30 is the standard for a purchase with litigation
risk in mind). Each link must show: the seller of one deed as the buyer of the previous one, full legal names and
identifiers (not "S/o" shorthand that matches three people in the village), the survey number and boundaries,
and the consideration and payment mode traceable through bank records.

| Check                       | Document                                                       | Where to verify                                     |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Ownership chain             | Sale deeds, gift deeds, inheritance/will, partition deeds      | Sub-registrar's records; certified copies on demand |
| Encumbrances                | Encumbrance Certificate (EC) for the relevant period           | Sub-registrar / state land records portal           |
| Revenue entry               | Mutation record, khata/patta/record-of-rights                  | Tehsildar's office, state bhulekh-type portals      |
| Possession                  | Physical inspection, encroachment status, boundary demarcation | Site visit + licensed surveyor                      |
| Litigation                  | Case search on the property and the sellers                    | District court, High Court portals; local lawyer    |
| Dues                        | Property tax receipts, electricity, water dues                 | Municipal records                                   |
| Approvals (if construction) | Building plan sanction, layout approval, CLU                   | Urban development authority                         |
| Land-use compliance         | Master plan zoning of the survey number                        | Development authority's master plan                 |

The chain breaks in predictable ways: a deceased owner's legal heirs never executed a release deed; a family
partition was recorded in a family arrangement never registered; a power of attorney used instead of a sale deed
(SC's 2011 ruling in _Suraj Lamp_ famously held that POA "sales" do not transfer title, leaving a generation of
such transactions needing regularisation). Every break is fixable — by a release deed, a ratification, a
succession certificate — and every unfixed break is the next 20-year litigation.

## The encumbrance certificate, decoded

The EC is the sub-registrar's certified record of registered transactions against the property for a period —
sales, mortgages, leases, lis pendens where registered. It comes in two flavours that buyers routinely confuse:

| Type                                   | What it shows                                                           | What it does NOT show                                                               |
| -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Encumbered EC (Form 15 in most states) | Every registered transaction in the period — mortgages, releases, sales | Unregistered arrangements                                                           |
| Nil EC (Form 16)                       | "No transactions recorded" for the period                               | Unregistered mortgages, prior-period encumbrances, court attachments not registered |

Three structural gaps to know. First, **the EC only records registered instruments** — an unregistered mortgage by
deposit of title deeds (the common form for banks in some states historically) or a family arrangement leaves no
EC trace. Second, **the EC is period-bound**; ask for the full period from the origin of title, not just 13 years.
Third, in states with computerised records the EC is reliable; in states with partial digitisation, the EC's
coverage of older documents depends on what the sub-registrar's office actually indexed — which is why a
**physical search of the index books for the pre-digitisation years** is still standard practice in serious
transactions, done through a local lawyer.

Registration mechanics themselves come from the Registration Act, 1908 and the Indian Stamp Act (with state
amendments) — the registration fee and stamp duty schedules by state are published by the state registration
departments; the central e-governance framework is documented on [india.gov.in](https://www.india.gov.in/), and
each state's registration department portal — Kaveri in Karnataka at
[kaveri.karnataka.gov.in](https://kaveri.karnataka.gov.in/), IGR Maharashtra at
[igrmaharashtra.gov.in](https://igrmaharashtra.gov.in/), UP's IGRS at [igrsup.gov.in](https://igrsup.gov.in/) —
is the authoritative source for encumbrance certificate applications and certified copy requests.

## Mutation, khata and the difference between ownership and revenue entry

Mutation is the revenue record's entry of a new owner after a registered transfer. It is _not_ proof of title —
the courts are clear that mutation entries are for fiscal purposes (tax liability) and do not confer or
extinguish title — but its absence signals a seller who has been casual with their own paper, and its presence
with the correct names is evidence of continuous, undisputed possession. The khata/patta/record-of-rights is what
the local revenue officer, the electricity board and the municipality will demand when the buyer seeks utilities
and building sanction; a property without khata registration is a property about to generate the buyer's next
expense.

## The litigation and possession checks

Court searches are the step skipped most often and regretted most expensively:

| Search                                                                | Purpose                                                                 |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| District court cases naming the seller and the property survey number | Pending suits, injunctions on alienation                                |
| CERSAI's central registry (mortgage registrations)                    | Security interests registered under SARFAESI/Companies Act              |
| Revenue court records                                                 | Tenancy and ceiling disputes on agricultural land                       |
| Sub-registrar's lis pendens register (where maintained)               | Registered notices of pending litigation                                |
| Physical site inspection with the seller                              | Actual possession, who is in occupation, encroachments, boundary stones |

The possession check is where oral testimony beats paper: a plot shown empty on paper may have a tenant under an
unregistered agreement, an encroacher with a pending regularisation application, or a neighbour whose boundary
wall sits two feet inside the survey line. Commission a licensed surveyor to demarcate the survey number against
the government's survey records — the ₹15,000-₹25,000 fee is the cheapest insurance in the transaction, and the
surveyor's report is admissible when the boundary dispute arrives.

## The grey zones buyers accept, and their consequences

Indian land practice carries several widely-used structures whose legal status is weaker than the market treats
them:

- **POA-based sales** — after _Suraj Lamp & Industries v. State of Haryana_ (2011), sale through general power of
  attorney without a registered conveyance does not transfer title; the buyer holds a precarious interest and the
  transaction invites **consequences** — the stamp authority can treat it as a conveyance and demand duty plus
  **penalt**y, and a later claimant with better paper can dispossess the POA "buyer".
- **Agricultural land bought by non-agriculturists** in states with land-ceiling and purchaser-eligibility rules
  (Karnataka's old framework, several others) — the eligibility question is state-specific and a violation can
  void the transfer.
- **Unapproved layouts** — plots carved without the development authority's approval carry no building sanction
  possibility; regularisation schemes come and cost, and the **consequences** of buying unapproved include
  demolition risk on construction.
- **GPA + will arrangements and benami holdings** — a benami transaction sits inside the Prohibition of Benami
  Property Transactions Act, 1988 (as amended 2016), under which the benamidar's holding can be attached and
  eventually acquired by the state, with **prosecut**ion available against the parties to the arrangement; the
  buyer who takes land "in someone else's name for convenience" is signing up for exactly this regime.

None of these make a transaction illegal per se — the grey is in the execution and the documentation, and the
**consequences** fall on whoever holds the weaker paper at the moment of dispute.

## The closing sequence, in the right order

A defensible purchase closes like this: title search and EC for 30 years; court and CERSAI searches; survey
demarcation; draft agreement with clear possession date and title-condition precedents; payment through banking
channels (the stamp authority will demand the source; cash consideration components create both stamp and tax
problems); registration at the sub-registrar's office with witnesses and biometrics; mutation application within
the statutory window; khata transfer and utility name changes; physical possession handover with an inventory.
Each step has a document; a transaction missing any document is a transaction with a future problem attached to
a present discount.
