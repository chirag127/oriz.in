Every July the same argument restarts on the internet, and both sides are usually working from stale numbers.
Since the Finance Act (No. 2) 2025 rewrote the new-regime slabs and raised the Section 87A rebate, the arithmetic
has changed materially for incomes up to ₹24 lakh — and the answer to "which regime is better" is now _no longer
the same_ for a person earning ₹10 lakh and a person earning ₹20 lakh. This walkthrough runs both regimes for
four representative salaries, using the FY 2025-26 (AY 2026-27) rules, so you can see where the crossover sits.

## The two structures, side by side

**New regime (Section 115BAC, default).** Slabs for FY 2025-26: nil up to ₹4,00,000; 5% from ₹4-8 lakh; 10%
from ₹8-12 lakh; 15% from ₹12-16 lakh; 20% from ₹16-20 lakh; 25% from ₹20-24 lakh; 30% above ₹24 lakh. The
Section 87A rebate for resident individuals: nil tax up to ₹12 lakh income (₹60,000 rebate; marginal relief
applies just above it). Salary-income earners also get a **standard deduction of ₹75,000** in the new regime,
which effectively makes ₹12.75 lakh of gross salary tax-free. Surcharge thresholds and rates (10% above
₹50 lakh, 15% above ₹1 crore, 25% above ₹2 crore, 37% above ₹5 crore — the 37% band not applying under 115BAC's
marginal-relief structure for certain incomes) remain, with the surcharge capped at 25% in the new regime.
Cess is 4% on tax plus surcharge.

**Old regime (optional).** Slabs: nil to ₹2.5 lakh; 5% to ₹5 lakh; 20% to ₹10 lakh; 30% above. The ₹87A rebate
gives nil tax up to ₹5 lakh (rebate ₹12,500; ₹25,000 for senior citizens up to ₹5 lakh income). The value of the
old regime lives entirely in its deductions: 80C (₹1.5 lakh), 80D health insurance, 80TTA/80TTB, HRA under
Section 10(13A), home-loan interest under Section 24(b), NPS under 80CCD(1B), and the ₹50,000 standard
deduction.

| Item                                  | Old regime                            | New regime (115BAC)                                                             |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Basic exemption (under 60)            | ₹2,50,000                             | ₹4,00,000                                                                       |
| Slab rates                            | 5% / 20% / 30%                        | 5/10/15/20/25/30%                                                               |
| Standard deduction (salary)           | ₹50,000                               | ₹75,000                                                                         |
| 87A rebate limit                      | ₹5 lakh income                        | ₹12 lakh income                                                                 |
| 80C, 80D, 80CCD(1B), HRA, 24(b)       | Allowed                               | Mostly _not_ allowed                                                            |
| NPS employer contribution 80CCD(2)    | Up to 10% of salary                   | Up to 14% of salary                                                             |
| Setting off / carrying forward losses | Allowed                               | House-property loss set-off restricted; unabsorbed depreciation carried forward |
| The regime to file for                | Optional, chosen each year in the ITR | Default; salaried choose via the employer declaration                           |

## Four worked examples, FY 2025-26

All four are salaried, under 60, with the same two deduction scenarios: (A) minimal deductions — a person who
does little tax planning; (B) deduction-heavy — ₹1.5 lakh of 80C, ₹25,000 of 80D, ₹2 lakh home-loan interest,
₹50,000 of NPS 80CCD(1B).

**₹10 lakh gross salary.** New regime: ₹10,00,000 − ₹75,000 = ₹9,25,000 taxable; tax ₹47,500 (5% band ₹4-8 lakh
= ₹20,000; 10% band ₹8-9.25 lakh = ₹12,500); rebate brings it to **nil** — income after standard deduction is
under ₹12 lakh. Old regime with deductions (B): ₹10,00,000 − ₹50,000 − ₹4,25,000 = ₹5,25,000; tax ₹13,750 plus
cess = **₹14,300**. New regime wins by ₹14,300.

**₹15 lakh gross salary.** New regime: ₹14,25,000 taxable. Slab by slab: ₹4-8 lakh @ 5% = ₹20,000; ₹8-12 lakh
@ 10% = ₹40,000; ₹12-14.25 lakh @ 15% = ₹33,750. Total ₹93,750; +4% cess = **₹97,500**. Old regime (B): ₹15,00,000 − ₹50,000 − ₹4,25,000 = ₹10,25,000; tax ₹1,37,500;
cess ₹5,500 = **₹1,43,000**. New regime wins by ₹45,500.

**₹20 lakh gross salary.** New regime: ₹19,25,000 taxable; 4-8 ₹20,000; 8-12 ₹40,000; 12-16 ₹60,000;
16-19.25 @20% = ₹65,000; total ₹1,85,000; cess ₹7,400 = **₹1,92,400**. Old regime (B): ₹20,00,000 − ₹4,75,000 =
₹15,25,000; tax ₹2,80,000; cess ₹11,200 = **₹2,91,200**. New regime wins by ₹98,800.

**₹30 lakh gross salary.** New regime: ₹29,25,000 taxable; slabs to ₹24 lakh total ₹3,00,000; 24-29.25 @30% =
₹1,57,500; total ₹4,57,500; cess ₹18,300 = **₹4,75,800**. Old regime (B): ₹30,00,000 − ₹4,75,000 = ₹25,25,000;
tax ₹6,57,500; cess ₹26,300 = **₹6,83,800**. New regime wins by ₹2,08,000 — the gap widens because the old
regime's deductions are a fixed ₹4-5 lakh against a much larger income.

| Gross salary | New regime tax | Old regime tax (deduction-heavy) | Winner |
| ------------ | -------------- | -------------------------------- | ------ |
| ₹10,00,000   | ₹0             | ₹14,300                          | New    |
| ₹15,00,000   | ₹97,500        | ₹1,43,000                        | New    |
| ₹20,00,000   | ₹1,92,400      | ₹2,91,200                        | New    |
| ₹30,00,000   | ₹4,75,800      | ₹6,83,800                        | New    |

The pattern in FY 2025-26 is blunt: for salaried individuals, the new regime wins at almost every income level
unless the deduction load is unusually high — a housing-loan interest deduction above ₹2 lakh combined with high
rent and an HRA that actually computes favourably, in metros, with dependants' health insurance. Even then the
gap has narrowed to the point where the old regime rarely wins by much.

## What the new regime still lets you claim

The list is shorter than the internet suggests, but not empty. The employer's NPS contribution under
**80CCD(2)** up to 14% of salary is allowed and is one of the few ways to add real deduction under the new
regime. The standard deduction of ₹75,000 applies. Employer EPF contributions are excluded from income up to
the statutory limit. Interest on a home loan for a _let-out_ property is deductible up to ₹2 lakh under
Section 24(b) — the restriction is only on the self-occupied property's interest. Gifted amounts up to ₹50,000
from specified relatives remain exempt; gratuity exemptions under Section 10(10), leave encashment under
10(10AA) and retrenchment compensation retain their limits. Agniveer Corpus Fund contributions, family-pension
deduction of ₹25,000 under 80D, and interest up to ₹2,000 on savings under 80TTA for the old regime only.
The important losses: 80C on PPF/ELSS/insurance, 80D on own health premiums (except the family-pension case),
80TTB, 80U, 80G beyond the limited new-regime allowance, and most crucially **the home-loan interest on a
self-occupied house**.

## The legality note

Choosing a regime is legal every year and irreversible only in the narrow sense: an individual with business
income who opts for the old regime can switch back to the new regime only once in a lifetime (Section 115BAC(5)
proviso); salaried individuals without business income can switch freely every year. The consequences of getting
the declaration wrong are practical, not criminal: the employer deducts TDS under Section 192 on the wrong
assumption and the correction happens at filing, with interest under Sections 234B/234C on any shortfall. What
carries penalties is manufacturing deductions: fake donation receipts, inflated HRA backed by rent to relatives
who do not actually pay (Section 270A treats this as misreporting with up to 200% penalty), or 80C claims for
premiums paid in the name of ineligible relatives. Deductions must be real, paid, and in your own name. Rates
and slabs are at
[incometax.gov.in](https://incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/).

## The decision rule

If your total old-regime deductions are under ₹4.5 lakh and you are salaried, take the new regime and stop
re-litigating it every year. If your deductions are materially higher, run the calculation once in April with
real numbers. Either way, the money you were spending on tax-avoidance instruments purely for the deduction —
the fifth ELSS, the unnecessary LIC endowment — is now free to be invested for returns rather than for Section
80C compliance. That is the actual prize of the new regime: it converted tax planning back into investment
planning.
