TDS is the tax department's cash-flow insurance: tax collected at the source of payment rather than at the year's
end. For a salaried person, the practical reality is that TDS is mostly _your_ money being held at zero interest
by other people until you file a return. Getting the declarations right in April is the difference between a
refund you wait four months for and a tax bill you pay in instalments. These are the sections that touch
salaried life.

## Section 192: salary TDS, the one that governs your monthly payslip

Your employer deducts tax on salary at slab rates averaged over the year, _after_ allowing the deductions you
declared. Section 192 has no threshold of its own — TDS applies whenever total tax on estimated salary exceeds
₹2,500 a year in practice (employers must deduct once tax exceeds the basic exemption). The mechanics people get
wrong:

- The employer deducts on the **regime you declared** (new by default since FY 2023-24 unless you opted out).
  Change the declaration by writing to the employer; a change in March is a valid change, though the TDS for the
  earlier months was already deducted on the old basis and refunds happen only at filing.
- **Perquisites are salary.** ESOP perquisite at exercise, rent-free accommodation valued per rules, employer's
  NPS contribution above 14% (new regime) or 10% (old regime), stock options of foreign parents, Club memberships.
  Employees who forget perquisites get under-deducted TDS and a March surprise.
- **Other income can be declared to the employer.** Under Section 192(2C), you can declare interest, dividends,
  rental income and capital gains so the employer includes them in TDS computation. Declared, the TDS covers it;
  undeclared, you pay advance tax on it or face 234B/234C interest.

| Item                              | Treated as            | TDS by employer             | Notes                                                               |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Basic + HRA + allowances          | Salary head           | Yes, averaged               | HRA exemption reduces taxable salary if declared with rent receipts |
| Employer NPS 80CCD(2)             | Deduction             | Reduces TDS base            | Up to 14% of salary (new regime), 10% (old)                         |
| ESOP exercised                    | Perquisite            | Yes, on FMV at exercise     | Also creates cost base for future capital gains                     |
| Gratuity (private, non-PF-exempt) | Exempt up to ₹20 lakh | Reduces TDS in receipt year | Section 10(10) limit                                                |
| Leave encashment at retirement    | Exempt up to ₹25 lakh | Case-by-case                | Section 10(10AA)                                                    |

## Sections beyond salary: the ones salaried people actually meet

**194A — bank interest.** TDS at 10% when aggregate interest from a bank branch exceeds ₹40,000 a year
(₹1,00,000 for senior citizens). Post offices and co-operative banks have their own thresholds (₹40,000/₹50,000
for seniors, raised by recent amendments). The trap: a person with five FDs across branches can cross the
threshold at each branch without realising; each branch deducts separately on its own crossing. Form 15G (under 60) or 15H (senior) stops TDS at source _only if_ total tax is genuinely nil — filing them while taxable is a
false declaration.

**194-IA — property purchase.** If you buy property for over ₹50 lakh, _you_ are the deductor: 1% TDS (2% from
23 July 2024 for consideration above ₹50 lakh on immovable property other than residential, per the 2024
changes) deposited by the buyer, with Form 26QB. A salaried person buying a flat becomes a deductor and faces
Section 201 interest and penalty for default if it is missed.

**194-I — rent paid.** If you pay rent over ₹50,000 a month as an individual (liable to tax audit only, per the
2019 amendment individuals are outside TDS under 194-I unless under audit; but the 194-IB 2%/5% regime applies
to individuals/HUFs not under audit, at 2% from October 2024), you deduct TDS on rent. Tenants frequently
discover this when the landlord's PAN is demanded months into the tenancy.

**194J — professional fees.** If you consult or freelance and receive fees, the _payer_ deducts 10% (2% for
technical services) above ₹50,000 (₹30,000 for some categories). Your "salary-adjacent" freelance income is
therefore TDS-taxed and must still be shown as business/professional income in the ITR.

**194 Dividends and 194A on bonds.** Dividends over ₹5,000 from a single company: 10% TDS. Interest on listed
NCDs and debentures: 10% above ₹5,000 (from FY 2025-26 thresholds aligned by the Finance Act 2025 amendments).

| Section | Payment                             | Threshold (FY 2025-26)     | Rate                                     | Who deducts     |
| ------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | --------------- |
| 192     | Salary                              | Tax over basic exemption   | Slab                                     | Employer        |
| 194A    | Bank interest                       | ₹40,000 (₹1,00,000 senior) | 10%                                      | Bank            |
| 194     | Dividends                           | ₹5,000 per company         | 10%                                      | Company         |
| 194-IA  | Property purchase by you            | ₹50 lakh                   | 1% (2% non-residential from 23 Jul 2024) | You, the buyer  |

## Form 16, Form 26AS and the refund you are financing

Form 16 Part A is the employer's TDS certificate, generated from the department's TRACES portal, with quarterly
deduction details. Part B is the annexure of salary, perquisites, deductions allowed. It is a _certificate of
what was deducted_, not a statement of what you owe. The reconciliation mistake: employees check Form 16, not
26AS, and file based on Form 16 alone. If the employer's TDS return was filed with an error (a wrong challan
mapping), the credit is not in 26AS, and a return claiming a credit not yet visible gets flagged for
verification. Check 26AS before filing; chase discrepancies in April, not July.

The bigger error is structural: over-deducted TDS is a loan to the government at zero interest, returned after
processing the ITR — typically 3-5 months after filing. Under-deducted TDS is a liability with interest under
234B (1% a month from April) and 234C. A person with ₹40,000 of non-salary interest income who declared nothing
to the employer under-advances tax by roughly ₹12,400 at the 31% slab and owes 234B/234C interest of about
₹700-900 — small but entirely avoidable by filing Form 15G when eligible, or paying advance tax by 15 June
when not.

| Scenario                                     | What happens          | Cost                                 |
| -------------------------------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| All income declared to employer              | TDS covers everything | No refund wait, no 234B              |
| Interest income not declared, no advance tax | 234B + 234C interest  | ~₹700-1,000 on ₹12,000 of tax        |
| Form 15G filed while taxable                 | False declaration     | TDS still due, scrutiny risk         |
| TDS credit missing in 26AS at filing         | Return flagged        | Weeks of delay, verification notices |

## The deadlines that matter for the deductors you deal with

Deductors file quarterly TDS returns; deductions from April-June are due by 31 July, with certificates due
within 15 days of the filing due date. If your employer is late, your Form 16 is late and your filing slips —
which is why the _employer's_ compliance, not yours, is often what delays a refund. For property purchases, the
buyer's 26QB is due within 30 days of month-end of deduction; late payment accrues interest at 1% a day-month
(1.5% for the deductor's default on other sections) and a penalty under Section 271H up to ₹1 lakh for late
filing of the statement.

## The legality note

TDS compliance is mostly the deductor's problem, but the consequences spill onto you. Claiming TDS credit for
amounts not actually deducted and deposited is a misclaim — Section 199 ties credit to actual deposit, and
mismatched claims delay processing; inflating TDS claims deliberately is concealment of income with penalty up
to 200% of tax under Section 270A and interest under 234B/234C. Filing Form 15G/15H when taxable is a false
declaration and can attract the penalty under Section 277 — prosecution-eligible for large amounts, and always
embarrassing at scrutiny. For deductors reading this: late deduction costs interest at 1% per month under
Section 201(1A), late deposit 1.5%, late filing a fee under Section 234E of ₹200 a day and penalty under 271H.
Forms, thresholds and notifications are at
[incometax.gov.in](https://incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/).

## The two checks that end TDS trouble

Once a year, in February: log into TRACES or the e-filing portal, download 26AS, and match every TDS entry
against your own certificates. Once a year, in April: make the declarations to your employer. These two
one-hour habits handle 95% of TDS problems an individual will ever have — the rest are deductors' errors, which
you fix by chasing them in writing, with the 26AS screenshot attached, in the month you find them.
