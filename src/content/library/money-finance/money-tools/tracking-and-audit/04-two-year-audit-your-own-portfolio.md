Two years is the shortest honest review period. One year of returns is noise with a graph; three years feels
distant. Twenty-four months of statements contain at least one correction, one rally, one regulatory change
and one of your own mistakes — enough evidence to audit something real. This article is the audit: eight
questions, each answerable from documents you already have, each ending in a decision. Book it as a recurring
calendar item; the questions do not change, which is what makes the answers comparable over years.

## Setup: assemble the evidence, two hours

Pull, before starting: the annual CAS (Consolidated Account Statement from NSDL/CAMS) for both years, the
demat and broker contract notes, the bank statements, the EPFO passbook, the ITR acknowledgements with
capital-gains schedules, and — if you kept one — the decision log or the notes app where you recorded why you
bought things. The audit is only as good as the evidence, and the CAS is the spine: every regulated holding
under one PAN, one document. Request it free from the NSDL facility at
[nsdl.co.in](https://nsdl.co.in) if you do not already receive it.

## The eight questions

**1. What is the actual portfolio now, and how did it drift?** Compute asset-class weights today and at the
audit's start. A 60/30/10 that became 71/22/7 over two years has been running a different plan than the one
you wrote. The drift number is the audit's opening finding, and it decides whether a rebalance is due.

**2. What did it cost, all-in?** Total the direct expense ratios × holding values, the regular-plan trail you
are still paying, the brokerage, the transaction charges, the STT. On a ₹50 lakh portfolio, the difference
between all-direct and two regular funds is ₹30,000-50,000 a year — the audit's most common actionable
finding.

**3. What did the behaviour cost?** Count the transactions that were not SIP instalments or rebalances: the
tactical switches, the theme exits, the "repositionings". Each realised switch is a tax event and a
dispersion event. The honest arithmetic: sum the realised gains on switches (tax) and compare the switched
funds' forward returns with the ones you left. Most two-year windows show the switches net-cost 0.5-1.5% of
the switched amount.

**4. Which holding would you not buy today?** For each holding, one sentence: "I would buy this today
because ___." Where the sentence needs an excuse ("it will recover", "I'm attached to it"), you have found
the sell-list — the decision to actually sell belongs to the rebalance, but the list belongs to the audit.

**5. Which holding did you forget?** The account you last opened in 2023, the fund whose statements go to an
old email, the employer NPS from two jobs ago. Forgotten holdings are unmanaged ones; the audit's job here is
consolidation and nomination renewal, not strategy.

**6. Did the plan have a bad year, or did the plan get abandoned?** Separate the two. A 60/40 through a
correcting market has bad years by design; that is not failure. A plan abandoned mid-correction — SIPs
paused in month three of a drawdown — is the failure, and the fix is mechanical: the SIP date and amount are
set where a bad quarter cannot break them.

**7. What did you learn from the two years' one-way doors?** List decisions that cannot be unwound cheaply:
the ULIP with a five-year lock-in, the PMS ticket, the property registration. One-way doors deserve a
paragraph each in the audit, because their cost compounds silently and they are the reason the next one-way
door should be opened slower.

**8. What is the next two years' change in obligations?** School admissions, a possible home purchase, parents'
health trajectories. The audit ends forward: the corpus's liquidity requirements for the coming 24 months get
checked against the debt sleeve, and shortfalls move money now rather than in the month of the need.

## The scoring sheet

| #   | Question             | Finding type      | Action slot                |
| --- | -------------------- | ----------------- | -------------------------- |
| 1   | Drift                | Rebalance trigger | Quarterly band check       |
| 2   | Costs                | Money back        | Regular-to-direct switches |
| 3   | Behaviour tax        | Habit finding     | Rule for next 24 months    |
| 4   | Would-not-rebuy list | Sell-list         | Next rebalance             |
| 5   | Forgotten holdings   | Housekeeping      | Consolidate, re-nominate   |
| 6   | Plan vs adherence    | Process finding   | SIP size and date fix      |
| 7   | One-way doors        | Reflection        | Slower decision protocol   |
| 8   | Coming obligations   | Liquidity         | Debt-sleeve adjustment     |

Write the answers down — a dated one-page memo per audit. The sequence of memos, read three audits apart, is
the most accurate picture of your own judgement you will ever own, and it beats any app's insight cards,
because it records the reasoning, not just the positions.

## The instruments this audit quietly fixes

Two pieces of housekeeping surface almost every time and are worth their own lines in the memo. **Nomination
rot:** EPFO nominations older than two changes of job, demat nominations in a dead nominee's name, insurance
policies naming a parent who has since passed — each is a claim-time disaster queued for the future, and each
takes ten minutes to fix once seen. **KYC and contact rot:** the old email receiving CAS statements, the
dead mobile number registered with the broker, the PAN-Aadhaar link that lapsed. Neither finding changes your
returns; both decide whether your family's access to the money survives an emergency, which is the audit's
less-advertised but higher-stakes half.

## The two findings that repeat in almost every first audit

**Cost leakage nobody priced:** the regular-plan funds from the 2021 era, the insurance-investment mix, the
savings account earning 2.7% while holding ₹8 lakh. First audits commonly surface ₹25,000-60,000 a year of
leakage on a ₹50 lakh portfolio — without a single risky change being required.

**The behaviour tax already paid:** switches, exits and theme-hops whose combined tax-plus-dispersion cost
exceeds every rupee of fees saved by picking funds carefully. The audit's behavioural conclusion is
unfashionable and reliable: fewer decisions, made on a calendar, cost less than better decisions made often.

## The bottom line

The two-year audit is the portfolio's equivalent of a blood panel: unglamorous, scheduled, and the reason
small problems get treated before they are expensive. Eight questions, two hours, one memo — and the answers
compound across audits the way the corpus compounds across quarters. The first one is uncomfortable for the
same reason the first blood panel is: it reports on behaviour, not intentions. Book it for the first Sunday
after your ITR is filed, when the year's paperwork is already on the desk.
