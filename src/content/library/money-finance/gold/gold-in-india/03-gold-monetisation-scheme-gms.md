India holds an estimated 25,000+ tonnes of private gold, and most of it sits in lockers earning nothing. The Gold
Monetisation Scheme (GMS) is the government's standing attempt to put that metal to work: you deposit jewellery
with a bank, it is melted and assayed, and the bank pays you interest on the gold's weight — with the principal
returned in gold or its monetary equivalent at maturity. The economics are sound. The behaviour is the obstacle,
and this article is honest about both.

## The scheme's structure

GMS runs on the 2015 framework (the successor to the 1999 Gold Deposit Scheme), with deposit certificates issued
by designated banks after testing and refining at a Purity Verification Centre:

| Tenor                   | Minimum  | Interest rate (gold-based)    | Redemption                                          |
| ----------------------- | -------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Short-term (1-3 years)  | 30 grams | Set by bank (~2.25-2.5% p.a.) | In gold or money, at the bank's option per contract |
| Medium-term (5-7 years) | 30 grams | 2.25% p.a.                    | In gold or money, bank's option                     |
| Long-term (12-15 years) | 30 grams | 2.50% p.a.                    | In gold or money, bank's option                     |

The rates are gold-denominated: deposit 100 grams for a year and earn roughly 2.25 grams of gold-equivalent
interest, irrespective of the metal's price path. The minimum deposit is 30 grams of raw gold, and any purity
above 14 karat is acceptable — the assay determines the fine gold content, and interest accrues on that refined
figure, not on what you handed in. Prepayment is allowed after the minimum lock-in (short-term: 6 months;
medium and long-term: 3 years) with the interest rate adjusted to the shorter tenor's slab.

The interest rate calendar matters: the government resets GMS rates periodically alongside small-savings
notifications, and the current rates (2.25 per cent medium, 2.5 per cent long-term) have held since the July 2020
revision. Verify the current notification before assuming these figures — the scheme's operational circulars and
rate notifications are published through the Department of Economic Affairs and the RBI, with the operative
details at [rbi.org.in](https://www.rbi.org.in/).

## The mechanics, step by step

The process is more invasive than any other gold product, which is the first behavioural barrier:

1. **Walk in with the gold** — ornaments, coins, bars, any form above 14 karat, minimum 30 grams, with identity
   documents (PAN mandatory above the threshold; no income-proof requirements).
2. **The Purity Verification Centre tests and melts.** This is the step families hesitate at: your grandmother's
   bangle goes into a fire assay, and what comes back is refined gold credited to a certificate. The design loss
   on melting is real but small (typically under 0.5 per cent for bullion-grade metal; more for stoned or
   low-purity jewellery where the stones are separated and returned or valued separately).
3. **The bank issues a certificate** for the fine gold content, denominated in grams, with the tenor and rate.
4. **Interest is paid in gold-equivalent** — credited as grams or paid in money at the prevailing price, per the
   option you choose; both are taxable.
5. **At maturity**, the principal is returned in gold or its rupee equivalent, at the bank's option under the
   scheme terms — which is the product's structural quirk, discussed below.

## The tax treatment

| Event                                 | Tax                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Interest earned (gold-denominated)    | Taxable as income from other sources at slab, valued at the prevailing price                                                                                                                  |
| Capital gains on redemption           | The deposit is exempt from capital gains tax on the redemption of gold principal — the scheme's tax incentive under Section 47 (no capital gains on conversion of gold into deposit and back) |
| TDS                                   | Not deducted on interest in most retail cases; declare in ITR                                                                                                                                 |
| Deposit at maturity returned as money | Gains/losses per capital gains provisions on the gold value                                                                                                                                   |

The Section 47 conversion exemption is the scheme's quiet gift: moving physical gold into a GMS deposit and back
out at maturity does not trigger capital gains tax on the price appreciation that occurred in between. Compare
that to selling the same jewellery (LTCG at 12.5 per cent above the exemption) and buying an SGB with the proceeds
— a taxable round trip the GMS avoids. For a family holding gold bought at ₹12,000 per 10 grams now marked at
₹74,000, the difference between a taxable sale and a GMS deposit is material: on 100 grams, the deferred gain is
₹62 lakh, and the LTCG tax on it at 12.5 per cent (after the ₹1.25 lakh annual exemption) would be around
₹7.6 lakh — avoided by the deposit route if held to maturity.

## Why families still say no

The scheme's uptake has been modest relative to the metal in Indian lockers, and the reasons are behavioural and
structural in equal measure:

| Objection                          | The real substance                                                                                                             |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| "The melting is unacceptable"      | Sentiment and design are destroyed; studded pieces lose their work; the certificate replaces the heirloom                      |
| "Rates are too low"                | 2.25-2.5% gold-denominated competes with nothing else gold offers; it is a yield on idle metal, not a return on a growth asset |
| "The bank returns money, not gold" | At the bank's option; a family that wants the metal back may face redemption in rupees at the then-price                       |
| "Purity test will embarrass us"    | Low-purity or undercarataged jewellery returns a smaller gram figure than the family believes                                  |
| "Locker feels safer than a bank"   | The trust asymmetry: physical possession versus an institution's promise                                                       |

The honest rebuttal to the rate objection: GMS is not an investment competing with equity or even with SGB — it is
a yield on an asset that would otherwise earn zero, with the principal's price risk fully retained (you still own
the gold's upside and downside). A family with 200 grams of idle metal in a locker earns ~4.5 grams a year on a
long-term deposit for doing nothing except accepting the assay — roughly ₹33,000 of gold at current prices, every
year, tax-effected through slab rates.

## Where GMS fits, and where it does not

**Fits:** inherited or scrap gold with no design value (chains, coins, broken pieces, mismatched sets); metal
held purely as a store of value by a family that does not wear it; temple-trust and institution holdings; a
bequest being consolidated by heirs who do not want the locker. **Does not fit:** worn jewellery the family will
keep wearing (the melting is disqualifying); studded designer pieces where work is a large share of value; gold
the family wants to pledge later for a loan (a GMS deposit is not collateral for your own borrowing in the way a
locker's metal is); anyone who wants price upside _plus_ an exit option before the tenor.

For the "fits" column, GMS competes directly with three alternatives — sell and buy SGBs (taxable round trip,
higher yield of 2.5 per cent plus market upside on the _new_ instrument), keep in locker (zero yield, zero
friction), or gold loan against the metal (keeps possession claim but pays the bank interest instead of earning
it). The GMS's Section 47 exemption and its sovereign backing make it the cleanest of these for genuinely idle
metal.

## The operational fine print

Three clauses cause disputes. First, the redemption option: the scheme permits banks to redeem in money at their
option, and a family planning to receive physical gold back may be disappointed — clarify the redemption mode in
writing at deposit. Second, the assay's gram figure is the deposit's basis; if the family's mental inventory says
110 grams and the certificate says 96, the gap is the purity and design loss, and it is permanent. Third,
deposits above the monetary threshold are subject to KYC and reporting, and while the scheme itself is
government-backed and fully legal, depositing **unaccounted gold** — metal whose acquisition cannot be documented —
into the banking system has been the scheme's intended policy purpose, but a depositor's own tax position on the
metal's provenance is not cured by the deposit: the scheme is not an amnesty, and income-tax scrutiny of
unexplained holdings follows its own rules with their own **consequences**. Deposit gold whose story you can tell.
