Gold is the Indian household's default asset — inherited, gifted, accumulated, occasionally sold in a
crisis — and the only asset class whose most popular form (jewellery) is also its worst investment form. The
decision between physical gold, sovereign paper and ETFs is worth more than the decision to hold gold at
all, because the spread between jewellery's real return and the bond's is measured in percentages per year.
This article prices each route.

## The routes, priced for what they actually return

| Route                    | Typical cost at entry          | Ongoing cost             | Exit                                | Extra yield          |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------ | ----------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Sovereign Gold Bonds     | Issue price, no making charge  | None (demat/certificate) | Secondary market or maturity 8 yr   | 2.5% annual interest |
| Gold ETF                 | Brokerage, expense ratio ~0.5% | Expense ratio            | Sell on exchange same day           | None                 |
| Gold fund of funds       | Entry load (some)              | ~0.2-0.5% over ETF       | Redeem, 1-2 days                    | None                 |
| Digital gold (app-based) | 2-3% spread + GST              | Platform spread          | Sell back at platform's price       | None                 |
| Jewellery                | 8-25% making charges + 3% GST  | Locker, insurance        | Sold at net weight × rate − melting | None                 |
| Gold coins (bank/BBPS)   | 5-10% over spot                | Storage                  | Buyer's discount applies            | None                 |

The making-charge haircut is the number jewellery buyers never compute: a ₹1,00,000 purchase with 15% making
charges holds ₹85,000 of gold value; selling at spot minus melting losses returns roughly ₹80,000-82,000 —
an immediate 18-20% haircut that gold's 8-9% annual appreciation takes two years to recover. Jewellery is
consumption with an asset's memory; sovereign bonds and ETFs are the asset itself.

## The sovereign gold bond's structure and its tax edges

SGBs pay 2.5% annual interest on the issue price (credited semi-annually, taxable at slab), are issued in
gram-denominated units at the market price, mature at eight years with a five-year exit window from year
five (interest payment dates), and — the part that changed — _capital gains on SGB maturity_ to individuals
were exempt under the 2025 Budget's changes, while earlier redemption windows and secondary-market sales
followed the standard capital-gains rules (the exemption's scope matters: check the current notification
before structuring a sale). The bond's practical superiority:

- No storage, no purity question, no locker fee.
- The 2.5% interest is a yield jewellery never had.
- Priced at the official rate, not a jeweller's spread.
- The exit window from year five means the "eight-year lock" is softer than it sounds.

The weaknesses: issuance windows (not always open), a secondary market thin enough to price at a discount
in bad weeks, and the demat-vs-certificate custody question — demat holdings transfer to nominees
mechanically; certificates do not.

## Allocation: how much gold, and in which form

Gold's role is diversification and crisis liquidity — a hedge that historically moves differently from
equity in Indian stress periods. The allocation logic:

| Portfolio stage        | Gold allocation                   | Form                                          |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Accumulating (20s-30s) | 5-10%                             | SGB/ETF, no jewellery purchases as investment |
| Established (30s-50s)  | 10-15% incl. household's existing | SGB/ETF for new money; inherited gold held    |
| Near/after retirement  | 10-15%                            | The liquidity role grows; SGB ladder          |

Household gold already held is an existing allocation — it counts in the percentage, which is exactly why
new jewellery purchases for "investment" reasons usually overshoot the class. The gift economy (weddings,
festivals) makes jewellery a cultural flow rather than an investment decision; the financial advice is not
"never buy jewellery" but "count it, and know that its return net of making charges is lower than the same
money in an SGB by a wide margin".

## The gold-loan and the monetisation routes worth knowing

The gold loan is the Indian household's cheapest accessible credit: rates at 9-12% against the ornament's
value, disbursed in hours, with the RBI's loan-to-value norms (75% for gold loans) and pledged-storage
rules. Its role in a household's financial plan is the emergency bridge — cheaper than personal loans
(13-24%) and unregulated apps (24-60%), and available against an asset the household already holds. The
conditions: the jeweller-appraised value varies by lender, the interest accrues monthly, and the released-on-
repayment receipt matters for the bureau record (a closed gold loan should show "closed", not "settled",
on the credit report).

The other routes: the Gold Monetisation Scheme (depositing idle jewellery for interest — operationally
clunky, culturally resisted, and rarely chosen), and the "sell old gold, buy SGB" rebalance when the
household's gold percentage has overshot and part of the jewellery serves no emotional purpose.

## The legality note

Gold has a compliance surface people forget: large cash purchases above ₹2 lakh attract reporting (a
jeweller's cash sale above the threshold reports to the department); the 1% TCS on cash jewellery purchases
above ₹2 lakh under Section 206C applies; and unexplained gold found in searches is a classic source question
— the purchase invoices, the inheritance documents and the gift deeds are the defence. Digital-gold platforms
are unregulated intermediaries (the SEBI-regulated alternatives are ETFs and the RBI-issued SGBs — the
[RBI](https://www.rbi.org.in/) SGB pages and
[SEBI](https://www.sebi.gov.in/) ETF disclosures are the official sources). Storing gold in bank lockers has
its own liability limits; insure standalone jewellery where the locker does not.

## The bottom line

Count the household's gold as the allocation it already is; direct new gold money to SGBs or ETFs where the
2.5% yield and the clean exit live; treat jewellery as consumption priced with its making charges; and know
the crisis-liquidity behaviours in advance, because the difference between the forms is only ever paid on
the Sunday it matters.
