The Sovereign Gold Bond was, on paper, the best gold product India ever designed: gold's price movement, plus 2.5
per cent a year of coupon, with no making charges, no GST, no storage cost and no purity risk. The Government
discontinued fresh issuances in FY 2025-26, which turned the scheme's long-term story from "should you subscribe?"
to "what do you do with the ones you hold?" — a question with a specific tax answer and a specific liquidity
answer.

## The bond's structure, as issued

SGBs were 8-year government securities denominated in grams of gold, issued at the prevailing IBJA reference
price, with:

| Feature                     | Term                                                                |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tenor                       | 8 years, exit option after year 5 on interest payment dates         |
| Coupon                      | 2.50% p.a., half-yearly, on the issue price                         |
| Denomination                | 1 gram minimum, up to 4 kg per individual per fiscal year           |
| Interest taxation           | Fully taxable at slab, as other income, each half-year              |
| Capital gains on redemption | Exempt for individuals at maturity (government assurance in budget) |
| Trading                     | Listed on NSE/BSE; secondary market sale possible anytime           |
| Loan eligibility            | Eligible as collateral (LTV per RBI norms for gilts)                |

The 2.5 per cent coupon was the product's margin over physical gold: a holder of one gram bought at ₹6,000 (the
2015-16 era issues) collected ₹150 a year while owning the gram itself. Across the scheme's life, issues priced
from ₹2,684 (2015) to ₹7,483 (early 2024 tranche) — and early subscribers ended up with bond tranches marked to
gold prices two to three times their issue price, plus eight years of coupon. The post-issue performance is the
reason the scheme developed a cult following among retail investors: it was, historically, a leveraged-feeling bet
on gold with the government paying you to hold it.

## The tax treatment, precisely

| Event                                   | Tax                                                                                                                                                              |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Half-yearly coupon                      | Slab rate, other income; TDS not deducted generally but declared in ITR                                                                                          |
| Redemption at maturity by individual    | Capital gains exempt per the government's assurance in the Finance Act (the redemption of SGB by an individual holding to maturity is not taxed as capital gain) |
| Secondary market sale before maturity   | Capital gains: STCG at slab if under 36 months; LTCG at 12.5% (post-July 2024 regime) above ₹1.25 lakh exemption, no indexation for individuals                  |
| Conversion to demat, holding across     | Continuity preserved; holding period from original allotment                                                                                                     |
| Interest on premature exit via exchange | Coupon accrued till exit paid; no penalty                                                                                                                        |

The maturity-exemption point is the single most valuable clause in the product: an individual who holds to the
8-year redemption pays no capital gains tax on the gold price appreciation. A comparable physical-gold sale after
July 2024 would face 12.5 per cent LTCG above the exemption; a gold ETF or fund, the same. On a ₹10 lakh gain,
that is a ₹1.25 lakh difference in the maturity year, per holder. Secondary-market sales before maturity lose
the exemption and fall into the ordinary capital-gains regime — which is why the tax-optimal hold is to maturity,
and the liquidity-optimal hold uses the year-5 exit option or the secondary market knowingly.

## What the discontinuation changes

The Budget for FY 2025-26 announced that the government would not issue fresh SGB tranches, citing the cost of the
coupon against the government's own borrowing programme — the scheme's economics had turned expensive as gold
prices rose, since the coupon is a fixed cash outgo and the redemption is a large one-time liability. What remains:

| Existing holder's options | Mechanics                                                                                                               |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hold to maturity          | Redemption at prevailing IBJA price; no action needed; ensure bank/RBI CSGL record is current                           |
| Early redemption window   | From year 5, on coupon payment dates, via the RBI's RBI-CSGL route or through the bank; request window opens in advance |
| Sell on exchange          | Any trading day; spreads are tight on liquid tranches, wide on old small ones                                           |
| Pledge as collateral      | LTV at gilt norms; works with most large lenders                                                                        |

For existing holders the practical instruction is unchanged from before the announcement: keep the certificate
(and the CSGL/demat record) in order, track the redemption dates (published at the RBI's press releases at
[rbi.org.in](https://www.rbi.org.in/)), and remember that the redemption credit goes to the bank account linked
to the original application. The secondary market remains liquid for the big tranches; the 2020-2022 issues
traded near spot minus a small discount, with the coupon value partially offsetting it.

## The secondary market's discount, decoded

SGB prices on exchange track gold spot plus the coupon's present value minus a liquidity/tenor discount. The
observed discount on Indian exchanges has typically been 1-4 per cent below the theoretical value, widening for
small or old tranches and narrowing for large recent ones. That discount is the exit cost for anyone who needs
liquidity before maturity — and it is also, for a buyer in the secondary market, the source of the interesting
arithmetic: buying an SGB at a 2 per cent discount to spot-plus-coupon, with 3 years to run and the maturity
exemption intact, is buying gold at a markdown with the government's coupon attached.

| Tranche situation                       | Typical secondary market pricing                    |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Large recent issue, 5-7 years remaining | 1-2% below fair value                               |
| Older small issue, 1-2 years remaining  | 0.5-1.5% below, converging to redemption            |
| Any issue in a gold drawdown            | Discount widens with sentiment; exit windows matter |

## SGB against the alternatives, once more

| Instrument               | Cost of ownership                                 | Yield                       | Tax at exit                                    |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| SGB (held to maturity)   | Zero ongoing                                      | 2.5% coupon                 | Redemption exempt (individual)                 |
| Gold ETF                 | 0.3-0.8% p.a. expense                             | None                        | LTCG 12.5% above ₹1.25 lakh                    |
| Gold fund of fund        | 0.5-1.1% (double layer)                           | None                        | LTCG rules as per fund                         |
| Physical gold            | Making charges 8-25% at purchase; storage, purity | None                        | 12.5% LTCG above ₹1.25 lakh (24-month holding) |
| Digital gold apps        | Spread of 2-6% at buy/sell                        | None                        | Treated as physical for tax                    |
| Gold monetisation scheme | Zero; interest earned                             | 2.25-2.5% on deposited gold | Interest taxed at slab                         |

The SGB remains the benchmark against which the others must justify themselves. The Gold Monetisation Scheme
comes closest on economics (a gold-linked interest payment, no price risk on the metal) but requires depositing
physical gold into the banking system; the ETF wins on convenience and SIP-ability; physical gold wins only on
cultural and collateral-in-hand grounds.

## What to do with an SGB book today

Three defensible postures. **Hold to maturity** for tranches inside five years of redemption: the exemption and
the coupon are worth more than any reinvestment you will find. **Sell into strength** for tranches whose gold
price has run far above issue price and where you have a better use for the money — the secondary market exit is
the liquidity valve, accept the 1-3 per cent discount and the capital gains tax, and stop pretending you will
"get back in lower". **Pledge, do not sell**, when the need is liquidity rather than exit: an SGB pledged at
gilt LTV norms against an overdraft keeps the coupon and the exemption while funding the need, and is among the
cheapest collateralised borrowing available to a retail holder.

The one posture that no longer makes sense is subscribing to new issues — there are none — and the one mistake
that still costs money is confusing the scheme's discontinuation with a default: every outstanding bond is a
Government of India security, coupon payments and redemptions are scheduled and backed, and the RBI's investor
pages list each tranche's dates explicitly.
