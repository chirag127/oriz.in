Fractional real estate sells a simple dream: own a slice of a Grade A office building in Bengaluru or Pune with
₹10–25 lakh instead of ₹10 crore, collect rent quarterly, and exit when the property is sold. The dream is
directionally real — the asset class exists, it is regulated, and net yields of 7–9% on office property beat
residential rental yields of 2–3%. What the pitch decks compress is the plumbing: who holds the title, what
fees come off the rent, what happens when a tenant leaves, and how you actually get out.

## The two legal wrappers

Fractional ownership in India now runs through two distinct regulatory structures, and the difference decides
everything from fee disclosure to exit.

**SM REITs (Small and Medium REITs).** SEBI's SM REIT Regulations, notified in March 2024, formalized
fractional ownership of assets worth ₹50–500 crore. The property is held in a special purpose vehicle, units
are listed on an exchange, a qualified merchant banker supervises the issue, minimum asset ownership is
prescribed, and investors hold units that look and behave like small REIT units. Sponsor compliance is
continuous — asset-level disclosure, quarterly reporting, related-party transaction limits.

**Unlisted fractional platforms.** The older model: an LLP or private limited company holds the title, investors
hold LLP shares or units, and the platform charges for sourcing, structuring, and management. These sit outside
SM REIT — some predate the regulation, some simply never registered. The regulatory comfort is thinner; your
protections come from the LLP agreement you probably did not read clause by clause.

| Structure                                          | Regulator                         | Minimum investment                               | Liquidity                 |
| -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------- |
| Listed large REIT (Embassy, Mindspace, Brookfield) | SEBI                              | ₹200–300 (unit price)                            | exchange-traded daily     |
| SM REIT                                            | SEBI                              | ₹1 crore for the sponsor; unit sizes ₹10–25 lakh | listed, thin trading      |
| Unlisted fractional platform                       | None specific (companies/LLP law) | ₹10–25 lakh typically                            | platform-run exit windows |

## The yield math, gross to net

Platforms advertise "cap rate" or "expected yield" of 8–10%. What reaches your bank account is a narrower
number. A ₹50 lakh fractional stake in a Bengaluru office asset, 9% gross cap rate, behaves like this:

| Line                                     | Amount (₹, annual) | Note                                        |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| Gross rent on ₹50 lakh stake at 9%       | 4,50,000           | cap-rate basis, full occupancy assumed      |
| Property management fee                  | 27,000–45,000      | 6–10% of collections                        |
| Asset-level maintenance / CAM shortfall  | 20,000–35,000      | common-area service charges                 |
| Platform asset management fee            | 25,000–50,000      | 0.5–1% of asset value                       |
| Vacancy risk (assumed 5% in a good year) | 22,500             | office vacancy in Indian metros runs 15–20% |
| Net to investor                          | ~2,90,000–3,40,000 | 5.8–6.8% effective                          |

That effective 6% is still respectable against residential. The point is not that fractional real estate is bad;
it is that the gap between the advertised 9% and the received 6% is where every decision should be made.

## The four risks the pitch deck buries

**Tenant concentration.** A single-tenant asset is a corporate bond with a building attached. If the anchor
tenant (often a coworking operator subleasing to startups) exits, rent goes to zero until re-leasing — and
Indian office markets can take 9–18 months to re-lease a floor. Ask for the tenant's lease expiry schedule and
the lock-in remaining, not the current occupancy.

**Exit is the product, not the rent.** Most fractional pitches quietly assume the real return is capital
appreciation on sale in 3–5 years. But there is no market maker. Platform-run exit windows let sellers list
units at a discount, and buyers of ₹25 lakh slivers of a leased office building are rare. SM REIT units at least
have an exchange listing; unlisted LLP stakes can be illiquid for years.

**Title and structuring risk.** The SPV holds the title. Verify that the SPV's shareholding is genuinely
clean — the platform's own litigation, a lender's charge on the SPV, or a promoter dispute upstream can impair
your claim even though _your_ slice of the building is fine. REITs publish diligence documents; unlisted
platforms often do not.

**Development-stage masquerading as income-stage.** Some "fractional" offerings are actually pre-launch real
estate in fractional wrapping: the rent is projected, the building is not finished. The risk profile is
development, not income — a different asset class wearing the same clothes.

## Tax and the regulatory floor

Rental income from a fractional stake is taxed as "income from house property" at slab rates, after a standard
30% deduction on the underlying property's rental income (Section 24(a)). On sale, capital gains tax applies at
the usual real-estate rates — 12.5% without indexation (or 20% with indexation for property acquired before
July 2024, at the option of the taxpayer where available) for long-term gains. Unlike stocks, there is no
₹1.25 lakh exemption; unlike residential property, Section 54 exemptions do not apply to commercial assets.

Regulatorily, the floor is set by SEBI's SM REIT framework and the existing REIT regime, which you can read at
[sebi.gov.in](https://www.sebi.gov.in/).
For unlisted platforms, the honest answer is that SEBI has no direct oversight; your protections are contract
law and whatever disclosure the platform volunteers.

## The legality note

Fractional ownership through SM REITs is fully legal and regulated. Unlisted fractional platforms are legal
too — they operate as LLPs and private companies under the Companies Act and LLP Act, and [mca.gov.in](https://www.mca.gov.in)
shows every SPV's filings. The grey zone is conduct, not structure: platforms that guarantee returns, pool money
into collective investment schemes without SEBI registration, or promise buy-backs they cannot fund have faced
regulatory action under CIS norms. Consequences of operating an unregistered collective scheme include
deregistration, refund orders with interest, and prosecution of the platform's officers under Section 44 of the
SEBI Act. As an investor your exposure is not prosecution but recovery risk — an unregistered platform that
collapses leaves you inside a long insolvency queue. Verify the SPV's filings, insist on SM REIT or listed REIT
wrappers for sizeable allocations, and treat a guaranteed-yield pitch as a red flag rather than a feature.

## The bottom line

Fractional real estate earns its place in a portfolio as a **yield** asset, not a growth one: an effective
5.5–7% net rental yield on Grade A commercial, with an illiquid exit and a tenant-concentration tail. If you
want real estate exposure with daily liquidity and ₹500 of entry cost, the listed large REITs already do it —
at lower fees and with audited disclosure. Fractional platforms earn their premium only when the specific asset
is materially better than what listed REITs own: newer building, longer lease, higher-quality tenant, and a
structurally lower cap rate entry than the listed market prices. Choose on those terms and skip the rest.
