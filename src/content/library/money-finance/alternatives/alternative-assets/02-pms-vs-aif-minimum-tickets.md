Portfolio Management Services and Alternative Investment Funds both promise professionally managed money beyond
mutual funds, both are SEBI-regulated, and both require money most investors do not have. They are not
interchangeable. The differences — who owns the securities, how fees are charged, what gets disclosed, and how
gains are taxed — change outcomes more than the manager's skill does.

## The structural difference that decides everything

In a **PMS**, the securities are held in _your_ demat account. The manager executes on each client account
separately; you own the individual stocks directly. In an **AIF**, your money is pooled into a trust or LLP
whose units you hold; the fund owns the securities, you own units.

This single difference cascades everywhere:

| Feature                       | PMS                                           | AIF (Category I/II)                           | AIF (Category III)                                 |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Ownership of securities       | Direct, in your demat                         | Units of a pooled trust                       | Units of a pooled trust                            |
| Minimum investment            | ₹50 lakh (SEBI floor)                         | ₹1 crore                                      | ₹1 crore                                           |
| Minimum ticket per fund offer | ₹50 lakh                                      | ₹1 crore                                      | ₹1 crore                                           |
| Manager's fee                 | Fixed + share of AUM or profits               | Management fee + carry                        | Management fee + carry                             |
| Taxation of gains             | At your slab / capital gains on your holdings | Pass-through where available, else fund-level | Fund-level, often slab-equivalent at trustee level |
| Liquidity                     | Weekly to monthly, at NAV or per contract     | Lock-in per fund deed, often 3+ years         | Per fund deed, variable                            |
| Disclosure                    | Client-level portfolio statement              | Fund-level reports; limited look-through      | Fund-level, performance net of fees                |

SEBI's PMS regulations require a minimum investment of ₹50 lakh per client, and the AIF Regulations set ₹1
crore — both floors were raised from ₹25 lakh in SEBI's 2019–2020 review. The thresholds are not marketing;
they are the regulator's own statement that these products are for people who can absorb concentrated
professional-management fees on a large base.

## The fee stack, netted out

PMS fees are more visible than AIF fees because they are charged per account. A typical discretionary equity
PMS charges a fixed fee of 1–2.5% of AUM plus 10–20% of gains above a hurdle, and the arithmetic is directly
visible on your statement. AIF fees look similar at the fund level but are compounded by _portfolio-level_
costs the fund pays inside: broker commissions, STT, and in Category III, financing costs on any leverage.

| Cost layer                              | PMS                      | Category II AIF                  | Category III AIF   |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------ | -------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| Management fee                          | 1–2.5% p.a.              | 1.5–2% p.a.                      | 1.5–2.5% p.a.      |
| Performance fee                         | 10–20% over hurdle       | 10–20% over hurdle               | 15–25% over hurdle |
| Brokerage and transaction costs         | Passed to client account | Fund pays from NAV               | Fund pays from NAV |
| Set-up / demat / custodian              | On your account          | Trustee and custodian, from fund | Same               |
| Effective drag on ₹1 crore over 5 years | ~8–12% of AUM            | ~9–13% of NAV                    | ~10–15% of NAV     |

The drag column is the one to stare at. A PMS or AIF has to outperform a low-cost index fund by roughly 1.5–2.5
percentage points a year just to break even after fees — before any claim of skill is even tested. That is
achievable for genuinely skilled managers; it is not automatic, and the industry's own performance dispersion
shows it.

## What each product is actually for

PMS suits an investor who wants direct stock ownership with professional discretion, is comfortable seeing every
trade on their own statement, and wants the tax flexibility of choosing _which_ shares to sell — because the
securities are theirs, they can harvest gains and losses at the account level. AIF Category I and II suit
investors who want exposure to private credit, distressed assets, or early-stage equity — areas a demat
account cannot reach — and accept illiquidity for it. Category III AIFs run long-short and tactical strategies
with leverage; they are closest to hedge funds and are the most fee-intensive.

| Investor need                                | Better fit        | Reason                             |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Professional stock picking, direct ownership | PMS               | Individual account, visible trades |
| Private credit / pre-IPO / distressed        | Category II AIF   | Access requires pooled structure   |
| Long-short, market-neutral strategies        | Category III AIF  | Leverage and shorting permitted    |
| Small ticket, daily liquidity, low cost      | Index mutual fund | None of the above thresholds apply |

The last row is the honest default for most investors below the ₹50 lakh threshold — and above it too, unless
they specifically want what PMS/AIF offers. A ₹50 lakh requirement is the _minimum_, not a quality certificate.

## Disclosure, and the performance reporting trap

PMS reporting is client-level and relatively standardized: your statement shows holdings, realized and
unrealized gains, and fee deductions. AIF reporting varies more by fund deed — some publish quarterly NAV and
portfolio summary, some disclose position-level detail to investors only. The performance trap is in the
_baseline_: PMS performance is often shown from the manager's model portfolio inception date rather than the
date your money entered, and AIF performance is often shown net of fees for a vintage year that closed before
the current market cycle. Ask for the performance _since your own investment date_, net of all fees, and
compare it against the same period for a comparable index.

## The legality note

Both PMS and AIF are fully regulated — PMS under SEBI (Portfolio Managers) Regulations, 2020, AIF under SEBI
(AIF) Regulations, 2012, with the 2024 amendments co-investment framework. Verify a manager's registration
number on [sebi.gov.in](https://www.sebi.gov.in) before signing anything; unregistered portfolio managers
operating outside the framework have historically been the source of most "guaranteed return" wealth-management
frauds. Consequences of running an unregistered PMS include SEBI deregistration orders, disgorgement of fees
with interest, refund directions and prosecution of the principals under Section 24 of the SEBI Act; accepting
money without registration is an offence regardless of whether performance was honest. For investors, the grey
zone is mis-selling rather than structure: a distributor describing a Category III AIF as "safe fixed-income"
is a sales problem the regulator will not fix for you. Read the fund deed's fee clause and the side-pocket
provision yourself — those two pages decide more of your outcome than the pitch.

## The bottom line

PMS and AIF are legitimate structures with real regulatory protection and, in specific strategies, real value.
The discipline is entry-level: do not enter because the minimum says you are rich enough; enter because the
strategy — private credit, distressed, long-short, concentrated stock picking — cannot be reached any cheaper,
and because the manager's net-of-fee track record survives your scrutiny across a full market cycle. If neither
condition holds, the ₹50 lakh minimum is better deployed across an index core and a few direct bets, at a fee
drag the alternatives do not impose.
