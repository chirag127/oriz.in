Whisky cask and fine-wine investing arrived in India as the plausible face of alternative assets: a physical
thing, centuries of history, and returns that headline well. The asset is real. So is the fee structure, the
illiquidity, and the gap between the brochure's compounding chart and what a cask actually sells for when _you_
need to sell. This article is the arithmetic of the asset class as an Indian investor meets it.

## What you actually own

Buy a cask through a platform and you own — depending on the platform's structure — either the physical cask
itself (documented by a delivery order or warehouse receipt from a bonded warehouse) or units in an entity that
holds it. The cask sits in a bonded warehouse in Scotland, the US, Japan or, for Indian players, in domestic
bonded facilities. Bonded means duty is unpaid until the whisky is bottled and removed; the storage facility,
not the platform, is your custodian of record.

Wine works the same way with a different custodian: fine wine is held in bonded cellars in the UK or France, with
provenance records (château, vintage, storage history) doing the work that a warehouse receipt does for a cask.

| Element                  | Whisky cask                        | Fine wine                        |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Minimum entry in India   | ₹35,000–1,00,000                   | ₹25,000–75,000                   |
| Physical custody         | Bonded warehouse, duty unpaid      | Bonded cellar, duty unpaid       |
| Annual storage/insurance | 1–2% of value                      | 1–2.5% of value                  |
| Exit route               | Bottling, auction, platform resale | Auction, merchant resale         |
| Indian tax on sale       | Capital gains on a capital asset   | Capital gains on a capital asset |
| Dividend-like income     | None                               | None                             |

## The return arithmetic, gross to net

The headline number in whisky marketing is usually the compound growth of rare-cask indices over the last
decade — numbers in the 8–15% p.a. range. Three adjustments cut it.

**Storage and insurance.** 1–2% a year, deducted from value or paid in cash, comes straight off the return.
**Platform spread.** The platform buys the cask at trade price and sells it to you at a retail markup of
10–25%; that markup is your instant drawdown, and you recover it only if the underlying appreciates more than
the markup plus fees. **Exit costs.** Auction commissions run 10–15% on the buyer and seller sides combined,
and a bottling exit requires duty, bottling, shipping and excise paperwork before the whisky is saleable in
India.

| Scenario                                     | Gross growth p.a. | Fees and spread           | Net to investor p.a. |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Rare single malt, 10-year hold, auction exit | 12%               | ~2.5% per year equivalent | ~9.5%                |
| Standard cask, platform resale exit          | 8%                | ~3.5% per year equivalent | ~4.5%                |
| Young cask, 5-year hold, bottling exit       | 6%                | ~4% per year equivalent   | ~2%                  |

The rare-cask market genuinely has produced high returns — the auction indices for Japanese and closed-distillery
malts rose dramatically through 2010–2022. But the indices track _rare_ casks, and the casks platforms sell to
retail investors at ₹40,000 are usually standard production from operating distilleries, whose appreciation
tracks the ordinary market much more closely.

## The three structural risks

**The market can fall, and has.** Whisky auction indices peaked in 2022–23 and corrected significantly through
2023–25 as speculative demand faded; the Macallan and Japanese indices fell 20–40% from peak. A 10-year holding
period is not a guarantee, it is a hope. **The exit is the asset.** A cask has no rent, no coupon, no
dividend — every rupee of return comes from a future buyer agreeing with your valuation. That makes the asset
class behave like collectibles, not like productive property. **The platform is the counterparty.** If the
platform goes insolvent, your claim is against the warehouse receipt or the SPV holding the cask, and the
strength of that claim depends on documents most investors never read. Ask specifically: is the delivery order
in my name, is the warehouse's storage contract with me or with the platform, and who pays the duty when the
cask leaves the warehouse.

## The India-specific picture

Importing a cask into India involves customs duty on bottled spirits, which can add materially to the cost when
the whisky is bottled and shipped. Some platforms structure ownership to stay in bonded storage abroad, with
the investor holding a document rather than the liquid — which is legal but makes the asset a paper claim on a
Scottish warehouse, not a bottle in your cupboard. Verify which structure you are buying.

Tax-wise, the cask or wine case is a capital asset; gains on sale after 24 months are long-term at 12.5% without
indexation under the post-July 2024 rules, and gains within 24 months are taxed at slab. The platforms' own
fee income is GST-able, and the storage fees you pay are not deductible against your eventual gain.

Regulatory context: whisky and wine platforms are not individually licensed as investment intermediaries; some
operate as crowdfunding or marketplace entities under company law. SEBI's [collective investment scheme
framework](https://www.sebi.gov.in) governs pooled investment structures, and the boundary between a
"marketplace for physical assets" and a "collective investment scheme" is where several platforms operate.
The asset itself is legal; the wrapper deserves your scrutiny.

## The legality note

Buying and holding whisky casks or fine wine is legal in India for personal investment. Three legal notes
matter. First, alcohol is a state subject — selling whisky _within_ India, even yours, requires the state's
excise permits, which is why platforms route exits through auctions or bottling-and-export rather than a
domestic resale market. Second, the grey zone is the platform wrapper: a platform pooling money into a common
enterprise with promised returns edges towards an unregistered collective investment scheme, and consequences
of operating one include SEBI refund orders with interest, deregistration, and prosecution of the platform's
officers under Sections 11AA and 44 of the SEBI Act. Third, claiming the asset is "SEBI-registered" when the
registration belongs to a different entity or product is misrepresentation — a consumer complaint with
[consumerhelpline.gov.in](https://consumerhelpline.gov.in) or SEBI's SCORES portal is the remedy. Read the
warehouse receipt and the platform's terms before funding; the documents are the asset.

## The bottom line

Whisky and wine are legitimate collectibles with a genuine market, and a bad substitute for productive assets.
The correct mental model: a small allocation (under 5% of the portfolio), bought for the underlying asset's
quality — a specific distillery, a specific château vintage with provenance — not for the platform's projected
compounding chart. Insist on documents in your name, understand the exit costs before entry, and assume that
the realistic net return on standard casks is single-digit, not the index-quoted double-digit headline. If a
platform promises a fixed return on a physical cask, the return is being funded by something other than the
whisky — and that is the moment to walk away.
