The Liberalised Remittance Scheme is the pipe through which resident Indians send money abroad — for a child's
education, a relative's gift, foreign stocks, or a deposit in an overseas account. The scheme allows up to
USD 250,000 per person per financial year, and its rules decide what you can buy, what tax is collected at
source, and what forms the bank files. Used properly it is uneventful; used carelessly it generates FEMA
notices.

## The limit and how it is counted

USD 250,000 per financial year (April to March), per individual, including minors — a family of four has a
combined USD 1 million ceiling, and there is no carry-forward of unused limit. The limit covers the _purpose_
sub-ceiling splits the scheme imposes:

| Purpose                                   | Sub-limit within USD 250,000 | Notes                                  |
| ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Private travel                            | USD 250,000                  | Included in the overall limit          |
| Education                                 | USD 250,000                  | Tuition and living; proof of admission |
| Medical treatment                         | USD 250,000                  | Estimates from the hospital            |
| Gifts and donations                       | USD 250,000                  | Combined ceiling                       |
| Investment in foreign shares, ETFs, funds | USD 250,000                  | Through LRS route                      |
| Purchase of immovable property abroad     | USD 250,000                  | Direct or through LRS                  |
| Maintenance of close relatives            | USD 250,000                  | Standard purpose codes                 |

The sub-limits are cumulative within the single USD 250,000 envelope — USD 100,000 of foreign stock purchases
plus USD 150,000 of education remittances exhausts the year. Emigration remittances (settling abroad) are
outside the LRS envelope and use separate procedures under FEMA's emigration provisions.

## What TCS does to the flow

Since October 2023, tax collected at source under Section 206C(1G) applies to LRS remittances above the
threshold: 5% on remittances above ₹7 lakh (for education and medical purposes, on the amount exceeding ₹7
lakh), and 20% on other purposes — travel, gifts, investments — above the ₹7 lakh threshold. The TCS is not
a cost: it is credit you claim when filing your return, or adjust against the tax deducted elsewhere. But it
is _cash flow out_, in the same remittance, and it changes the arithmetic of small recurring investments.

| Remittance purpose        | Amount in FY | TCS rate           | TCS amount |
| ------------------------- | ------------ | ------------------ | ---------- |
| Education (loan-funded)   | ₹20,00,000   | 0.5% above ₹7 lakh | ₹6,500     |
| Education (self-funded)   | ₹20,00,000   | 5% above ₹7 lakh   | ₹65,000    |
| Foreign equity investment | ₹30,00,000   | 20% above ₹7 lakh  | ₹4,60,000  |
| Family gift               | ₹15,00,000   | 20% above ₹7 lakh  | ₹1,60,000  |

A ₹30 lakh investment remittance carries ₹4.6 lakh of TCS _in the year of remittance_ — recoverable at
filing, but it ties up the cash until the refund arrives, and if your total tax liability is otherwise low,
the recovery is a refund cycle, not an adjustment. Investors who dollar-cost-average monthly through LRS
routinely hit the ₹7 lakh threshold by August; the TCS then applies to every subsequent instalment.

## What LRS permits and what it does not

The scheme's permitted list is wide — foreign shares, ETFs, bonds, bank deposits abroad, property, travel,
gifts — with hard prohibitions that catch people out:

- No remittance for **margins or leveraged derivatives** on overseas exchanges; buying foreign equity is
  permitted, margin trading is not.
- No remittance to **countries flagged by FATF** as high-risk, and no remittances to Nepal or Bhutan for
  capital-account purchases (they have separate arrangements).
- No use of **another person's LRS limit** — each individual remits under their own entitlement; routing a
  spouse's investment through your limit misstates both returns.
- No **Lottery winnings, visa fees for work abroad or sale of assets abroad** through LRS — those have their
  own current-account and capital-account routes under FEMA.
- **Round-tripping** — sending money out under LRS and investing it back into India through a foreign entity
  to disguise the source — is a prohibited use and is specifically watched.

The banking mechanics are forms A2 (the application for remittance) and the purpose-code declaration, with
the bank filing the relevant returns to RBI. The [RBI's LRS master direction](https://www.rbi.org.in) is the
authoritative text, and it is amended more often than most people re-read it — the ₹7 lakh TCS threshold and
the 20% rate were both introduced after 2023, which is why pre-2023 blog advice is now wrong on the tax line.

## Investment via LRS: the practical architecture

Buying foreign stocks or ETFs through a broker requires the funds to move under LRS with the correct purpose
code ("investment in shares/securities abroad"). The mechanics that matter:

1. **The remittance is from your own account to your own overseas broker account** — third-party funding is
   not permitted under LRS for investment purposes.
2. **The USD 250,000 limit is per person, per FY** — a couple can double the envelope by remitting separately,
   but not by one spouse remitting on the other's behalf.
3. **Gains and dividends are taxable in India** as resident income; the broker's country may withhold tax on
   dividends (the US at 25% under the India-US treaty with a W-8BEN on file), and the treaty credit needs
   Form 67 filed before the ITR.
4. **The TCS credit** appears in your Form 26AS via the bank's filing — reconcile it before filing, because
   the bank's TCS return is what the department matches, not your bank statement.

| Step       | Document                   | Failure mode if skipped                   |
| ---------- | -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Remittance | Form A2 with purpose code  | Bank rejects or misclassifies the purpose |
| Broker KYC | W-8BEN (US)                | 30% dividend withholding instead of 25%   |
| Tax credit | Form 26AS reconciliation   | TCS not claimed, refund lost              |
| Filing     | Form 67 for treaty credits | Foreign tax credit denied for the year    |

## The legality note

LRS is a fully legal, RBI-administered route. The grey and illegal zones are about _purpose and source_:
remitting for a prohibited purpose under a wrong purpose code, splitting one remittance across family members
to skirt sub-limits, or sending money abroad from undisclosed income sources. Consequences of FEMA
contraventions are quantified by the Reserve Bank's adjudication framework — penalties that can extend up to
three times the amount involved in certain contraventions, compounding fees when the violation is regularized,
and in persistent or serious cases, enforcement proceedings. The department also cross-matches LRS remittances
against ITRs: a remittance of ₹40 lakh on a return showing ₹12 lakh of income is a routine enquiry trigger.
Keep the source-of-funds documentation — tax returns, sale deeds, gift deeds — with the remittance file, not
scrambled for at enquiry time.

## The bottom line

LRS does what it promises: USD 250,000 a year, per person, for a wide list of legitimate purposes. The two
things that trip people are the TCS cash-flow bite above ₹7 lakh and the purpose-code discipline — both are
solvable by planning the remittance calendar (use April–June for the year's big education payments, spread
investment remittances to control TCS timing) and by reading the current master direction rather than a
two-year-old blog. The scheme is generous; the paperwork is what keeps it that way.
