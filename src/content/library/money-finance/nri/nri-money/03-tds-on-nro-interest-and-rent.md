An NRI renting out a Bengaluru flat or holding NRO fixed deposits meets Indian tax as a _deduction at source_
problem before it is ever a filing problem. Banks deduct TDS on NRO interest; tenants deduct it on rent. The
domestic rates are high — 30% plus cess on interest, and a slab-equivalent regime on rent — and the treaty
rate is lower, but only when the paperwork exists before the payment, not after.

## Interest: Section 195 and the 30% default

Interest paid to a non-resident falls under Section 195: the payer (the bank) must deduct tax at the rate
applicable to the income, and for NRO interest that is 30% plus 4% cess, effectively 31.2%. The Reserve Bank's
[NRI account guidance](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/FAQView.aspx?Id=65) confirms the machinery. Banks
apply it monthly on accrual, not annually — a ₹10 lakh NRO FD at 7% sees ₹2,184 deducted every month on the
₹7,000 of interest it accrues.

| Interest income (₹/year) | TDS deducted (30% + 4%) | Net received |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------- | ------------ |
| 2,00,000                 | 62,400                  | 1,37,600     |
| 7,00,000                 | 2,18,400                | 4,81,600     |
| 15,00,000                | 4,68,000                | 10,32,000    |

No basic exemption protection applies at the bank's counter. If your only Indian income is ₹4 lakh of NRO
interest, the bank still deducts 30% — you recover the excess through filing a return, because your total tax
on ₹4 lakh under the new regime is zero. The recovery is real but slow, and it is why Form 15G/15H and treaty
paperwork matter more for NRIs than for residents.

## Rent: Section 195's slab-equivalent mechanism

Rent paid to a non-resident is TDS-deductible under Section 195 at the rate applicable to the _income_ —
which means the tenant computes the owner's notional slab and deducts accordingly, a mechanism most tenants
get wrong. The practical fix is a certificate: the NRI owner files Form 13 with the jurisdictional officer
for a lower-deduction certificate, which instructs the tenant on a specific rate.

| Monthly rent (₹) | Tenant's typical deduction                    | Notes                                                     |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| 40,000           | ₹480 (15%, if computed with deduction u/s 24) | With 30% standard deduction applied to compute net income |
| 1,00,000         | ₹1,200–3,000                                  | Varies with the owner's notional slab                     |
| 2,50,000         | ₹7,500–9,375                                  | Higher slabs; certificate cleans this up                  |

The rate confusion is real: Section 195 does not prescribe a flat rate on rent; it prescribes the rate
applicable to the income after allowable deductions (including the 30% standard deduction under Section
24(a) for let-out property). Tenants who deduct 30% of the _gross_ rent over-deduct massively. A Form 13
certificate stating "deduct at 15% of net" or whatever the correct figure is resolves it — the certificate
is issued to the _payer_, and the tenant's compliance risk disappears.

## The DTAA rate: TRC, Form 10F and the bank's timing

The treaty rate on interest is usually 10–15% depending on the country — India-US is 15% (with carve-outs for
bank lending), India-UAE 12.5%, India-Singapore 15%, India-UK 15%. To apply it, the bank wants three documents
**before** the interest credit: a Tax Residency Certificate from the foreign country, Form 10F (electronically
generated on the income-tax portal once you have a PAN), and a no-PE declaration or the treaty-specific
beneficiary form some banks require.

| Country              | Treaty rate on interest (typical) | Rate on rent under Article 6/7 pathway           |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| United States        | 15%                               | Business/property income rules, often protective |
| United Arab Emirates | 12.5%                             | Property income taxable in India, relief abroad  |
| Singapore            | 15%                               | Same                                             |
| United Kingdom       | 15%                               | Same                                             |

The sequence matters. A bank that receives the TRC in March applies the treaty rate from the _next_ payment;
TDS already deducted at 30% is recoverable only by filing. If your treaty entitlement is real, the
administrative order is: PAN first, TRC second, Form 10F third, bank mandate fourth, then the first interest
credit. Every step done late costs a year of refunds.

## The refund machinery, when TDS has over-deducted

An NRI's ITR is where excess TDS comes home. File ITR-2 (NRO interest and rent with TDS credits cannot go on
ITR-1), reconcile every deduction against Form 26AS and the AIS, and claim the refund of the excess. The
processing timeline is weeks to months; the refund lands with interest under Section 244A at 0.5% per month
from the due date of filing in cases where the assessment is delayed beyond the statutory window.

Two friction points are specific to NRIs. First, the refund requires a pre-validated bank account on the
portal — an NRO account with the IFSC correctly mapped, and the PAN linked; refunds to foreign accounts are
possible through specific request routes but slower. Second, if the return is filed late, the refund loses
its interest accrual for the delay period — and in some cases the late-filing fee under Section 234F (₹5,000
where income exceeds ₹5 lakh) applies on top.

## Practical structure for an NRI with rent and deposits

The clean configuration for an NRI with an Indian property and NRO deposits:

1. **Form 13 certificate** for rent, instructing the tenant on the correct net-of-deductions rate.
2. **TRC + Form 10F + bank mandate** filed with the bank before the first interest accrual, locking the
   treaty rate on NRO interest (typically 15% instead of 31.2%).
3. **Form 15CA/CB** for any remittance of that rent or interest abroad, within the USD 1 million NRO limit.
4. **ITR-2 every year** even when the total tax is nil, because the refund exists only through filing.

| Step                                           | Saves per year (illustrative, ₹10L interest + ₹12L rent) |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Treaty rate on interest (15% vs 31.2%)         | ₹1,62,000                                                |
| Form 13 certificate on rent (correct net rate) | ₹50,000–90,000                                           |
| Filing the return to claim residual refunds    | ₹10,000–40,000                                           |

## The legality note

The TDS machinery is compliance law, not advisory: a payer who fails to deduct faces disallowance and interest,
and the NRI's obligation is to file and reconcile regardless of what the bank got wrong. The grey zones are
misuse of the treaty — claiming resident status of a treaty country you are not actually resident in, or
filing a no-PE declaration that is false. Consequences of treaty misuse are concrete: the department can deny
the benefit, raise demand for the shortfall with interest under Sections 234B and 234C, and levy penalty under
Section 270A for mis-declaration; deliberate false certification of residency has been treated as
misrepresentation attracting prosecution under Section 277 of the Income-tax Act in serious cases. The clean
path costs one courier: keep the TRC current for each year the treaty is claimed, because a TRC for FY 2024-25
does not cover FY 2026-27.

## The bottom line

The 31.2% default on NRO interest and the confusion around NRO rent are both _paperwork-solvable_. Treaty
rates exist precisely for this, and the three documents that unlock them are cheap: TRC from your country of
residence, Form 10F from the portal, and a bank mandate filed before the first accrual. Do the sequence once,
renew the TRC annually, and the deduction machinery works for you instead of against you — leaving the refund
season for the years the bank still gets it wrong.
