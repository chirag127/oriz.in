Residency is the hinge every NRI tax decision swings on. It decides whether your foreign salary is taxable in
India, whether your Indian interest escapes, and whether the RNOR window — the most under-used transition
status in the Act — is available to you. This article maps Section 6 of the Income-tax Act to real moving
dates, and shows where the status changes cost money.

## The three statuses and how the days are counted

India's tax residency is a day-count test on _physical presence_. For any financial year (April to March), you
are a **resident** if you are in India for 182 days or more, _or_ if you are here for 60 days or more _and_
you were here for 365 days or more across the preceding four years. The 60-day limb is relaxed to 182 days for
Indian citizens leaving for employment abroad and for crew of Indian ships — which is why a short-visit
returning expat can tip into residency on a technicality the 182-day rule alone would not trigger.

If resident, the **resident-but-not-ordinarily-resident (RNOR)** test applies: you are RNOR if you have been
non-resident in nine of the preceding ten years, _or_ you have been in India for 729 days or less in the
preceding seven years. Otherwise you are **ordinarily resident (ROR)** — full worldwide taxation.

| Status            | Indian-source income | Foreign income (received abroad)                               | Foreign income (received in India) |
| ----------------- | -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Non-resident (NR) | Taxable              | Not taxable                                                    | Taxable                            |
| RNOR              | Taxable              | Not taxable, except income from a business controlled in India | Taxable                            |
| ROR               | Taxable              | Taxable, worldwide                                             | Taxable                            |

The RNOR window is what a returning NRI gets: typically two to three financial years after a long posting
abroad, during which foreign income stays outside India's tax net. A returning professional with a nine-year
Dubai stint, foreign deposits and a retained foreign employer pension can structure the return so foreign
receipts arrive during RNOR years and Indian-sourced income is what the return declares.

## The deemed-residency rule for high earners

Section 6(1A) deems an Indian citizen to be a resident if their India-derived income exceeds ₹15 lakh and
they are _not liable to tax_ in any other country — the so-called "stateless" rule, aimed at citizens who
live nowhere for tax purposes. It does **not** catch citizens who pay tax abroad, even if they spend few days
in India. The carve-outs matter: a UAE-based professional with no personal income tax there but genuine
residency and employment income there can still trip the "not liable to tax" phrase, because _not liable_ is
about liability, not payment. Practitioners treat the rule narrowly; the department has rarely had to enforce
it, but the paperwork — residency certificates from the foreign country — is the defence.

## The income that follows you across statuses

Two categories behave differently on the way back.

**Interest on NRE and FCNR deposits.** While you are non-resident, NRE and FCNR interest is exempt. Once you
become resident, the exemption on FCNR deposits survives until maturity (and under the current framework, NRE
interest continues to be exempt until the account is redesignated); after that, interest is taxed at slab.
Plan the deposit maturities against the RNOR window — an FCNR booked early in a foreign posting, maturing in
RNOR year two, captures the exemption for the full run.

**Foreign retirement accounts.** A US 401(k), UK pension or Singapore CPF is foreign income. As NR and RNOR,
its growth and withdrawals stay out of the Indian tax net; as ROR, they become taxable in India, with foreign
tax credits under the treaty and Form 67 for claiming them. The RNOR-to-ROR transition is the moment a 401(k)
balloon enters the Indian net — a reason to sequence withdrawals into RNOR years where the amounts are large.

| Account type                         | While NR             | While RNOR                          | Once ROR                              |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| NRE / FCNR deposits                  | Interest exempt      | Exempt until redesignation/maturity | Taxable at slab (FCNR until maturity) |
| Foreign pension and 401(k)           | Not taxable in India | Not taxable in India                | Taxable; treaty credits apply         |
| Indian rent, dividends, NRO interest | Taxable              | Taxable                             | Taxable                               |
| Capital gains on Indian assets       | Taxable              | Taxable                             | Taxable                               |

## The DTAA and Form 67 mechanics

When foreign income enters the Indian net, relief comes through the treaty. You need the foreign country's tax
residency certificate, Form 10F (now fillable electronically on the income-tax portal), and the treaty article
that governs the income type — salaries under Article 16, pensions under Article 18, interest under Article 11.
Form 67 must be filed before the ITR to claim foreign tax credits; missing it forfeits the credit for the
year even when the underlying treaty entitlement is clear. The [income-tax portal](https://incometax.gov.in)
hosts the current Form 10F and 67 workflows and the notified treaty texts.

## Common situations, worked

**The 180-day remote worker.** A professional employed abroad, paid abroad, but physically in India for 190
days in FY 2025-26 becomes resident. Their foreign salary is taxable in India unless the treaty's dependent
personal services article shifts the taxation abroad — which requires showing the employer is foreign, the
salary is deducted abroad, and a permanent establishment does not exist in India. Days planned carelessly
create a filing obligation in two countries for the same salary.

**The returning retiree.** Twelve years in Singapore, returning with CPF and foreign rental income. RNOR
status applies for two years if the 9-out-of-10 test is met. Rental income from the Singapore flat during
RNOR years is not Indian-taxed; from ROR year one it is, with Singapore credits under Article 13 of the
India-Singapore treaty. The sequence — withdrawal before ROR year one — is worth real money.

**The crew member.** Shipping employment trips the special 182-day rule; a sailor on an Indian vessel who
mistakenly counts days under the 60-day limb becomes resident when the certificate says otherwise.

## The legality note

Residency status is a legal classification with consequences, not an opinion. Getting it wrong in the
_expensive_ direction — claiming non-residency while living in India beyond the day counts — is a misstatement
on the return. Consequences of a wrong residency claim are the standard assessment machinery: reassessment
under Section 147 when the department's information (TDS filings, travel data, AEOI bank data) contradicts the
claim, tax on the omitted income with interest under Sections 234A/B/C, and penalty up to 200% of the tax
under Section 270A for under-reporting. The Automated Exchange of Information framework means foreign bank
balances are visible to the department; the "they will not find my offshore account" era is over. The clean
path is a day-count log and a status memo each April, filed with the ITR papers.

## The bottom line

Track your days like a compliance calendar, because the statute does. Before a move, plan the deposit mix —
NRE/FCNR against future residency, NRO for Indian income — and time the big foreign receipts into RNOR years
where they exist. The two-year RNOR window after a long posting is the single most valuable tax-planning asset
a returning NRI has, and it is only usable if you know it exists before the first ROR return is due.
