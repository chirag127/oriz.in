The NRE/NRO distinction is the first thing every NRI gets wrong and the last thing anyone explains properly.
Both are rupee accounts for non-residents, both look identical on a bank app, and they differ in exactly three
ways — the currency the money comes from, the tax on interest, and whether you can take it out of India. Every
NRI banking mistake traces back to missing one of the three.

## The definitions, without the jargon

An **NRE account** holds foreign earnings. You fund it from your salary abroad, and the money stays in rupees
inside India but remains freely repatriable — principal and interest both move out without permission or limit.
An **NRO account** holds India-earned money: rent from the Mumbai flat, dividends, a pension, proceeds of a
sale of property bought while resident, interest on deposits that predate your move. Its outflows abroad are
capped — up to USD 1 million per financial year, per the FEMA current-account rules, with documentation.

| Feature                | NRE account                                                              | NRO account                                                   |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Source of funds        | Foreign income only                                                      | Indian income only                                            |
| Income-tax on interest | Exempt in India                                                          | 30% slab-equivalent plus cess, TDS 30%                        |
| Repatriation           | Fully, principal and interest                                            | Up to USD 1 million per FY with forms                         |
| Joint holding          | Only with another NRI (close relative as second holder on some products) | With a resident close relative, on 'former or survivor' basis |
| Deposit types          | Savings, current, FD, RFC-linked                                         | Savings, current, FD                                          |
| Exchange-rate risk     | Yes — INR balances, foreign funding                                      | Yes — INR balances, Indian funding                            |

A resident close relative can be a second holder on an NRO account — spouse, parent, sibling on a
former-or-survivor basis — but cannot operate an NRE account jointly except with another NRI. Banks enforce
this at opening; the errors surface later when the resident spouse tries to sign on an NRE FD.

## The tax difference that changes everything

NRO interest is taxed in India at 30% plus the applicable cess under Section 115A — not at slab rates. On
₹10 lakh of NRO fixed-deposit interest, the bank deducts 30% TDS (plus 4% cess at payment under Section 195's
machinery), leaving you ₹6.96 lakh before you have filed anything. NRE interest is exempt from Indian income
tax for non-residents not ordinarily liable, and banks do not deduct TDS on NRE deposits for NRI customers
who maintain their status.

| Interest earned (₹) | NRE account: tax | NRO account: TDS and tax       |
| ------------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------ |
| 2,00,000            | Nil              | ₹61,200 deducted at 30% + cess |
| 6,00,000            | Nil              | ₹1,83,600 deducted             |
| 12,00,000           | Nil              | ₹3,67,200 deducted             |

The NRO number is harsher than it needs to be in two situations. If your total Indian income is below the
basic exemption limit, the TDS still happens; you file a return and claim the refund, or you file Form 15G/15H
in advance if your final liability is genuinely nil. And if a Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement applies, the
TDS can be reduced to the treaty rate — usually 10–15% on interest — but only after the bank has your
[e-PAN-linked](https://incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/) details, tax residency certificate and the treaty forms
on file. Without the paperwork the bank deducts at the domestic rate; the refund comes only through filing.

## Repatriation: what moves freely and what does not

NRE balances move out freely — no form, no limit, wire out whenever. NRO balances require the paperwork
cadence: Form 15CA and, depending on the amount and purpose, Form 15CB from a chartered accountant, submitted
through the bank, up to USD 1 million per financial year. The 15CA/CB machinery exists so the tax department
sees every outflow against your tax position; the bank will not process an NRO remittance without them.

Three practical consequences. Sale proceeds of property held in India sit in NRO, and the USD 1 million cap
covers the aggregated remittances for the year — plan a property sale and a dividend windfall in the same year
as one budget, not two. Interest earned on NRO deposits cannot be quietly shifted into the NRE account; income
earned in India stays in NRO. And the exchange-rate risk on NRO balances is the same as on NRE — both hold
rupees — but NRO money that cannot leave cheaply compounds the FX decision: you cannot dollar-cost-average an
account whose outflows are rationed.

## The compliance traps NRIs actually hit

**Status conversion.** The moment you become non-resident, your resident savings account must be converted to
NRO — holding a resident account as an NRI violates FEMA and attracts penalties under the Foreign Exchange
Management Act; banks sweep this in periodic KYC reviews. Converting late is fixable; the interest earned in
the interim is the messy part.

**Interest mislabelling.** Putting foreign salary into the NRO account and Indian rent into the NRE account is
a common, incorrect habit. The classification follows the source of funds, not convenience, and it determines
both the tax treatment and the repatriation rights. Getting it backwards means NRE balances that are actually
Indian income — which are not freely repatriable — and NRO balances taxed as if they were foreign earnings.

**Fixed deposits: NRE vs FCNR vs NRO.** An NRE FD gives tax-free interest and free repatriation but full
currency risk on rupee depreciation. An FCNR (Foreign Currency Non-Resident) deposit holds USD, GBP, EUR, JPY,
AUD or CAD — the interest is tax-free, the principal is not exposed to the rupee, but the rate is lower. An NRO
FD is the only home for Indian-source income and pays the 30% TDS. Choosing among the three is a currency
decision as much as a tax one.

| Deposit | Currency         | Interest tax in India     | Repatriation           |
| ------- | ---------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| NRE FD  | INR              | Exempt                    | Free                   |
| FCNR FD | Foreign currency | Exempt                    | Free                   |
| NRO FD  | INR              | 30% + cess, TDS at source | USD 1M per FY, 15CA/CB |

## The legality note

Everything above is standard and legal; the accounts themselves are governed by FEMA's deposit regulations, and
the RBI's master directions on [deposits and accounts](https://www.rbi.org.in) set what each account may hold.
The grey zones NRIs drift into are structural: continuing to run a resident savings account after becoming
non-resident, using a resident family member's account to route NRI earnings, or opening accounts without
updating KYC residency status. Consequences of FEMA violations are concrete: compounding penalties levied by
the Reserve Bank, monetary penalties for contraventions (which can extend up to three times the amount involved
in certain violations under FEMA's adjudication framework), and for serious cases, enforcement directorate
proceedings. The clean path costs nothing: notify the bank of the status change before you leave, convert the
resident account, and route foreign earnings through NRE and Indian earnings through NRO from day one.

## The bottom line

NRE is for foreign money and gives you tax-free interest with free movement out. NRO is for Indian money and
gives you a 30% tax and a USD 1 million annual ceiling. A working NRI setup is two accounts, correctly fed,
with the treaty paperwork (residency certificate, Form 10F, bank mandate) filed at the Indian bank _before_
the first interest payment, not after the first TDS hit. Do that and the accounts do what they were designed
to do — keep your cross-border money cheap to hold and possible to move.
