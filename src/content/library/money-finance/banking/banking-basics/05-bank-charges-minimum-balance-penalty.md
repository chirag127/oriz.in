Bank fee schedules are written in small type and read by almost no one, which is precisely how they survive. The
typical metro savings account demands a ₹10,000 quarterly average balance and charges ₹400–600 plus GST when you
miss it — a penalty that can exceed the entire year's interest on a modest balance. Add card fees, SMS charges,
and the small transaction fees, and a family with three accounts can lose more to fees than the deposits earn.
This article is a fee schedule, read properly, with the fixes.

## The minimum-balance trap, quantified

Banks set a Quarterly Average Balance (QAB) or Average Monthly Balance (AMB) by branch location. The penalty is
levied per quarter of shortfall:

| Bank tier                        | Typical QAB/AMB requirement          | Shortfall penalty (per quarter) |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| PSU bank, metro branch           | ₹5,000–10,000 QAB                    | ₹300–600 + GST                  |
| Large private bank, metro        | ₹10,000–25,000 AMB                   | ₹500–750 + GST                  |
| Large private bank, premium tier | ₹50,000–1,00,000                     | ₹800–1,500 + GST                |
| Rural/semi-urban branches        | ₹1,000–2,500                         | ₹100–250 + GST                  |
| Zero-balance salary account      | None — while salary credits continue | None (but see below)            |

The arithmetic that hurts: a ₹20,000 average balance at 2.75% earns ₹550 a year. One missed quarter at ₹600 plus
GST is ₹708 — more than the year's entire interest. And the salary account's zero-balance status is conditional:
**when salary credits stop — job change, sabbatical, layoff — the account converts to a regular one and the
minimum-balance penalties start**, usually without a notification that registers. This is the classic post-job-change
fee leak.

## The full fee zoo

| Charge                               | Typical amount            | Trigger                                   |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Debit card annual/replacement        | ₹200–750/year             | Auto-renewed; replacement ₹200–500        |
| SMS/alert charges                    | ₹15–25/quarter            | Often waived above a balance              |
| Cheque return (inward)               | ₹300–590                  | Bounced incoming cheque                   |
| ECS/ACH/NACH debit return            | ₹300–500                  | The failed SIP/EMI auto-debit             |
| IMPS beyond free limit               | ₹2.5–25 per txn           | Most banks give generous free quotas now  |
| NEFT/RTGS                            | Free online at most banks | Branch-based RTGS can cost ₹25–55         |
| Duplicate statement/passbook         | ₹50–200                   |                                           |
| Dormant account reactivation         | Free–₹500                 | Inactivity beyond 2 years                 |
| Locker rent + access charges         | ₹1,000–6,000/year         | Annual, auto-debited                      |
| Closure of account within 30–90 days | ₹500                      | Opening-closing churn                     |
| Demand draft issuance                | ₹50–250 + ₹2.5/₹1,000     | The old-school payments people still need |

Two of these deserve emphasis. **The ECS return charge** (₹300–500) is what a failed SIP or EMI auto-debit costs —
and it recurs; a month of low balance can trigger three bounce charges plus the AMC's own penalties plus a late
fee on the loan. **The dormant-account cascade:** an account unused for 24 months is marked inoperative; credits
still arrive, but debits stop until reactivation with in-person KYC — which is exactly the account a family member
tries to use in an emergency.

## The regulatory backstop

The RBI's framework on "levy of charges in payment systems" and the Master Direction on customer service set
limits and disclosure duties: banks must publish their full schedule of charges on their websites, display it at
branches, and — critically — **notify the customer before levying a charge** for insufficient balance in payment
systems (capped at ₹100 + GST for such failures since 2019 for certain payment categories). Interest on savings
balances must be credited at least quarterly (most banks do monthly on daily balances). The full consumer
protection framework lives at [rbi.org.in](https://www.rbi.org.in/), and the Banking Ombudsman (now the
Integrated Ombudsman Scheme) handles complaints a bank has not resolved within 30 days.

## The zero-fee account stack

The fix is structural, not behavioural. Build the stack so fees have nothing to attach to:

| Account                     | Choice                                                                          | Fee exposure                    |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Salary account              | Keep it, spend from it                                                          | Zero-balance while salary flows |
| Secondary/primary savings   | Pick a bank whose QAB you genuinely keep, or a zero-balance digital/SFB account | Zero if chosen honestly         |
| Old accounts from every job | **Close them**                                                                  | They are dormant-fee generators |
| Emergency/sinking funds     | Separate bank, high-yield, no card                                              | Check its schedule once a year  |

The old-accounts row is the one everyone skips. Three former salary accounts from three employers, each with a
debit card and an inactive net-banking profile, are three dormant-account and card-fee generators — plus a KYC
liability and a fraud surface. Closing an account takes one branch visit or, increasingly, an in-app request; the
ten-minute payoff is permanent.

## The appeal process that actually works

When a charge is levied wrongly — and banks do err, especially on system-conversion days — the escalation ladder
is: **(1)** raise it in the app or net-banking with the transaction ID; most are reversed in a day for genuine
errors, and a polite, factual note citing the published schedule speeds this. **(2)** Call the customer-care line
and note the complaint reference number — this is the timestamp that starts the 30-day clock. **(3)** File at the
bank's named nodal officer (email on the website). **(4)** The RBI Integrated Ombudsman, cms.rbi.org.in, free, with
the complaint reference from step 2 in hand. Charges reversed at step 1: usually ₹300–600. Charges pursued to
step 4: rarely worth it below ₹1,000 — but the _pattern_ of a wrongly recurring charge is worth escalating fully,
because it fixes the system for everyone.

## The annual fee audit

Add one line to the monthly review ritual: every quarter, scan the account's "charges" filter in the statement.
Any fee that recurred twice — card fee, SMS charge, minimum-balance penalty — becomes a decision: cancel the card,
switch the account, maintain the balance, or close the account. Fees are the only expense line in personal finance
that can be reduced to _zero_ by paperwork alone; a family spending ₹2,000 a year on bank fees is not paying for
a service, it is paying for not reading a page.

## The legality note

Bank fee-charging is fully legal within the RBI's disclosure framework — the "grey" lives in the enforcement gap:
charges levied without the mandated notice, or schedules not published as required. The consequences run both
ways: a bank levying undisclosed charges faces ombudsman awards and, at scale, RBI supervisory action; a customer
who disputes a _properly disclosed and levied_ charge without grounds spends effort for nothing. Read your bank's
current Schedule of Charges once — it is one page, it is mandated, and it converts the small print from a trap
into a checklist.
