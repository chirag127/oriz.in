The savings account is where money goes to feel safe and earn 2.7%. Most large banks pay 2.5–3% on balances up to
₹50 lakh — below inflation, below every alternative with the same liquidity, and yet the default resting place
for the average household's entire financial life. The account earns a pass because it feels familiar. This
article treats it as what it is: an instrument with a rate, a structure, and alternatives that should be compared
rather than assumed.

## How savings interest is actually calculated

Two facts surprise almost everyone. First, since April 2010, banks pay interest on the **daily balance**, not the
minimum monthly balance — every day's closing balance earns that day's rate. Second, the rate is annualised; the
calculation is balance × rate ÷ 365 for each day. The practical consequence: the timing of inflows matters. A
₹2 lakh credit that sits for 20 days before being spent or invested earned 20 days of 3%, not zero — but a
₹2 lakh balance swept to investments on day one earns the fund's rate for all 20 days instead.

| Balance                          | Rate                 | Interest/year | Interest if idle 10 years |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------- |
| ₹50,000                          | 2.75%                | ₹1,375        | ₹13,750                   |
| ₹2,00,000                        | 2.75%                | ₹5,500        | ₹55,000                   |
| ₹5,00,000                        | 2.75% (typical slab) | ₹13,750       | ₹1,37,500                 |
| ₹5,00,000 in a liquid fund at 6% | —                    | ₹30,000       | ₹3,00,000+                |

The last row's difference — about ₹16,000 a year on ₹5 lakh — is the entire argument of this article. Note the
contrast is not hypothetical: liquid funds with instant-redemption access currently yield roughly 5–6.5%
pre-tax against the large banks' 2.5–3%.

## What the high-yield banks pay

Small finance banks compete on rate, and their savings rates are materially higher — typically 3.5–4.5% on
regular balances, some offering ~7.25% on slices up to a few lakh, with Deposit Insurance cover (more in the
DICGC article in this track) applying equally up to ₹5 lakh per bank per depositor.

| Bank type                        | Typical savings rate (FY 2025-26) | Notes                              |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Large PSU banks                  | 2.5–3.0%                          | Ubiquitous branches, worst rates   |
| Large private banks              | 2.5–3.0%                          | Some slabs to 3.5%                 |
| Small finance banks              | 3.5–7.25% on slices               | Higher but with balance conditions |
| Sweep-in FD linked to savings    | ~FD rate on the swept slice       | Breaks automatically on withdrawal |
| Liquid fund + instant redemption | 5–6.5% pre-tax                    | T+1 settlement for the rest        |

The structure that works: keep one month's expenses plus the EMI-buffer in the best-paying liquid account, keep
the deep reserve in a liquid fund (see the emergency-fund article), and let the salary account be a salary
_account_ — a pass-through, not a vault.

## Sweep-in FDs: the free lunch most people never order

A sweep-in (multi-deposit) FD links a fixed deposit to your savings account. Balances above a threshold you set
automatically split into FD-sized slices earning the FD rate (one year FDs at major banks currently ~6.6–7%);
withdrawals break the smallest slice first, in FIFO order, with interest paid to the day of break — no penalty
if the FD has run past its minimum period. This is the rare instrument with no real trade-off: FD-grade yield,
savings-grade access.

| Setup                 | Rate earned on idle cash | Liquidity                       | Effort                      |
| --------------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Plain savings only    | 2.5–3%                   | Instant                         | None                        |
| Savings + sweep-in FD | ~6.5–7% on swept slice   | Instant (breaks slices)         | One-time setup              |
| Savings + liquid fund | 5–6.5% pre-tax           | T+1 (instant up to ₹50,000/day) | Monthly transfer discipline |

Sweep-in caps per bank vary (some limit sweeps to ₹15–20 lakh total), and the FD slices attract TDS on interest
like any FD — which brings up the tax point.

## The tax arithmetic nobody runs

Savings interest enjoys a deduction only in the old regime: Section 80TTA allows ₹10,000 of savings interest for
under-60s; 80TTB allows ₹50,000 for senior citizens (and also covers FD interest). In the new regime, neither
deduction exists — savings and FD interest is taxed at slab, fully. FD interest attracts TDS at 10% (20% without
PAN) once annual interest crosses ₹40,000 (₹1,00,000 for senior citizens) as of FY 2025-26. And liquid-fund
returns are taxed at your slab as short-term capital gains for holdings under three years — the "debt fund
indexation" era ended with Finance Act 2023, and the yield comparisons must be made post-tax.

| Instrument         | Pre-tax yield | Post-tax at 30% slab | Post-tax at 5% slab |
| ------------------ | ------------- | -------------------- | ------------------- |
| Savings account 3% | 3.0%          | 2.1%                 | 2.85%               |
| Sweep-in FD 7%     | 7.0%          | 4.9%                 | 6.65%               |
| Liquid fund 6.2%   | 6.2%          | 4.34%                | 5.89%               |

The post-tax comparison is the honest one, and it still says the same thing: idle cash in a 2.75% account is a
subsidy you are paying to your bank.

## The fees that quietly erase the interest

The interest earned is gross; the account's fee schedule is the net.

| Fee                             | Typical amount       | How it triggers                            |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Minimum balance penalty (metro) | ₹350–600 per quarter | QAB shortfall, metro branches ₹10,000+ QAB |
| Debit card annual fee           | ₹250–750/year        | Auto-renewed, auto-debited                 |
| SMS/alert charges               | ₹15–25/quarter       | Sometimes free above a balance             |
| Cheque book beyond free pages   | ₹1–2/page            | Rare, but real                             |
| IMPS/NEFT beyond free limits    | ₹2.5–25              | Most retail volumes are free now           |

A quarterly minimum-balance penalty of ₹500 against a ₹2 lakh average balance earning 2.75% (₹5,500/year) erases
more than a third of the year's interest. Choosing a zero-balance account (salary accounts are typically
zero-balance; some small-finance and digital accounts are too) or an account whose QAB you genuinely maintain is
worth more than a 25-basis-point rate difference.

## The structure, summarised

One salary account (zero-balance, best app, the bank your EMIs live with), one high-yield savings or sweep-in
account for the float and sinking funds, and a liquid fund for the emergency buffer's middle layer. Set up the
sweep once, automate the transfers on salary day, and the savings account reverts to its correct role: a doorway,
not a destination. The Reserve Bank's consumer pages
([rbi.org.in](https://www.rbi.org.in/)) list the interest-rate and fee disclosures every bank must publish —
reading your own bank's current schedule once a year is part of the annual review ritual.

## What to do this week

Check the actual rate your main account pays (it is on the bank's website, not in the app). Check your average
balance over the last six months. Compute interest earned versus what the sweep-in or liquid-fund version would
have paid. The gap is the article's thesis in rupees — and moving it takes one afternoon and zero risk tolerance,
because nothing here involves equity, volatility, or opinions about markets.
