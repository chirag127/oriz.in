Input tax credit is the GST system's load-bearing wall: you pay GST on what you buy, deduct it from GST you
collect, and remit the difference. It is also where GST fraud concentrates, because ITC is a claim built on
other people's paperwork — and when the paperwork is fake, the money is real. The department's enforcement
data and the case record show a small set of repeated patterns. If you run a business, or you are the
accountant or the investor analysing one, these patterns and the thresholds that turn them from a demand
notice into a criminal case are worth knowing precisely.

## The four patterns that account for most cases

**1. The shell-supplier chain.** A firm bills you ₹50 lakh of "goods" — steel, scrap, fabric — with 9% GST,
₹4.5 lakh of ITC. The supplier exists on paper: a PAN, a GSTIN, a rented shutter, no godown, no transport.
The department's own figure from repeated operations is that a single mastermind's chain of 500-1,000 fake
GSTINs issues invoices worth ₹100-500 crore of fake ITC. Your ₹4.5 lakh claim is one leaf on that tree.

**2. The fake-invoice-no-supply variant.** Invoices exist, goods do not. E-way bills are generated to match,
sometimes by operators who run the same truck's number through multiple routes. Detection is arithmetic: a
trader claiming ITC on ₹10 crore of purchases while showing ₹11 crore of sales has no inventory or margin
that supports it.

**3. Inflated or inverted ITC.** The goods were real; the credit claimed is not — claiming ITC on exempt
supplies' inputs, on goods used for personal consumption, on capital goods at full value instead of the
apportioned amount, or the classic: claiming ITC on motor vehicles where the law allows it only for specific
vehicle businesses.

**4. The mismatch claim.** Claiming ITC the supplier never reported in his GSTR-1/3B — the pre-2017 style
self-declaration claim that the department has been systematically closing since the Section 16(2)(aa)
amendment made supplier-reported filing a condition of your credit.

| Pattern               | Detection signal                                          | Typical consequence            |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Shell supplier chain  | Supplier non-filing, no e-way bills, doorstep check fails | Full ITC demand + interest     |
| Fake invoices         | Purchases ≈ sales, no inventory build-up                  | Demand + penalty + prosecution |
| Inflated/inverted ITC | 2A/2B vs 3B mismatch, GSTR-9 reconciliation               | Demand + interest + penalty    |
| Mismatch claims       | Supplier's GSTR-1 does not show the invoice               | ITC disallowed                 |

## The thresholds: when it becomes criminal

The GST law's escalation has clear rungs. A mismatched or excess claim is first a **demand**: Section 73/74
notice, tax plus interest at 18% per annum plus penalty (Section 73's routine cases, Section 74's
fraud-supposition cases where the penalty is 25% of tax if paid within the notice window, or 50% otherwise).
The criminal rungs: Section 132 of the CGST Act makes issuing invoices without supply a punishable offence —
where the fake ITC exceeds ₹2 crore, imprisonment of one year and fine; above ₹5 crore, five years and fine;
the fake-billing prosecutions you read about in the business press are this section being applied to
masterminds. The Act and rules are at [gst.gov.in](https://www.gst.gov.in), the department's own portal.

## The honest-business problem: ITC you claimed, on real purchases, from a fraudster

This is the pattern that hurts readers of a finance blog. You bought genuine goods, paid GST with real money,
claimed ITC honestly — and the supplier was a shell that collected your GST and vanished without remitting or
filing. Section 16(2)(aa) and Rule 36(4) restrict your claim to what the supplier reported; the department's
demand lands on you anyway, and the law's answer is Section 16(5), which lets you re-claim ITC with
documentation if you prove possession of goods, the invoice, the payment (typically bank-paid, not cash) and
the supplier's identity. The practical defences, in order of weight:

1. **Pay the supplier from the bank, always.** Cash-purchased GST invoices are the first filter the
   department applies.
2. **Check the supplier's GSTIN filing status before the first big purchase** — the portal's "search
   taxpayer" shows return-filing history. A supplier who has not filed GSTR-3B for six months is a claim
   waiting to be disallowed.
3. **Reconcile GSTR-2B to your books monthly**, not at year-end. The 2B-vs-3B gap is the demand notice's
   raw material.
4. **Document possession** — godown entries, e-way bills, weighbridge slips. Section 16(5) relief is
   evidence-shaped.

## The compliance arithmetic that makes honesty cheap

A ₹1 crore turnover trader with real purchases of ₹60 lakh at 18% has ₹10.8 lakh of genuine ITC. The cost of
keeping it clean: a part-time accountant reconciling 2B monthly, ₹6,000-8,000/month. The cost of getting it
wrong via one shell supplier: ₹4-5 lakh demand, 18% interest from the claim date, a penalty cycle, and —
where the department reads the pattern as participation — Section 132 exposure. The asymmetry is not subtle.

## The legality note

Fake ITC is **illegal**, not grey: issuing an invoice without supply is an offence under Section 132 CGST,
with the imprisonment thresholds above; availing credit not entitled to is recoverable with interest, and
Section 125's general penalty runs to ₹25,000. Prosecutions under Section 132 against masterminds and
recurring suppliers are routine in the 2022-2025 enforcement record; recipients who merely failed to verify
suppliers face the demand, the interest and a penalty, not usually prosecution — the difference is intent
evidence, and the evidence of intent is mostly paper: cash payments, missing goods records, identity-borrowed
GSTINs. Consequences for the honest recipient who documented everything are recoverable and limited to the
re-claim procedure of Section 16(5).

## The bottom line

The GST ITC system shifted its verification burden onto the buyer, and that is the structural fact to plan
around: your credit is only as clean as your supplier's filing discipline. The patterns that destroy
businesses — shell chains, fake invoices, inflated claims — are all visible in advance in the same three
places: the supplier's filing history, the 2B reconciliation, and the bank statement. The fraud economics work
on volume and on buyers who never check; the honest economics work on ₹6,000 a month and an afternoon of
reconciliation. The demand notice arrives with a choice between those two prices already priced in.
