Buying property in someone else's name — a parent, a sibling, the household staff whose PAN looks cleaner — is
common enough in India to feel normal. Most of it is lawful. Some of it is benami, and benami is not a grey
area: since the 2016 amendment it is a prohibit-and-confiscate regime, with a 100% penalty and jail. The
difference between the lawful arrangement and the prohibited one is not the presence of another name on the
deed; it is whose money bought the asset and who benefits from it. That line, drawn precisely, is this article.

## What the law actually prohibits

The Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988, rewritten by the 2016 amendment and enforced from November
2016, defines a benami transaction as one where property is transferred to a person for consideration paid by
another, and the property is held _for the benefit_ of the payer. The classic pattern: your ₹80 lakh flat is
bought in your cousin's name, with your money, and your cousin has no real say and no real interest — the
cousin is the _benamidar_, you are the _beneficial owner_, and the arrangement is exactly what the Act kills.
The initiator can be a company too — shell companies parking promoters' assets under employees' names is the
second classic pattern.

The consequence structure is the harshest in Indian tax-adjacent law short of prosecution for fraud:

| Stage        | Authority                | Outcome                                           |
| ------------ | ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Initiation   | Joint/Range Commissioner | Notice, inquiry, holding as benami                |
| Confiscation | Adjudicating Authority   | Property vests in the Government, no compensation |
| Penalty      | Adjudicating Authority   | 25% of fair market value of the property          |
| Prosecution  | Central Government       | Rigorous imprisonment 1-7 years + fine            |
| Appeal       | Tribunal, then HC/SC     | Standard appellate route                          |

The 25% penalty is on fair market value — not the purchase price. An ₹80 lakh flat bought in 2014 and worth
₹2 crore at initiation carries a ₹50 lakh penalty, plus confiscation of the property itself. Prosecution
under Section 69 runs alongside: one to seven years' rigorous imprisonment where the arrangement was entered
into with knowledge. The text of the Act and amendment are on
[incometaxindia.gov.in](https://incometaxindia.gov.in/Pages/acts.aspx), the department's statutes page.

## What remains perfectly legal

The Act's Section 2(9) carve-outs matter, because almost every legitimate family arrangement lives inside
them:

1. **Property held in the name of a spouse or child, paid from _known sources_ of the spouse or parent.** A
   father buying a flat for his minor daughter from disclosed, taxed income is exempt. The joint ownership
   clause covers spouses; the tax treatment separately (clubbing under Sections 64 and 64A of the
   Income-tax Act) still taxes the income to the provider — legal ownership and taxability stay separate.
2. **A coparcener holding HUF property in their own name.** Partition and family arrangements in the ordinary
   course are outside the Act.
3. **Property held by a person in a fiduciary capacity** — trustee, director of a company holding company
   assets, executor.
4. **An individual in the name of a brother or sister or lineal ascendant or descendant**, where again the
   purchase money comes from known sources. The "known sources" phrase is the load-bearing one: it means
   explainable, taxed, documented money. Loan the ₹80 lakh to your brother and you must show the gift deed or
   the loan agreement and your own money trail.
5. **A loan in the ordinary course** taken in another's name where the borrower genuinely holds and repays.

The pattern that is benami: money sourced from undisclosed income, the name-lender holding nothing, no
document, and the beneficial owner continuing to enjoy the asset — rent collected in cash, sale proceeds
routed back. The judge of record in benami disputes looks for exactly that bundle: who paid, who benefits,
whether there is a contemporaneous document.

## The way people get caught

Benami detection is no longer accidental. The Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Units (PBPT Units,
under the Income-tax department) work from data: AIS and SFT reporting of high-value property registrations,
mismatch between a buyer's known income and registered assets, and purchase trails across registrars. A
salaried person with ₹18 lakh annual income showing up as the payer on four properties across two states is
a system-generated flag, not an informant's tip. Provisional attachment follows — the property cannot be
sold, transferred or mortgaged while attached — and the 90-day adjudication clock starts. Once vested in the
Government, recovery of the property is effectively over; appeals continue but possession does not revert
while the case runs.

## Structure it lawfully instead

The legal substitutes achieve most of what people wanted from benami:

- **Genuine joint ownership with documented contribution.** Both names on the deed, each person's payment
  traceable from their own bank account, a simple declaration of beneficial shares. Income is taxed to each
  owner in the ratio of funding, which is the legal version of what benami tried to fake.
- **Loan, not front.** Fund the relative's purchase as a documented loan with interest at a defensible rate
  (the department's reference rates, currently around 9-10% for informal family loans above the gift
  threshold). The relative owns the property outright; you own a receivable.
- **Gift with clubbing eyes open.** Gift to a minor child is legal and the income clubs to you; gift to a
  major child or spouse taxed under 64(1)(iv) only for spouse; the asset's later sale by the holder is their
  capital gain, not yours.
- **Corporate or trust structures** for genuinely larger arrangements — a private trust with a registered
  deed holds family property transparently, at the cost of compliance.

None of these hide income from tax, which is the point: benami's use case was almost always undisclosed
income, and the Act was written to make that use case expensive.

## The legality note

Benami is **illegal**, full stop, and the consequences are confiscatory: attachment and vesting of the
property in the State without compensation, a penalty of 25% of fair market value under Section 70, and
prosecution under Section 69 with rigorous imprisonment from one to seven years plus fine, where the person
entered the arrangement knowingly. The benamidar (name-holder) is prosecutable too — being the front is not
a defence. There is one narrow window: transactions were contractually barred from enforcement for the 1988
Act's core provision until the 2016 amendment made it operative, so arrangements predating enforcement are
treated on their own facts, but any _new_ benami arrangement after November 2016 is fully exposed. Complaints
and information go through the department; the Act's text, rules and the Benami Transactions (Prohibition)
Rules are all at incometaxindia.gov.in.

## The bottom line

The test is one sentence long: **whose money bought it, from what sources, and who enjoys it?** If all three
answers are the same documented person, nothing is benami. If the money and the benefit are yours while the
name is someone else's, the arrangement sits inside a statute that confiscates the asset, takes a quarter of
its value again, and can take the years of your life in between. Family property planning in India is legal,
well-trodden and cheap — a gift deed, a co-ownership agreement, a loan agreement, a trust. The benami route
survives only because people confuse "everyone does it" with "it is allowed". Since 2016, it is not.
