India's informal workforce — well over 400 million people by most government counts — is paid mostly in cash,
frequently with no appointment letter, no salary slip, no EPF. If you are one of them, the problem is not the
cash; cash is legal pay. The problem is the _record_. A salary that exists nowhere in writing cannot be shown
to a bank, a visa officer, an insurer underwriting your term plan, or the income-tax department, and each of
them prices you as having no income at all. This article prices the gap.

## What the employer is really doing

An employer who pays ₹25,000 a month in cash with no salary slip is usually doing one of two things: paying a
genuine wage in a genuinely informal arrangement (a family shop, a local clinic), or keeping you off the books
to avoid statutory costs. The statutory bill on a ₹25,000 wage looks like this:

| Head                                    | Employer share | Your share (deducted)  |
| --------------------------------------- | -------------- | ---------------------- |
| EPF (12% each on ₹15,000 statutory cap) | ₹1,800/month   | ₹1,800/month           |
| ESIC (3.25% / 0.75%) if covered         | ₹813/month     | ₹188/month             |
| Professional tax                        | —              | ₹200/month (state-set) |
| Total monthly statutory cost            | ₹2,613         | ₹2,188                 |

On ₹3 lakh a year, that is roughly ₹55,000 a year of statutory money that never enters any system — the
employer's contribution you never earn, the EPF you never accumulate, the ESI cover you never hold. The
employer saves it; you lose it. The EPF loss alone compounds: ₹1,800 plus ₹1,800 a month at 8.25% (the EPFO
interest rate for 2024-25, continued in 2025-26) for twenty years builds about ₹22.7 lakh — against zero.
The tax department's e-filing portal, [incometax.gov.in](https://incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/), is where the
absence shows up first: no employer files TDS against your PAN, so no employer ever appears in your Form 26AS.

## What you lose, item by item

**Credit.** A bank underwriting a personal loan or home loan wants proof of income: salary slips for three to
six months, Form 16, bank statements showing a repeating credit. Cash-paid means you can show a bank statement
full of cash deposits — which underwriters read as unverifiable income and score conservatively. The
difference is not cosmetic. A salaried borrower with documented ₹25,000 salary may be offered a personal loan
at 11-13%; a cash-income borrower the same bank can underwrite at all is pushed toward 15-18%, or refused.
On a ₹3 lakh, four-year loan, 12% versus 16% is ₹11,200 more in interest over the tenure.

**Pension.** EPF membership carries EPS pension membership for salaried covered employment. An unrecorded
worker gets none, and also cannot build the NPS or Atal Pension tiers on employment income — Atal Pension
requires a bank-linked savings account and targets unorganised workers precisely, but the enrolment has to
happen. None of it happens by itself.

**Insurance and government benefits.** Hospital cash policies, term insurance financial underwriting, and the
income proof for a subsidised scheme or a scholarship for your child all ask for the same documents. The
e-Shram registration for unorganised workers exists precisely to give informal workers an official identity —
at [eshram.gov.in](https://eshram.gov.in) — and costs nothing, yet most cash-paid workers have never
registered.

## Making cash employment as documented as you can

1. **Get something in writing.** A one-page appointment letter, signed, stating role, monthly wage, and date of
   joining. If the employer refuses, you have learned something about the arrangement's stability.
2. **Generate your own paper trail.** Bank every rupee — deposit the cash yourself and let the account show a
   consistent ₹25,000/month pattern. Consistency is what converts cash into evidence.
3. **File an ITR anyway.** Income below the basic exemption is not taxable but filing creates the record.
   Under the new regime the exemption is ₹4 lakh (FY 2025-26); a ₹3 lakh salary owes no tax, and an ITR-1
   filed from the portal builds a three-, five-, ten-year income history that a bank can read. This is the
   single highest-return document habit in informal work.
4. **Open a VYAJ or similar recurring discipline.** Automate a small SIP — ₹1,000-2,000/month — so the bank
   statement shows _financial_ behaviour, not just deposits. Lenders weight payment history heavily.
5. **Register on e-Shram and link UAN if any past employer ever deducted EPF.** Old, dormant UANs hold real
   money; passbook checks are free at the EPFO member portal.

## When it becomes a legal problem

Cash pay is legal. What is not: the employer deducting EPF from your wage and not depositing it (a Section 14
EPF Act offence, with damages up to 25% per annum of the arrears under Section 14B), or an arrangement that
exists to hide the employment from a statute. And there is a worker-side version: claiming unemployment
benefits or a government scheme while drawing a cash salary is fraud, not grey.

## The legality note

For the worker, being paid in cash carries no penal consequences — there is no law against receiving wages in
currency. The legal exposure sits almost entirely with the employer: non-registration under the EPF Act where
20 or more are employed, non-deposit of deducted contributions (prosecutable under Section 14), and evasion of
ESI, professional tax and the Shops Act registrations. Workers do face one real risk: if your employer's
non-compliance surfaces in an inspection, your own continuity of employment is the casualty, which is why
self-generated documentation — bank credits, ITR history, e-Shram ID — matters more than fighting the employer.
Consequences for misusing the informality yourself, such as drawing scheme benefits while employed, are
recovery plus possible prosecution under the scheme's own penal section.

## The bottom line

You cannot force a small employer to become compliant. You can make your own employment legible: an appointment
letter you wrote yourself and got signed, a bank account that shows ₹25,000 arriving every month, an ITR filed
every year since 2022, an e-Shram card, a small SIP, a UAN retrieved. That file gets a cash earner treated
almost like a documented one — better pricing on loans, an income history for visas and schools, and a paper
trail if the arrangement ends abruptly. The ₹55,000 a year of statutory money is gone unless the employer
formalises; the record is yours to build either way, and it compounds exactly like the money does.
