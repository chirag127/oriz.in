Round-tripping is the circuit where Indian money leaves the country, dons a foreign passport, and returns as
"foreign investment" — often into the same promoter group it came from. The structure is not inherently
illegal; it is a routing choice with a compliance cost, and for years it also had a tax arbitrage worth the
routing. That arbitrage has been largely closed since 2017, which changed round-tripping from a tax strategy
into a paperwork habit — and, where the money leaving was undisclosed, into a black-money problem with real
teeth. Understanding the circuit is also how you read corporate India better: a surprising share of "FDI" in
the press releases is Indian money coming home.

## The circuit, step by step

1. **Origin.** A promoter (or an HNI, or a business with export earnings) has money in India — taxed profits,
   retained earnings, or in the aggressive versions, money that never entered the books.
2. **Exit.** The money leaves through a legitimate door: overseas direct investment under the ODI rules
   (RBI's automated route for bonafide business), export proceeds retained abroad, or in the black versions,
   under-invoiced exports and cash carried out over years.
3. **The shell.** It parks in a low-tax jurisdiction — historically Mauritius, now more often Singapore, the
   UAE or the Netherlands — in an SPV owned by the promoter's family or trusts.
4. **Re-entry.** The SPV invests into India as FDI (in listed shares, unlisted equity, or projects), often
   into the promoter's own group companies. The money is now "foreign investment" and enjoys the treaty
   benefits and the exit flexibility that label confers.

| Circuit stage                | Legal version cost                   | What it buys                                    |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Exit via ODI                 | RBI filing (FC/FLA), ₹0 tax          | Legitimate overseas presence, real business     |
| Shell in Mauritius/Singapore | Incorporation + audit, ₹5-15 lakh/yr | Treaty access, holding-company flexibility      |
| Re-entry as FDI              | FDI compliance filings               | No FDI caps friction, easy exits and re-entries |
| Dividends/gains out          | Withholding at treaty rate           | The old arbitrage: near-zero tax on exit        |

## What the old arbitrage was, and what killed it

Until 2017, the Mauritius treaty exempted capital gains on Indian shares from Indian tax — a Mauritius SPV
selling an Indian company paid no Indian capital gains tax, while an Indian resident selling the same shares
paid 20%-plus. The arithmetic on a ₹500 crore exit: ₹100 crore-plus saved. The treaty protocol of 2016
(operative April 2017) phased out that exemption — full Indian tax from April 2018 on old investments — and
the GAAR (General Anti-Avoidance Rules, operative from FY 2017-18) can disregard arrangements whose _main
purpose_ is tax benefit, once a threshold of ₹3 crore tax benefit in a year is crossed. The text and rules are
on [incometaxindia.gov.in](https://incometaxindia.gov.in/Pages/acts.aspx).

What survives: Singapore and UAE residencies with real substance, where the SPV has staff, board meetings and
a function beyond signing papers; the Netherlands and Mauritius for genuine holding structures; and the plain
commercial logic that a foreign holding company is useful for raising foreign capital, listing abroad, and
exit flexibility. What does not survive: the pure paper-shuffle where a two-director shell with no office
sits between the promoter and his own money.

## The black-money version, and how it differs

The grey in round-tripping is not the circuit — it is the source of the money entering it. Two variants:

- **Undisclosed money routed out** through under-invoiced exports (ship ₹100 crore of goods, invoice ₹70
  crore, keep ₹30 crore abroad), over-invoiced imports, or plain cash smuggling. That money, coming back as
  FDI into your own group, is the classic round-trip laundering pattern. The Black Money Act, 2015 taxes
  undisclosed foreign assets at 30% plus surcharge and cess with a 10% penalty on the tax, and prosecution
  for wilful evasion; the compliance-window era is over and the current regime is the punitive one.
- **Taxed money routed out but badly documented** — ODI without the required filings, or money out through
  current-account channels that the RBI's LRS rules do not permit. This is a contravention problem, not an
  evasion problem, and it compounds with compounding fees.

The enforcement tell: investigations into round-tripping follow the money's _origin_, not its route. A
documented, taxed rupee that becomes a Singapore dollar and comes back is boring. An undocumented rupee that
becomes foreign investment is a case.

## How to read it as an investor

When you analyse a company, round-tripping shows up in patterns worth flagging: promoter-group entities
registered in treaty jurisdictions with no visible business; related-party investments announced as "strategic
foreign investment" from entities three months old; layers of SPVs between the listed company and its actual
operating assets; and FDI statistics that look impressive until you notice the source country is Singapore or
Mauritius and the sector is the promoter's own. The MCA's company master data at
[mca.gov.in](https://www.mca.gov.in) shows the incorporation trail, and the annual report's related-party
schedule shows the money's family tree. None of these patterns is automatically wrong — the test is whether
the foreign entity has substance and whether the money's Indian origin is documented.

## The legality note

Round-tripping as a structure is **legal**; the legality questions attach to its inputs. Consequences land in
three places: undisclosed money in foreign shells triggers the Black Money Act — 30% tax plus 10% penalty on
the tax, and prosecution where wilful evasion is established; sham arrangements whose main purpose is tax
avoidance can be disregarded under GAAR, converting treaty benefits into an assessment with interest under
Sections 234B/234C; and FEMA contraventions in the exit leg attract penalties up to three times the amount
involved, with compounding available. The 2019-2024 enforcement record — investigations into FDI from
jurisdictions favoured for round-tripping — shows the departments' method: reconstruct the origin of funds
through bank trails, then test the substance of the foreign entity. Neither test is survived by paper.

## The bottom line

Round-tripping went from a tax arbitrage to a structure cost, and that is the useful summary of the last
decade: what GAAR and the treaty protocols did was remove the _reward_ for complexity, leaving only the
_cost_. A promoter with genuinely taxed money and real reasons for a foreign holding company still builds
one — for the Singapore listing, the foreign investor, the exit option. A promoter with undisclosed money
finds that the same structure is now the trail's most visible breadcrumb: foreign shells report, AIS matches
investments, and the family tree of the funds is reconstructible years later. The structure never was the
crime and never was the defence; the paperwork was both.
