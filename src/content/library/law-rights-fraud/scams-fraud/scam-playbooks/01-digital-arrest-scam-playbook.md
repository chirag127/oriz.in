The digital arrest scam is the most cinematic fraud running in India today: a video call in which someone in
a police or CBI uniform tells you that a parcel in your name contains drugs, or that your Aadhaar funded a
money-laundering case, and that you must stay "on camera" — under virtual arrest — while you transfer your
savings to "verify" them. Losses run into hundreds of crore annually, victims include educated professionals,
and the script is now standardised enough to be quoted line by line. Knowing the script is the whole defence.

## The standard script, stage by stage

The scam follows a fixed sequence, automated at the start and human from stage 2:

| Stage               | What happens                                                                                            | What they want                    |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| 1. The trigger      | Robo-call or SMS: "a parcel in your name with drugs has been seized at Mumbai customs"                  | A callback, on an ordinary number |
| 2. The transfer     | Call switches to WhatsApp/Facetime video; uniformed "officer", police-station backdrop                  | Credibility                       |
| 3. The escalation   | "Your Aadhaar was used to open a bank account with ₹4 crore of hawala" ; "FIR will be filed in 2 hours" | Panic                             |
| 4. The arrest       | "You are under digital arrest; keep your camera on and stay in one room"                                | Isolation                         |
| 5. The verification | "Transfer your funds to a RBI-verification account; they will be returned after clearance"              | The money                         |
| 6. The bleed        | More transfers for "FIR withdrawal", "CBI clearance", "Supreme Court verification"                      | Everything else                   |

Two features distinguish this script from older phone frauds. First, the _video_ component: seeing a uniform
and a badge defeats the scepticism that voice-only calls no longer trigger. Second, the _confinement_: keeping
the victim on camera, isolated from family, for six to ten hours compresses the decision timeline so that
nothing can be verified. The confinement is the actual scam; the money transfer is its product.

## The pressure clock: why every deadline is fake

Every demand in the script carries an artificial clock: "the FIR is being filed at 3 pm", "the account will be
frozen at 5 pm", "the judge needs the clearance today". None of these processes work on those timelines. Real
customs detention, real CBI summons, real court proceedings run on paperwork served in writing, with case
numbers, through channels you can independently contact. The clock exists to prevent the one action that
kills the scam: hanging up and calling the real institution.

The counter-clock is simple and reliable. The National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal
([cybercrime.gov.in](https://cybercrime.gov.in)) and the helpline **1930** are the two official channels; both
exist precisely for the window that the scam's clock is designed to close. Money transferred within the last
hours can often be interdicted — the 1930 mechanism alerts the receiving bank to freeze the fraud-linked
account before the money is layered onward.

## The first 30 minutes: what to actually do

If you or a family member is inside a call of this shape:

1. **Hang up. Not mute — end the call.** A genuine officer will not punish a callback to the official number.
2. **Call the real institution yourself.** Customs: the number on [cbic.gov.in](https://www.cbic.gov.in).
   CBI: the number on [cbi.gov.in](https://cbi.gov.in). Bank: the number on the card or the bank's website.
   Never the number the caller gave you.
3. **Check the parcel claim against the facts.** Track the actual consignment number on the courier's own
   site. A non-existent tracking number ends the script.
4. **Report to 1930 or cybercrime.gov.in within the hour** if any transfer has already happened. Speed
   determines recoverability.
5. **Tell one person in the house.** The isolation is enforced by the scam's rulebook; breaking it breaks
   the spell.

| Action                                              | Timeframe                     | Effect                                |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Hang up and verify independently                    | Immediately                   | Ends the psychological confinement    |
| Report to 1930 / cybercrime.gov.in                  | Within 1 hour of any transfer | Banks alerted; accounts can be frozen |
| Block the caller's numbers                          | Same day                      | Stops the follow-up calls             |
| Inform the bank to reissue cards/change credentials | Same day                      | Stops further debit attempts          |
| Preserve evidence (screenshots, numbers, UTRs)      | Same day                      | Required for the investigation        |

## Why educated people fall for it

The script is engineered against three reliable human reflexes. **Authority:** uniform, badge, police-station
backdrop, case numbers quoted with confidence. **Fear of legal consequence:** the threat of an FIR, of
Aadhaar misuse, of being named in a money-laundering case — none of which arrive by video call, but all of
which feel real at 4 pm on a Tuesday. **Sunk-time compliance:** after two hours on camera, the mind treats
compliance as the only way to end the ordeal, and the "verification transfer" feels like the exit.

The statistics make the scale clear: the I4C (Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre) data reported through
2024–2025 put digital-arrest and impersonation losses in the hundreds of crore for the year, with a rising
share of victims in the 40–70 age band and among professionals with meaningful savings. The scam targets
people with money to move — the qualification is solvency.

## After the money has moved

Recovery odds fall with every hour, but the process still matters:

1. **Report immediately** on cybercrime.gov.in with the transaction UTRs, the caller's numbers, and the
   screenshots. The complaint generates the inter-bank alert through the CFCFRMS mechanism.
2. **File a written complaint with your bank** the same day, requesting recall of the remittance and freezing
   of the beneficiary accounts named in the complaint.
3. **Follow the complaint status online** — the portal assigns an acknowledgement number that the bank's
   fraud desk and the investigating officer both work from.
4. **Do not pay "recovery agents"** who contact you promising to get the money back for a fee; the
   recovery-agent layer is itself a known second-stage scam.

Realistic expectation: a fraction of first-hour cases is frozen and returned; same-day-reported cases have
meaningful recovery rates; cases reported after a week rarely recover anything, because the mule accounts
have layered the funds through dozens of wallets and withdrawals.

## The legality note

Nothing about the scam is legally ambiguous — impersonating a public servant is an offence, and cheating with
dishonest inducement is punishable under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita's cheating provisions, alongside the IT
Act's identity-theft and cheating-by-personation sections (66C and 66D). The mule accounts that receive the
money belong, in many cases, to people who rented out their accounts — and account-holders who knowingly rent
their KYC'd accounts for others' transactions face exposure under the PMLA's money-laundering provisions and
account freezing by the issuing bank. Consequences for the operators are prosecution and, in the
large-organised cases that the CBI and state cyber cells have broken up, chargesheets covering hundreds of
accused across jurisdictions. The genuine risk for a victim is not prosecution — victims are not accused —
but the permanent loss of the transferred funds. One legal note protects you immediately: no genuine
government process requires _money to be transferred to verify anything_, ever. That single sentence, said
aloud during the video call, ends the script.

## The bottom line

The digital arrest scam weaponises authority, isolation and a fake clock — and it breaks under exactly two
counter-moves: hang up, and verify through the official channel yourself. Keep 1930 and cybercrime.gov.in in
the family's shared notes, brief the parents and the uncles who own the savings the scam targets, and treat
any "verification transfer" demand as the scam's confession. The uniform is a costume; the clock is a prop;
the only real thing in the call is the account number they want your money in.
