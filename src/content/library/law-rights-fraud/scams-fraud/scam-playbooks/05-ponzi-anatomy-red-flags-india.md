Every Indian Ponzi follows the same blueprint, because the blueprint works: a business story with no
verifiable cash flow, a return that never varies with the market, early payers who become the sales force, and
a collapse timed to the growth stall rather than the investment cycle. Saradha, PACL, the emu-farming schemes,
the ₹1,000-crore housing-cum-plantation frauds — different names, identical skeleton. Learn the skeleton once
and the next scheme is recognisable on sight.

## The anatomy: four moving parts

A Ponzi is not a bad investment; it is a _payment system_ where old investors are paid from new investors'
money. Four parts make it run:

1. **The story.** Teak plantations, emu farms, hospital projects, "agri-tech", crypto arbitrage, tourist
   resorts. The story's job is to explain why the return is both high and stable — which no real business
   achieves. Real investments trade return against volatility; the scheme offers both, which is the tell.
2. **The payment discipline.** Payouts on schedule, always. A scheme that pays 2% a month _on the day_ for
   three years is not proof of a working business; it is proof of working cash flow between investors.
3. **The distribution army.** Agents earn commission on the money they bring in — 5–10% in the West Bengal
   schemes. The agents are victims too, but during the growth phase they are the sales force, and their
   neighbourhood credibility is the scheme's distribution moat.
4. **The growth stall.** The scheme needs _new_ money to keep paying. When new inflows slow — a competing
   scheme, a regulatory raid, a bad news cycle — the payout calendar collides with reality and the collapse
   arrives in weeks, not years.

| Part           | Honest-investment version             | Ponzi version                           |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Return source  | Underlying assets, business cash flow | New investors' contributions            |
| Return pattern | Varies with markets and cycles        | Smooth, punctual, suspiciously stable   |
| Distribution   | Regulated platforms, direct           | Agents on 5–10% commission              |
| Failure mode   | Mark-to-market losses, recoverable    | Total, because the assets never existed |

## The five red flags, ranked by diagnostic power

**1. The return is both high and uncorrelated.** 12–20% "assured" with no drawdown years is the single most
predictive flag. Compare against the honest floor: bank FDs 6.5–7.5%, liquid funds 5–7%, Nifty 50 long-run
compounders around 12% _with_ multi-year drawdowns. An "assured" 2% monthly compounds to 26.8% a year — a rate
no legitimate business can pay sustainably and still survive its own cost of capital.

**2. The regulator is not the regulator.** A genuine collective scheme is either a mutual fund (SEBI), a
chit (state registrar under the Chit Funds Act), a deposit (RBI/Companies Act limits) or a listed issue. If
the entity collecting money is a proprietorship, an unregistered LLP selling "agri contracts", or a company
issuing "private placement" to thousands of people, the statutory boundary is already crossed — PACL's
₹49,000-crore order and Saradha's collapse both turned on exactly this.

**3. The exit is harder than the entry.** Entry is a doorstep agent with a receipt. Exit is "the lock-in ends
next quarter", "the crop is not ready", "submit the original bond for transfer". Legitimate products have
documented, dated exit mechanics; schemes have narratives.

**4. The paper is not a security.** A genuine investment produces a demat statement, a fund folio, a registered
debenture. Ponzi paper is a "land allotment letter", "emu purchase agreement", "agri bond" — instruments
designed to look like receipts for a real asset while conferring none of the ownership.

**5. The compounding is quoted in months.** "2% monthly", "3% per 45 days" is optical engineering: it makes
the annual number look smaller and the daily credibility look higher. Multiply before comparing — 2% monthly
is 26.8% yearly, and that number is the lie you are being asked to believe.

| Red flag                                  | The honest equivalent                                  |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Assured 2% monthly, no drawdown ever      | Nifty's 12% with 30–50% drawdowns                      |
| Commission to every investor who recruits | Regulated fund distribution with disclosed trail       |
| Exit "when the project completes"         | Documented exit via exchange, folio, or dated maturity |
| Paper receipt, no registration number     | Demat/folio statement with ISIN or scheme code         |
| Returns paid in cash                      | Bank-traceable payouts, TDS deducted                   |

## The math of the collapse

The collapse is arithmetic, not luck. Take a scheme paying 2% monthly with 10% agent commission on new money.
For every ₹100 of new inflow, ₹10 goes to agents, ₹26.8 a year goes to payouts — the scheme must grow new
collections by roughly 40% a year _just to stand still_ on the payment calendar, before any real business
expense. Growth at that rate doubles the sales force, the investor base, and the exposure every 24 months,
which is why Indian schemes collapse at the 3–7 year mark: the addressable neighbourhood is finite.

| Scheme age | Inflow needed to keep paying (illustrative) | What typically happens                            |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Year 1–2   | Manageable from inflows                     | The testimonial phase; early payers recruit       |
| Year 3–4   | New inflows must exceed payouts             | Expansion to new districts, new products          |
| Year 5+    | Inflow growth stalls                        | Payout delays begin, "technical issues"           |
| Collapse   | Inflows < payouts for one quarter           | Redemption freeze, agent panic, police complaints |

The mathematics also explains the recovery rate. Because payouts went out and commissions went out, the money
is gone in the accounting sense — what remains is land bought with investor money, which is why Saradha's
recovery process and PACL's Supreme Court-supervised sale took years to return a fraction of the principal.
The honest expectation for a collapsed scheme is partial recovery of principal, over years, and never the
promised return.

## What to check before investing in any collective scheme

A five-minute checklist that filters out nearly everything that will fail:

1. **Registration identity.** Is the collector registered with SEBI (mutual fund, debenture, AIF), the RBI
   (deposits, NBFC), the state chit registrar, or is it a listed company issue? Search the name at
   [sebi.gov.in](https://www.sebi.gov.in) and on the MCA master data.
2. **Instrument.** What do you hold? A demat ISIN, a folio number, a debenture trust deed — or a receipt?
3. **Exit document.** Where, when, and through what mechanism does the money come back? Written, not verbal.
4. **Return variability.** What happened in the worst year the product existed? "There was no bad year" is
   the answer that ends the meeting.
5. **Regulator jurisdiction.** Whose complaint portal handles this? SEBI SCORES, RBI's complaint portal, the
   state registrar, or nobody?

## The legality note

Unregistered collective investment schemes are **illegal**, not merely risky. Under Section 11AA of the SEBI
Act, any scheme where contributions are pooled, managed on behalf of participants, and profits are shared
without SEBI registration is a CIS — and the registration threshold has no small-investor exemption.
Consequences for the operators are concrete: SEBI issues cessation and refund orders directing repayment of
collections _with_ returns at 10–15% interest, attaches properties, and prosecutes under Section 44 of the
SEBI Act, which carries imprisonment up to ten years where the fraud involves the public. Saradha's promoters
and several state-level operators received exactly these outcomes alongside criminal charges under the
Cheating provisions of the IPC/BNS. Investors face no prosecution — the loss is the punishment — but agents
who actively solicited have faced recovery proceedings and criminal complaints from the investors they
recruited. If you are already inside a scheme showing payout delays, file the complaint early: complaints
filed before the collapse are in the queue that recovery actually reaches.

## The bottom line

Ponzi schemes do not survive scrutiny; they survive _unfamiliarity_. The five flags — assured high returns,
unregistered collector, one-way exit, receipt-not-security, monthly compounding optics — take five minutes to
check and describe every major Indian scheme failure of the last three decades. The regulation check is the
cheapest: find the collector on SEBI's, RBI's or MCA's registers, and treat the absence of a registration as
the answer. In this asset class, the absence of paperwork is not an inconvenience; it is the product.
