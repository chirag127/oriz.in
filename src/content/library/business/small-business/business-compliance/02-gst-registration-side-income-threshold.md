GST's registration thresholds were designed for small traders, but they are the first compliance question every
side income hits — the Etsy shop, the weekend photography, the consulting retainer, the Instagram store. The
thresholds are simple to state and misleading to apply, because "aggregate turnover" counts things people
assume it does not (platform sales, exports at zero rate, inter-state services), and because one category —
selling through e-commerce operators — has its own rules that override the headline numbers. This article is
the arithmetic of the decision to register.

## The thresholds as they actually stand (FY 2025-26)

| Supplier type                                | Registration threshold        | Notes                                         |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Services only                                | ₹20 lakh                      | ₹10 lakh in special-category states           |
| Goods (intra-state)                          | ₹40 lakh                      | ₹20 lakh special-category; some states opt in |
| Inter-state supply of goods (any)            | ₹20 lakh                      | Inter-state goods sales pull the lower limit  |
| Selling through e-commerce operators (goods) | No threshold if goods attract | Register before the first sale in some cases  |
| Casual taxable person (stalls, exhibitions)  | ₹0 — always register          | ₹5,000 deposit; 90-day registration           |
| reverse-charge supplies                      | ₹0                            | RCM liability needs registration regardless   |

The e-commerce row is where side incomes trip. Under Section 24(x) read with Notification 10/2017-IGST, a
person supplying goods through an e-commerce operator who is required to collect tax at source (an "e-commerce
operator" under Section 52) must register regardless of turnover — the classic case being a seller on a
marketplace platform. Services through platforms are treated differently: the operator may discharge GST
under Section 9(5) for specified services (ride-hailing, food delivery, certain housekeeping), in which case
the individual provider need not register. Goods sellers have no such comfort.

## What "aggregate turnover" counts, and what people wrongly exclude

Aggregate turnover (Section 2(6)) is the sum of taxable supplies, exempt supplies, exports (zero-rated counts
as turnover even though no GST is charged), and inter-state supplies — all India, PAN-level, all GSTINs
combined — _including_ supplies made through your unregistered period. It excludes the value of inward
supplies (purchases) and taxes. The common miscounts:

- **The foreign invoice at ₹8 lakh is turnover.** Zero-rated does not mean not-counted.
- **Exempt income counts.** Interest income is not supply; but any exempt _supply_ (certain services) counts
  in aggregate turnover even though no GST applies to it.
- **All PANs count together.** A side business registered in one state plus a job-city registration elsewhere
  share the threshold.
- **The ₹20 lakh is for the financial year, not rolling.** Cross it in December and registration follows within
  30 days of crossing; the clock resets each April.

## The voluntary registration decision, with numbers

Below the threshold you may register voluntarily — and often should, because input tax credit becomes real
money. A freelance video editor with a ₹18 lakh annual income from services buys a ₹1,20,000 laptop, ₹60,000
of software subscriptions (18% GST = ₹10,800 input) and works from a rented studio (18% on ₹2,40,000 =
₹43,200 input). Unregistered, all that input GST is a cost. Registered, it is credit against the 18% output
on invoices. The trade-offs:

| Factor           | Voluntary registration costs you                    | Voluntary registration earns you                   |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Compliance       | Quarterly/annual returns (QRMP), record-keeping     | Input credit on purchases, laptops, subscriptions  |
| Pricing          | 18% GST on every invoice to non-GST clients         | Ability to bill registered businesses who want ITC |
| Lock-in          | Cannot surrender for 1 year from registration       | LUT filing makes exports zero-rated with refunds   |
| RCM cash flow    | Reverse-charge imports/platform fees become payable | —                                                  |
| Penalty exposure | Filing obligations with late fees ₹50/day (₹20 cap) | —                                                  |

The breakeven is roughly when annual input GST exceeds the compliance cost and the price sensitivity of your
clients. Client mix decides most cases: registered businesses prefer registered vendors (their ITC depends on
it); individual consumers just see a higher invoice.

## The export case: LUT, zero-rating and the refund that is real

A service provider exporting services — the foreign client, foreign money, service consumed outside India —
makes a zero-rated supply. Without registration there is no legal zero-rating at all; with registration, file
the LUT (Form GST RFD-11) at the start of each financial year, and every export invoice goes out without GST.
Exports also count toward the threshold (above), so the growth path is: cross ₹20 lakh → register → LUT →
zero-rated invoices → claim refund of unutilised input credit or accumulate it. The classic failure is the
freelancer who charges 18% to a foreign client "because registration", recovering nothing — a refundable
error under Section 16 read with the zero-rated rules, recoverable only via refund claim within the window.

## The costs of registering late

Section 25 requires registration within 30 days of crossing the threshold. Late registration means GST due on
supplies from the crossing date, interest at 18% per annum on delay, and penalty ₹10,000 or 10% of tax,
whichever is higher, under Section 122. Departments match bank credits, platform 1099-equivalents (Form
26AS/AIS, TCS under Section 52), and foreign remittance certificates; the "my income is scattered across
accounts" defence has collapsed in the AIS era. Registering on crossing — or before the first marketplace
sale of goods — is cheaper than every alternative.

## The legality note

Operating above the threshold without registration is not a grey area — it is tax evasion with defined
consequences: tax plus interest plus penalty under Section 122 (₹10,000 or 10% of tax, higher of the two; up
to ₹25,000 for smaller defaults), and repeated or deliberate suppression escalates to Section 132 searches
and prosecution under Section 132 (imprisonment up to five years where tax exceeds ₹5 crore, one year above
₹2 crore for wilful fraud). Issuing invoices without a valid GSTIN while claiming GST, or claiming input
credit on purchases made "for the business" that never existed, are fraud-grade offences with the same
prosecution ladder. The
[gst.gov.in](https://www.gst.gov.in/) portal carries the current thresholds, notifications and forms —
thresholds change with Council meetings, so verify before April.

## The bottom line

Register when the law compels you (marketplace goods, inter-state goods, any size of casual activity,
RCM exposure), when exports begin (LUT economics are unambiguous), and when input credit plus client mix make
voluntary registration profitable — not because a YouTube video told you registration is compulsory for
everyone. The threshold is a floor, not an aspiration: staying below it legally is the smaller business's
genuine subsidy, and it resets every April.
