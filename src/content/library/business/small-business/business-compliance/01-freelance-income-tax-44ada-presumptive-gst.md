A freelancer's tax life is simpler than a business's and more complicated than a salary's, and the complication
is front-loaded into one decision made every April: whether to report income on actual books or under the
presumptive scheme. Everything else — GST, TDS, advance tax, the bank statements you keep — follows from that
decision and from one classification question (profession or business?) that the Income-tax Act answers with a
list and the CBDT answers with circulars. This article walks the decision as the law stands for FY 2025-26.

## First, classify: profession or business, and why the word matters

Section 44AA requires specified persons to keep books; Section 44AB requires an audit when turnover crosses
limits. Which of the two words applies to you changes the numbers. The Act's list of professions is in Section
2(36) read with Section 44AAA rules — the professions eligible for Section 44ADA are:

- legal, medical, engineering, architectural, accountancy, technical consultancy, interior decoration,
- film profession (artist, producer, director, etc.),
- and, added by the Finance Act 2023, any other profession notified by the Board.

Everyone else who works for themselves — content writers, designers outside interior decoration, tutors,
consultants in fields not notified, YouTubers, drop-shippers — is a _business_ under the general presumption
scheme, Section 44AD, not 44ADA. The distinction is not cosmetic: the presumptive rates differ (8%/6% vs
50%), the turnover limits differ (₹2 crore vs ₹75 lakh), and the audit thresholds differ.

| Question                             | Profession (44ADA)         | Business (44AD)                     |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Who qualifies                        | The 2(36)/44AAA list       | Any resident business (not agency)  |
| Presumptive income                   | 50% of receipts            | 8% cash / 6% digital receipts       |
| Eligible turnover ceiling            | ₹75 lakh (if ≥95% digital) | ₹2 crore (₹3 crore if ≥95% digital) |
| Audit-free limit without presumptive | ₹50 lakh                   | ₹2 crore (₹3 crore digital-heavy)   |
| Books mandatory below the limit      | No, if presumptive chosen  | No, if presumptive chosen           |

## The presumptive schemes in practice: what declaring 50% (or 8%) actually means

Choose Section 44ADA and you declare _at least_ half of your gross receipts as income; no expense records are
required, and all deductions — Section 80C, 80D, NPS — still apply on top. Choose Section 44AD as a business
and you declare 8% of cash receipts and 6% of receipts received digitally (bank transfer, UPI, card). The
schemes' fine print decides who they actually suit:

- **You cannot claim lower profit.** Once you opt in, the presumptive figure _is_ your income for the
  section's purposes. A freelancer with genuinely thin margins (heavy subcontracting, software licences) may
  pay more tax than books would show — in that case the alternative is maintaining books and getting the
  audit done above ₹50 lakh of gross receipts (profession) or ₹2 crore (business).
- **Advance tax becomes one instalment.** Presumptive assessee? The whole advance tax is due by 15 March.
  Miss it and interest under 234B/234C applies from the quarter you should have paid.
- **The 95% digital condition.** The higher limits (₹75 lakh/₹3 crore) require that ≥95% of receipts came
  through banking channels. Cash-heavy freelancers stay at the lower limits — and lose the presumption's
  simplicity at higher turnover.
- **Opting out has consequences.** If you declare profits below the presumptive rate and your income exceeds
  the threshold, you must keep books and get audited; you cannot switch back and forth opportunistically
  without the audit exposure for that year.

## GST: the ₹20 lakh line and the services you sell abroad

GST registration for services becomes mandatory when aggregate turnover in a financial year exceeds ₹20 lakh
(₹10 lakh for special-category states). Below it, registration is optional and mostly not worth the compliance
— with two exceptions freelancers meet early:

- **Export of services is zero-rated, but only if registered.** A freelancer invoicing a foreign client
  (receiving convertible forex through proper banking channels, with the client outside India and the service
  consumed abroad) makes a zero-rated supply — no GST on the invoice, but you must register to claim it, file
  LUT (Letter of Undertaking) annually, and can then claim refunds or accumulate input credit.
- **Platform-collected tax.** Some platforms (app stores, certain marketplaces) deduct GST under Section 9(5)
  themselves; your turnover counting and reporting still need care.

Above ₹20 lakh, you charge 18% GST on services and file returns — QRMP scheme (quarterly return, monthly
payment) keeps the compliance light for small service providers. The penalty for unregistered operation above
the threshold is tax plus interest plus penalty up to 100% of tax due; the department's data-matching of
foreign remittances (via Form 15CA/CB trails at banks) makes the "nobody notices" assumption weak.

## TDS on your invoices and the receipts discipline

Clients deduct TDS under Section 194J (10% professional, 2% technical services) above ₹50,000 a year per
client, or under 194C (1%/2%) for contracts. The TDS appears in your Form 26AS/AIS — reconcile before filing,
because a client who deducted and deposited wrongly (or deducted without your PAN quote) leaves a credit that
does not auto-appear. Two habits keep the receipts side clean:

| Practice                                       | Why it matters                                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Invoice with your PAN, bank account, and GSTIN | Matches TDS and GST trails to you without manual reconciliation       |
| Deposit every payment through the bank         | 44AD's 6% rate, 44ADA's higher limits, and 95% digital all read banks |
| Keep the invoice-to-credit register            | 26AS mismatches are your costliest filing delays                      |
| Bill advances separately from milestones       | Receipt-date clarity for presumptive counting and GST point of tax    |

## The four dates and the one form that decide your year

Advance tax for non-presumptive freelancers follows the standard schedule — up to 15% by 15 June, 45% by 15
September, 75% by 15 December, 100% by 15 March — computed on income as it arrives; presumptive assessee pay
100% by 15 March. ITR-3 (or ITR-4 for presumptive) is the form; ITR-4-Sugar is the presumptive variant that
keeps you audit-exempt. The AIS/26AS reconciliation in April catches client TDS errors while they are fixable —
in July they are refund delays.

## The legality note and the discipline that keeps it legal

Freelance income is fully legal income; the failure modes are all administrative. Not showing cash receipts
(UPI transfers into personal accounts, "gifts" from clients) is concealment — penalty 200% of tax under
Section 270A on underreported income, plus interest. Not registering for GST above the threshold while issuing
invoices that mention GSTIN (from an unregistered status) is misrepresentation with consequences under Section
122 of the CGST Act. Understating presumptive income below the actual profit to dodge the audit is a
declaration you sign; getting caught in scrutiny reopens the year at slab rates with interest. Rules, forms and
thresholds change at each Union Budget —
[incometax.gov.in](https://incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/) and
[gst.gov.in](https://www.gst.gov.in/) carry the current text.

## The bottom line

Pick the scheme deliberately: 44ADA if you are on the professions list and margins exceed 50%, books-plus-audit
if your real margins are thinner, 44AD for everyone else. Get the GST decision right the year you cross ₹20
lakh or take your first foreign client, not the year after. Keep every rupee in the banking system — the
digital-receipt rates, limits and audit exemptions are the tax code's only genuine gift to the self-employed,
and cash breaks every one of them.
