Creator income arrives as a pile of unrelated-looking credits — Google Singapore or Google Ireland remittances,
UPI transfers from brands, in-app tips, affiliate commissions — and the tax law files all of it under one
head with one question attached: is this your business, or are you an employee of nobody earning other income?
The practical answer for almost everyone drawing a living or a serious side income from content is business or
profession, with books, TDS reconciliation and a GST story. This article walks the money as it actually
arrives in an Indian creator's account.

## The heads: what each rupee is taxed as

| Revenue stream                   | Typical payer             | Tax head                 | TDS you may meet            |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------ | --------------------------- |
| AdSense (YouTube ads)            | Google's foreign entity   | Business/professional    | None on remittance (export) |
| Brand integrations, sponsorships | Indian company or agency  | Business/professional    | 194J/194C or none           |
| Super Thanks, memberships, chats | Platform                  | Business/professional    | 194O or none                |
| Affiliate commissions            | Indian or foreign network | Business/professional    | 194H possibly               |
| Merch / own products             | Customers via platforms   | Business                 | None                        |
| Licencing clips to media         | Media house               | Royalty (194J/section 9) | 10% or 2%                   |

The classification question matters for expenses: business income allows deducting expenses incurred _for_
the business — camera, lights, editor fees, subscriptions, a portion of home internet and a room's rent. A
creator reporting under "income from other sources" cannot deduct business expenses meaningfully. Gross
receipts of ₹15 lakh with ₹6 lakh of genuine costs are ₹9 lakh taxable as business, but ₹15 lakh taxable as
other income. The classification is a fact question — regularity, organisation, scale — and the department's
own guidance treats systematic content monetisation as business/profession.

## Section 194O and the platform's TDS

Since the 2020 amendment, e-commerce operators deduct TDS under Section 194O at 0.1% (1% for certain cases)
of gross sales facilitated — but for _services_ the more common deduction is 194J by Indian payers. AdSense
itself involves no Indian TDS because the payer is foreign and the supply is an export of service. The
creator's reconciliation job:

1. Collect every platform's annual statement (AdSense payments, brand invoices raised, membership payouts).
2. Match against AIS/26AS for any TDS entries — Indian brand TDS under 194J shows here.
3. Claim foreign tax, if any was deducted abroad (rare for AdSense; possible for some networks) only under
   the DTAA/foreign-tax-credit rules with Form 67 where applicable.

## GST on creator income: the export-of-service core

Ad revenue from YouTube's foreign entity is an export of services — zero-rated with LUT, provided the
registration, LUT filing and banking trail exist. Brand deals from Indian companies attract 18% GST
(consultancy/advertising services); Super Chats and memberships paid by Indian users attract GST; the same
paid by foreign users is export. The ₹20 lakh threshold applies to aggregate turnover including exports.
Creators commonly need registration earlier than they think, because AdSense remittances count in turnover.

| Income type                     | GST treatment              | Counts toward ₹20 lakh? |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Foreign AdSense (proper export) | Zero-rated with LUT        | Yes                     |
| Indian brand deal               | 18% output                 | Yes                     |
| Memberships, Super Thanks       | 18% output (Indian payers) | Yes                     |
| Affiliate commissions           | 18% as intermediary/agency | Yes                     |

## Expenses: the list that survives scrutiny and the list that does not

Survive: equipment used for the business (depreciated, not expensed — 15% depreciation on computers,
40% on some plant; the block system applies), editor/thumbnail/VA fees with invoices, software subscriptions,
ad spend to promote content, travel for shoots where the trip is demonstrably for content, home-office
portion (a defensible square-metre share of rent and internet), platform fees. Not survive: the entire home
rent when the studio is one corner, personal phone bills in full, a car's EMI for a vehicle used "sometimes"
for shoots (depreciation limited, perquisite rules for company cars do not apply here but the personal-use
disallowance does), purchases without invoices in the business's name.

| Expense                 | Treatment                                | Survives scrutiny when…                          |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Camera ₹2,00,000        | Capitalised, 15% depreciation block      | Invoice in your name, used for shoots            |
| Editor ₹30,000/month    | Fully deductible (194J TDS by you!)      | Invoices kept; TDS deducted and deposited        |
| Home internet ₹1,500/mo | Reasonable share deductible              | A stated, consistent allocation                  |
| Foreign conference trip | Deductible if business purpose is real   | Content produced, dates match, not a family trip |
| Gadgets "for review"    | Deductible as samples when kept for work | Review videos exist; gifts resold count as sales |

The "free products brands send" question: merchandise received for review is income (business receipt) at
fair value if retained or resold; the department's stance on barter and freebies has hardened, and AIS-level
visibility of high-value transactions makes ignoring it riskier than booking it.

## Advance tax and the lumpy year

Creator income is seasonal — the December brand rush, the March AdSense cycle — and advance tax follows
receipts: pay on income as it accrues through the year (15% June / 45% September / 75% December / 100% March)
on estimated annual income. The estimated-then-actual gap is normal; interest under 234B/234C applies only on
shortfalls beyond the norms. Presumptive taxation (Section 44AD/44ADA) remains available: declaring 6-8% of
turnover (business) or 50% (specified professions) eliminates bookkeeping, but also eliminates the expense
deductions that make creator economics work — a creator with 40% real margins pays more under 44AD's 8% only
if turnover exceeds books-derived income, which rarely happens; the trap is 44ADA's 50% for creators wrongly
counting themselves as "technical consultancy".

## The legality note

Booking creator income under other sources without expenses is legal but costly; not booking it at all is
concealment — penalty up to 200% of tax under Section 270A, plus interest, plus the AIS trail that already
shows the remittances. Not registering for GST above the aggregate threshold (AdSense counts) invites Section
122 penalties and interest at 18%. Deducting personal expenses as business ones is disallowance at scrutiny
with penalty under 270A where misreported. Foreign remittances must flow through banking channels with proper
purpose codes; unrecorded cash brand deals are the classic undeclared-income case. Current rules and forms:
[incometax.gov.in](https://incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/) and
[gst.gov.in](https://www.gst.gov.in/).

## The bottom line

Treat the channel as a business from the first payout: classify honestly (business/profession), book every
receipt including freebies and foreign remittances, register for GST when aggregate turnover crosses ₹20 lakh
(or immediately if exports begin and LUT economics apply), deduct only invoiced business expenses, and pay
advance tax on estimated receipts. The creator economy's tax burden is moderate when booked properly; it is
the unbooked year, discovered in a notice, that costs the channel its economics.
