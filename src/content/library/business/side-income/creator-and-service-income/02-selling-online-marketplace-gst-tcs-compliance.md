Selling online — handmade goods on a marketplace, resold electronics on classifieds, a hobby turned Shopify
store, a print-on-demand shop — is the side income with the widest gap between how easy it is to start and how
little most sellers understand about the three overlapping compliance layers beneath it: income tax on the
profit, GST on the sale, and the marketplace's tax-collected-at-source deduction. Each layer has a threshold,
and the layers do not agree with each other. This article separates them.

## The three layers, stated separately

| Layer                 | What it is                             | Trigger                             | Who it hits                  |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Income tax            | Tax on profit (sales − costs)          | Any income at all                   | Every seller, ₹0 upward      |
| GST                   | Tax on each sale                       | Thresholds; e-commerce rules        | Depends on goods and channel |
| TCS (Section 52, GST) | Marketplace deducts 0.5% (as notified) | Sale through an e-commerce operator | Every marketplace seller     |

The income-tax layer has no threshold: ₹40,000 of profit is taxable income at slab, reportable as business
income (or other sources for a genuinely casual, non-repeated sale — but repeated selling with intent to
profit is business). The GST layer has thresholds and special e-commerce rules. The TCS layer is
automatic and invisible until reconciliation.

## The GST layer for online sellers, settled

The confusion around the ₹20/40 lakh thresholds meets a specific override: a person supplying goods through
an e-commerce operator required to collect TCS must register for GST _irrespective of turnover_ (Section
24(x) read with Notification 10/2017-IGST). The practical matrix:

| You sell…                                        | GST registration                      |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| Goods on a marketplace (TCS operator)            | Mandatory, ₹0 turnover                |
| Goods on your own site/socials, intra-state only | ₹40 lakh threshold                    |
| Goods inter-state (any volume)                   | ₹20 lakh threshold                    |
| Services through platforms under Section 9(5)    | Operator pays; no registration needed |
| Services as a freelancer via a marketplace       | ₹20 lakh threshold (services)         |

Once registered, marketplace sales are taxable supplies with GST charged per the goods' rate schedule and
the marketplace's TCS deduction visible in the GST portal's TCS statement (GSTR-8 filed by the operator,
reflected to you). Composition scheme (1% turnover-based for small sellers up to ₹1.5 crore) is available to
marketplace sellers since the 2019 amendments — it trades input credit for a flat rate, sensible for
low-margin resale, less so for handmade goods with significant input costs.

## The TCS deduction: small, automatic, and reconciled in two portals

The e-commerce operator collects tax at source under Section 52 — currently 0.5% of the net taxable
supplies value as notified — and deposits it with a GSTR-8 filing. The seller's compliance is
reconciliation, twice: the TCS amount appears as GST credit in the seller's cash ledger (claimable, refundable
even for composition sellers), and the sales data appears in the operator's filings which the GST department
matches against the seller's own returns. Under-reporting sales in GSTR-1 while the operator reported them
fully is the most common notice generator among small sellers, because the matching is automated.

The income-tax side has its own TCS cousin: Section 194-O, where the operator deducts 0.1% (1% in some cases)
on _gross sales facilitated_, visible in 26AS/AIS. Two deductions on the same sale — one GST-side (credit in
GST ledgers), one income-tax-side (credit in 26AS) — and each must be reconciled in its own portal. Sellers
who never check 26AS discover the 194-O deduction only when it becomes a refund; sellers who over-report
nothing reconcile in minutes.

## The profit arithmetic: the expense ledger that survives

Business income means business expenses are deductible — and the online seller's costs are real:

| Cost                            | Treatment                       | Document needed                      |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Platform commission (5-25%)     | Fully deductible                | Platform's monthly statement/invoice |
| Shipping and packaging          | Fully deductible                | Courier invoices                     |
| Ads and platform promotions     | Fully deductible                | Platform ad statements               |
| Photography, samples, materials | Deductible (materials as stock) | Invoices                             |
| Camera, laptop                  | Depreciation (15-40% blocks)    | Invoice in business's name           |
| Home workspace share            | Reasonable portion deductible   | A consistent stated allocation       |
| Stock bought for resale         | Inventory — cost of goods sold  | Purchase bills (the GST trail helps) |

The seller who never raises a purchase invoice (buying stock for cash from a wholesaler who gives nothing)
pays tax on the whole sale price, not the margin — a 30-40% effective-overstatement of profit that no
deduction later repairs. The discipline is boring and total: every rupee of stock in, documented; every sale
out, platform-stated.

## The legality note

Everything here is law, not strategy: income tax on profit at slab (business head), GST registration rules
including the marketplace override, TCS under Section 52 and TDS under Section 194-O. The failure modes with
real consequences: selling through a marketplace without GST registration (Section 122 penalty — tax, interest,
₹10,000 or 10% of tax), under-reporting marketplace sales against the operator's GSTR-8 data (automated
mismatch, then notice, then 270A penalty of up to 200% on underreported tax), and suppressing cash-channel
sales entirely (concealment; the AIS and bank-credit trail make the "small amounts" defence weak at scale).
Thresholds and notifications shift with GST Council meetings —
[gst.gov.in](https://www.gst.gov.in/) and
[incometax.gov.in](https://incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/) carry the current text.

## The bottom line

Register for GST the day you list goods on a marketplace — the turnover thresholds do not apply there — and
reconcile both the GST TCS ledger and 26AS's 194-O deduction every quarter. Book every stock purchase with an
invoice, because profit without documented cost is taxed as revenue. And decide honestly whether each sale is
a personal asset leaving the house or a business making money: the tax law reads repetition and intent, and
the platforms' reports read it before you do.
