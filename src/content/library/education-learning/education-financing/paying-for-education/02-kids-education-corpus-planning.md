Education is the only major Indian household goal where the price rises faster than general inflation, the
product is sold with emotional leverage, and the purchase cannot be postponed. School fees in tier-2 cities
compound at 8–10% a year; private engineering and medical seats move with their own cycles; a foreign master's
is priced in a currency that has weakened the rupee by 3–4% a year on average for two decades. The corpus plan
has to beat all three, and the plan most parents run — a child plan from an insurer and hope — beats none.

## Sizing: work backwards from the fee, not forwards from the SIP

Pick the target year, estimate today's cost, then apply education inflation, not CPI.

| Goal                               | Today's cost | Years away | Assume 9% fee inflation | Corpus needed         |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------ | ---------- | ----------------------- | --------------------- |
| Private school admission (Class 1) | ₹1,50,000    | 6          | ×1.68                   | ₹2,50,000 + term fees |
| Engineering (4 years, private)     | ₹16,00,000   | 14         | ×3.25                   | ₹52,00,000            |
| Medical (MBBS, private)            | ₹40,00,000   | 16         | ×3.87                   | ₹1,55,00,000          |
| Master's abroad (2 years, USD)     | ₹60,00,000   | 18         | ×4.3 + rupee slide      | ₹1,00,00,000+         |

Then work out the SIP the corpus implies at a realistic return — 11% pre-retirement equity, 7% for the
hybrid years. A ₹52 lakh engineering need 14 years out needs roughly ₹10,000 a month. Two children double the
line, not the panic: the same table, two columns, started at different times. The honest part of sizing is the
endgame: if the corpus at 17 is at 60% of target, the gap is bridged by loan and college choice, not by a
last-minute shift into high-risk products that can take the 60% to 45% in the wrong year.

## The three-bucket plan

Education money fails when it is all in one asset that crashes at admission time, or all in one asset that
never grows. Three buckets by time-to-use fix both:

- **Bucket 1 — the next 3 years of fees,** in liquid funds, RDs and short-term FDs. This is _spendable_
  money; it is not an investment, it is a calendar.
- **Bucket 2 — years 3 to 7,** in hybrid or conservative equity funds. Growth with a shock absorber.
- **Bucket 3 — the far years (7+),** in equity index funds, reviewed annually. This bucket does the heavy
  lifting; it should hold 50–70% of the corpus until the child is ~14.

Every year, roll money forward: what enters the 3-year window moves into Bucket 1. This is the same glide path
pension funds use, applied by hand, and it is the single mechanism that prevents the classic Indian education
failure — the corpus that was 80% equity the year it was needed.

## How much education loan is rational

The loan-first decision most parents get backwards: they liquidate everything — including the emergency fund
and retirement money — to avoid a loan, then have no buffer when the child is 19. The rational order is the
reverse. Education loans up to ₹7.5 lakh carry no margin money and are backed by the CGFSEL credit guarantee
under the [education loan scheme framework](https://www.edcilindia.co.in); interest during the moratorium is
not subsidised for most private-college courses but the loan is unsecured below ₹7.5 lakh. Rule of thumb: fund
the first two years from the corpus and loan the rest — the loan keeps the family's liquidity alive, the
section 80E deduction (available for 8 years) softens the interest, and the repayment record is the child's
first credit history. What is _not_ rational is the ₹25 lakh unsecured personal loan at 14% taken in the
parent's name when a ₹10 lakh education loan at 9–10% was available — parents do this for speed, and pay
roughly ₹4–6 lakh extra for it.

## The grandparent route, done legally

Grandparents funding education is common and completely legitimate when documented. A gift from grandparent to
grandchild is tax-free to any amount (relatives as defined in the gift-tax rules), invested in the child's
name with the parent as guardian. Two clean structures: a minor's demat + fund folio operated by the parent
under the natural-guardian route, and an SSIA-style bank deposit ladder for the near fees. What breaks the
cleanliness: cash gifts with no paper trail, or investing the child's corpus in the grandmother's account
"for convenience" — which creates clubbing questions and, at claim time, an ownership dispute. One page, one
bank transfer, one gift declaration; that is the whole compliance load for a legitimate route.

## The mistake list, priced

The five expensive mistakes, with their approximate costs: the insurer's child plan (₹2–4 lakh of return
surrendered over 15 years versus a plain SIP at the same premium); the money parked in the child's name that
gets taxed as the parent's anyway above ₹1,500 of income (Section 10(32) limit) — hold the corpus in the
parent's PAN and earmark it instead; the foreign-exchange gamble (paying tuition from a base that ignores the
rupee's 3–4% drift); the emergency-fund raid in year 12; and the admissions-year portfolio that was 80%
equity. None of these are exotic. All of them are common, and every one is fixed by the table, the buckets
and the loan-first decision above.

## The annual maintenance of the plan

The education corpus is the household goal most likely to be forgotten, because the bill is a decade away
and today's fees are this month. The maintenance is one hour a year, at the same sitting as the household
money reset: re-price the target (fee inflation is not a constant — private school hikes have run 10-15%
post-2020), check the bucket glide (has anything that entered the 3-year window stayed in equity?), and top
up the SIP to the new number the table implies. A corpus reviewed once a year from birth is boring by
design — the drama of last-minute education funding is entirely a product of skipped reviews. And keep the
corpus in the parents' PAN with an earmarked note, not in the child's name: the ₹1,500 clubbing exemption
makes child-name holdings tax-inefficient above trivial amounts, and an 18-year-old with full legal control
of ₹50 lakh is a separate risk category that needs no help finding trouble.
